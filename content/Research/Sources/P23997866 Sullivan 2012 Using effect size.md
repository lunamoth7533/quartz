## Reported findings (checked full text)

Effect size is the magnitude of a difference; the absolute effect size is the raw difference, and standardized indices express it in standard deviation units.
^p23997866-magnitude

Cohen's d conventions of 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 are heuristics and ignore measurement accuracy and population diversity.
^p23997866-cohen

With large samples a significant difference can accompany a trivial one; P values do not convey size.
^p23997866-significance

An aspirin trial with a risk difference under one percent was statistically conclusive.
^p23997866-example

Effect sizes are the main finding and belong alongside significance tests; standardized measures allow comparison across studies.
^p23997866-report

## Scope as reported

A teaching article aimed at readers of medical education research. The thresholds it reviews are conventions rather than clinical decision rules, and the examples come from selected trials rather than a systematic sample.
^p23997866-limit

## Analyst cautions (AI synthesis)

These paragraphs are this library's own appraisal, not statements from the paper.

Small, medium and large labels are context-dependent. A small standardized difference can matter for a policy applied to a whole population, while a large difference on a badly chosen outcome can be irrelevant; the number needs the outcome, the comparator and the decision attached before it means anything.
