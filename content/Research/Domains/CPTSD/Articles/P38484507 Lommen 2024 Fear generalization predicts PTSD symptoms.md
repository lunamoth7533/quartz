# Fear generalization predicts post-traumatic stress symptoms: A two-year follow-up study in Dutch fire fighters.

**Authors.** Lommen MJJ, Hoekstra S, et al. (4 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38484507/)

**Published.** 2024 in Journal of anxiety disorders (volume 103, pages 102855).

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1016/j.janxdis.2024.102855](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.janxdis.2024.102855) · PMID [38484507](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38484507/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

Higher expectancy of an aversive outcome for stimuli resembling the safety signal predicted later PTSD symptoms after controlling for baseline symptoms.
^p38484507-prediction

Higher expectancy for stimuli resembling the danger signal was associated with neuroticism, and entering neuroticism weakened the predictive power of generalisation.
^p38484507-neuroticism

The authors propose problematic fear generalisation as a possible intervention target.
^p38484507-implication

## Scope as reported

Multi-wave longitudinal study in one high-risk occupation; generalisation was measured with expectancy ratings in a differential conditioning task.
^p38484507-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the paper.

A prospective association in a group supports generalisation as a risk marker, not as a predictor for an individual or a demonstrated cause, and an occupational cohort differs from clinical samples.
^p38484507-caution-predictor
