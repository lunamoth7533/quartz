# Population vs Individual Prediction of Poor Health From Results of Adverse Childhood Experiences Screening.

**Authors.** Baldwin JR, Caspi A, et al. (13 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33492366/)

**Published.** 2021 in JAMA pediatrics (volume 175, issue 4, pages 385-393).

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1001/jamapediatrics.2020.5602](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2020.5602) · PMID [33492366](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33492366/) · PMCID [PMC7835926](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7835926/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

In E-Risk, each additional ACE raised the risk of later mental (relative risk 1.14) and physical (1.09) health problems, over and above sex, socioeconomic disadvantage and earlier health problems.
^p33492366-group

Yet ACE scores predicted individual outcomes poorly: areas under the curve were about 0.58 for mental and 0.60 for physical problems, where 0.50 is chance.
^p33492366-individual

Dunedin gave similar results with both prospective and retrospective ACE measures, and the authors judge that targeting interventions by ACE screening is likely to be ineffective.
^p33492366-replication

## Scope as reported

Two population birth cohorts in which ACEs were measured prospectively through repeated interviews and observations, with retrospective reports at age 38 in Dunedin; mental health outcomes came from clinical interviews and physical outcomes from interviews, measurements and blood tests.
^p33492366-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the source.

The study separates two uses of the same number: an ACE score can describe group risk well and still misclassify most individuals, so a score cannot stand in for assessment of a particular child or adult.
^p33492366-caution-screening
