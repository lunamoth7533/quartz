# Recovery, function and social context

## Definition

Recovery in this literature includes symptom reduction but also functioning, participation and quality of life. Social conditions - safety, housing, income, discrimination - shape both exposure to trauma and the resources available afterwards.

## How it works

**The functioning framework.** The WHO's International Classification of Functioning describes functioning through body functions, activities, participation and environmental factors, and was endorsed by Member States in 2001 as the international standard for describing and measuring health and disability; it is why symptom scores alone understate or overstate change. Its linked generic instrument, WHODAS 2.0, covers six domains - cognition, mobility, self-care, getting along, life activities and participation - in 36-item and 12-item versions with cross-cultural, reliability and validity field testing. [[F23 WHO International Classification of Functioning#^f23-framework|International Classification of Functioning]] [[F23 WHO International Classification of Functioning#^f23-standard|International Classification of Functioning]] [[F23 WHO International Classification of Functioning#^f23-domains|International Classification of Functioning]] [[F23 WHO International Classification of Functioning#^f23-instrument|International Classification of Functioning]]

**What a functioning score is and is not.** Functioning instruments are general rather than trauma-specific: a functioning score summarises reported activity and participation, so its results describe participation rather than symptom severity. It is not a direct measure of symptom severity, capacity or potential, and it should not be read as a verdict about a person. How functioning is operationalised, scored and interpreted depends on the instrument, the informant and the study design, so two studies of 'functioning' can be measuring different things. [[F23 WHO International Classification of Functioning#^f23-limit|International Classification of Functioning]] [[F23 WHO International Classification of Functioning#^f23-caution-impairment|Appraisal: International Classification of Functioning]]

**Structural causes belong in the picture.** Stigma functions as a fundamental cause of health inequality because it constrains access to resources such as employment, housing and healthcare, which makes it part of the clinical picture; the framework distinguishes structural, interpersonal and internalised forms, each linked to measurable outcomes. The implication is that interventions targeting only individual behaviour miss mechanisms operating at the level of law, policy and institutions. For trauma-related conditions, that places safety, housing, income and discrimination among the conditions that shape recovery rather than in the background. [[P23488505 Hatzenbuehler 2013 Stigma as fundamental cause#^p23488505-argument|Stigma as a fundamental cause of health inequalities]] [[P23488505 Hatzenbuehler 2013 Stigma as fundamental cause#^p23488505-levels|Stigma as a fundamental cause of health inequalities]] [[P23488505 Hatzenbuehler 2013 Stigma as fundamental cause#^p23488505-implication|Stigma as a fundamental cause of health inequalities]]

**Service context shapes what guidelines can recommend.** Guideline products describe care pathways and support needs - information, support, relapse and symptom management as standard care rather than afterthoughts - with recommendations tied to an evidence review and to the funded service context, so they reflect service capacity as well as evidence. That is why they are service-level documents rather than sources of individual treatment advice. Availability of the recommended components varies between systems, and a recommendation is not a measurement of an individual's outcome. [[F96 NICE Multiple sclerosis NG220#^f96-care|Multiple sclerosis NG220]] [[F96 NICE Multiple sclerosis NG220#^f96-symptoms|Multiple sclerosis NG220]] [[F96 NICE Multiple sclerosis NG220#^f96-context|Multiple sclerosis NG220]] [[F96 NICE Multiple sclerosis NG220#^f96-limit|Multiple sclerosis NG220]]

**Reading rule.** Separate symptom change, functional change and social conditions. Recovery definitions vary between clinical, research and lived-experience perspectives, and outcome selection reflects that disagreement rather than resolving it. [[P40154799 Hu 2025 Psychological interventions for CPTSD#^p40154799-limit|Psychological interventions for CPTSD]]

## Evidence and status

Functioning and participation are established outcome domains, but the evidence linking specific trauma treatments to functional gains is thinner than for symptom outcomes: the CPTSD psychotherapy meta-analysis pooled PTSD, depression, anxiety and dissociation symptoms and reported that effect sizes decreased slightly at follow-up while remaining significant except for anxiety and dissociation, with moderators including childhood trauma and risk of bias. Functioning and quality of life were not the primary pooled outcomes. [[P40154799 Hu 2025 Psychological interventions for CPTSD#^p40154799-improvement|Psychological interventions for CPTSD]] [[P40154799 Hu 2025 Psychological interventions for CPTSD#^p40154799-followup|Psychological interventions for CPTSD]] [[P40154799 Hu 2025 Psychological interventions for CPTSD#^p40154799-moderators|Psychological interventions for CPTSD]] [[P40154799 Hu 2025 Psychological interventions for CPTSD#^p40154799-caution-category|Appraisal: Psychological interventions for CPTSD]]

## Recent research

- **2025 · Population survey (Journal of anxiety disorders).** In a probability-based Dutch sample, only about half of people with probable lifetime PTSD had sought professional help and about one-third had received first-line treatment, with lack of knowledge, shame and avoidance as common reasons; risk was concentrated among women, younger adults, less-educated people and people with a non-Dutch cultural background. [[P39808949 Hoeboer 2025 PTSD and CPTSD epidemiology Netherlands#^p39808949-care|Hoeboer 2025]] [[P39808949 Hoeboer 2025 PTSD and CPTSD epidemiology Netherlands#^p39808949-risk|Hoeboer 2025]] Help-seeking was self-reported, so the treatment gap is approximate. [[P39808949 Hoeboer 2025 PTSD and CPTSD epidemiology Netherlands#^p39808949-caution-systems|Appraisal: Hoeboer 2025]]
- **2024 · Systematic review and meta-analysis (European journal of psychotraumatology).** In trials of trauma-focused CBT or EMDR in young people, pooled change in social skills and functioning was small and not significant, and few trials measured social outcomes at all. [[P39450446 Phillips 2024 Social outcomes of youth trauma therapy#^p39450446-pooled|Phillips 2024]] [[P39450446 Phillips 2024 Social outcomes of youth trauma therapy#^p39450446-reporting|Phillips 2024]] The evidence comes from children and young people, not adults with complex PTSD. [[P39450446 Phillips 2024 Social outcomes of youth trauma therapy#^p39450446-caution-age|Appraisal: Phillips 2024]]
