# Pharmacodynamics and receptors

**Definition.** Pharmacodynamics describes what a drug does to the body, usually through receptors, enzymes, transporters or ion channels. [[F13 NIGMS How do medicines work#^f13-targets|How Do Medicines Work?]]

## Supported claims

- Drug targets include receptors, enzymes, transporters and ion channels. [[F13 NIGMS How do medicines work#^f13-targets|How Do Medicines Work?]]
- Agonists mimic or enhance a signal while antagonists block it, and one target can appear in many tissues. [[F13 NIGMS How do medicines work#^f13-agonism|How Do Medicines Work?]]
- Nicotinic receptors depolarise while muscarinic receptors can do either, depending on subtype. [[F04 OpenStax Communication between neurons#^f04-cholinergic|Communication Between Neurons]]

## How it works

**The action categories and what each predicts.** Agonists mimic or enhance the endogenous signal, producing the endogenous effect scaled by occupancy; partial agonists produce a submaximal response even at full occupancy, capping the response below the endogenous maximum, and can reduce it when endogenous tone is high; antagonists block without producing a response, shifting the effect toward whatever endogenous signalling exists; and allosteric modulators change how the receptor responds to its own endogenous transmitter. The categories predict different behaviour at different levels of endogenous transmitter, which is the practical reason they are distinguished: predicting a drug's effect requires knowing the mode, the endogenous tone and the receptor distribution - three facts, not one drug name. Because the same target appears in many tissues, specificity matters too. [[F13 NIGMS How do medicines work#^f13-agonism|How do medicines work]] [[Receptor families and second messengers]]

**State dependence follows from modulation.** Benzodiazepines potentiate GABA-A receptor function rather than activating it directly or opening the channel themselves, so their effect scales with how much endogenous GABA is present: the same dose does more where inhibitory tone is high and less where it is low, which is why their effects vary with arousal state and dose. [[P23789008 Griffin 2013 Benzodiazepine pharmacology#^p23789008-mechanism|Benzodiazepine pharmacology]] [[P23789008 Griffin 2013 Benzodiazepine pharmacology#^p23789008-effects|Benzodiazepine pharmacology]] This is the general pattern for modulators: the same medicine can look weak in one state and strong in another, "how much drug" is never the whole exposure question for drugs that modulate rather than drive, and generalising from a modulator to a direct agonist misstates the mechanism. [[Pharmacodynamics and receptors]] [[Receptor adaptation tolerance and dependence|Receptor adaptation, tolerance and dependence]]

**Off-target binding shapes the profile.** Most psychoactive drugs bind several receptor families with different affinities, so side effects often follow from targets other than the intended one. Antipsychotic motor and metabolic effects, for example, are attributed across dopaminergic, serotonergic, histaminergic, cholinergic and adrenergic binding rather than to D2 blockade alone: binding at serotonergic, histaminergic, cholinergic and adrenergic receptors contributes to sedation, weight gain and dysphoria, which is why side-effect profiles differ between drugs of the same class. [[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-offtarget|Antipsychotics mechanisms]]

**The occupancy-effect gap.** Receptor occupancy is the most measurable link in the chain from dose to outcome, but it does not map directly onto clinical effect. For the dopamine-blocking antipsychotics, response and several side effects occupy overlapping ranges of D2 blockade, which is why the field describes a window rather than a target to maximise; the effects themselves arise in different pathways, so occupancy names the receptor event rather than the clinical consequence and predicts where effects become possible, not which effects a person gets. [[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-d2|Antipsychotics mechanisms]] [[F34 OpenStax Pituitary gland and hypothalamus#^f34-prolactin|Pituitary Gland and Hypothalamus]] Every chain beyond occupancy - circuit state, adaptation, placebo context, measurement - is studied separately, and mechanism-only claims skip those links silently.

**Adaptation.** Repeated action at a target produces receptor-level changes, including desensitisation and trafficking, which is one basis for tolerance. [[P25566076 Allouche 2014 Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance#^p25566076-mechanism|Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance]]

**Reading rule.** A pharmacodynamic claim should name the receptor, the mode of action and the endogenous context; the same molecule can be an agonist at one target and an antagonist at another. [[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-limits|Antipsychotics mechanisms]]

**Cross-domain connection (curation).** Neurochemistry supplies the endogenous signalling that pharmacodynamics modulates: a receptor account without its transmitter's state is half a mechanism, which is why the two domains' articles are written as complements rather than summaries of each other. ## Limitation or common misconception

Receptor language is precise about molecules and imprecise about experience, because downstream effects depend on circuit state and time course.
