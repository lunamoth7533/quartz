# Comparative efficacy and acceptability of pharmacological, psychological, and neurostimulatory interventions for ADHD in adults: a systematic review and component network meta-analysis.

**Authors.** Ostinelli EG, Schulze M, et al. (13 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39701638/)

**Published.** 2025 in The lancet. Psychiatry.

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1016/s2215-0366(24)00360-2](https://doi.org/10.1016/s2215-0366(24)00360-2) · PMID [39701638](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39701638/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via the Europe PMC core API on 2026-09-24 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

Stimulants and atomoxetine had higher efficacy than placebo for reducing core ADHD symptoms at 12 weeks on both self-reported and clinician-reported scales.
^p39701638-stimulants

Cognitive behavioural therapy, cognitive remediation, mindfulness, psychoeducation and transcranial direct current stimulation were better than placebo only on clinician-reported measures.
^p39701638-nonpharm

Most therapeutic components were similar to placebo on all-cause discontinuation, but atomoxetine and guanfacine had lower acceptability.
^p39701638-acceptability

The authors report that ADHD medications were not efficacious on additional outcomes such as quality of life, and that evidence in the longer term is underinvestigated.
^p39701638-longterm

## Scope as reported

Randomized trials in adults; confidence in efficacy estimates ranged between very low and moderate, and interventions were dismantled into components.
^p39701638-limit

## Analyst cautions (AI synthesis)

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the paper.

A component analysis of average effects does not show that an intervention cannot help an individual, and missing evidence for an outcome is not evidence of no benefit.
