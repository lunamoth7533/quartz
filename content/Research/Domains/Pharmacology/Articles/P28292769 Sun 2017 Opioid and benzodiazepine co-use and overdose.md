# Association between concurrent use of prescription opioids and benzodiazepines and overdose: retrospective analysis.

**Authors.** Sun EC, Dixit A, et al. (6 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28292769/)

**Published.** 2017 in BMJ (Clinical research ed.) (first online 2017-03-14).

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1136/bmj.j760](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.j760) · PMID [28292769](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28292769/) · PMCID [PMC5421443](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5421443/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

The share of opioid users also taking a benzodiazepine rose from 9% in 2001 to 17% in 2013, driven mostly by intermittent rather than chronic opioid users.
^p28292769-trend

Overlapping prescriptions went with roughly doubled odds of an emergency visit or hospital admission for opioid overdose (adjusted odds ratio 2.14), and the odds were raised in both intermittent and chronic users.
^p28292769-overdose

The authors estimate that, if the association is causal, removing co-use would have prevented about 15% of overdose-related emergency visits and admissions.
^p28292769-attributable

## Scope as reported

Retrospective analysis of one US commercial claims database from 2001 to 2013, limited to privately insured people aged 18-64 and defining co-use as at least one day of overlap between prescriptions.
^p28292769-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the source.

People prescribed both drugs may differ in pain, anxiety, substance use and contact with prescribers, so the association does not by itself show that the combination caused the overdoses; the 15% estimate is conditional on causation, as the authors state, and a filled prescription does not show that the drug was taken.
^p28292769-caution-confounding
