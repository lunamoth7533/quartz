# Pharmacokinetics and ADME

**Definition.** Pharmacokinetics describes what the body does to a drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion. [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What Happens to Medicine in Your Body?]]

## Supported claims

- ADME describes the journey of a drug through the body from absorption to elimination. [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What Happens to Medicine in Your Body?]]
- Bioavailability is the fraction of a dose that reaches the bloodstream in active form and varies with route and formulation. [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-bioavailability|What Happens to Medicine in Your Body?]]

## How it works

**What the four processes decide.** Absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion determine how much drug reaches its target and for how long. [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What happens to medicine in your body]]

**Absorption and bioavailability.** Absorption determines how much of a dose reaches the circulation and how quickly. Bioavailability - the fraction of a dose that reaches the bloodstream in active form - varies with route, formulation and first-pass metabolism, so two products with the same nominal dose can differ in exposure, and the same molecule given two ways is two different exposures. That is why formulation and route belong to the mechanism description rather than to logistics. [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What happens to medicine in your body]] [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-bioavailability|What happens to medicine in your body]]

**Distribution.** Once absorbed, a drug distributes according to blood flow, tissue affinity and protein binding, which determines which compartments see the drug and is why the same plasma concentration can correspond to different tissue concentrations. The fraction bound to plasma proteins is inactive, and the blood-brain barrier restricts entry for many compounds, so central and peripheral effects can diverge in both magnitude and timing. [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What happens to medicine in your body]] [[F105 Neuroscience Online blood-brain barrier#^f105-transport|Blood Brain Barrier]]

**Metabolism.** Most metabolism occurs in the liver. It usually terminates activity, but it can convert a drug into active, inactive or toxic products, and for prodrugs or active metabolites it generates activity. [[F21 OpenStax Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics#^f21-model|Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics]] Because metabolism depends on enzyme populations that vary between people - genetic variation in metabolising enzymes changes exposure for a given dose - the same dose can produce different exposure, which is the mechanistic reason dose-response curves differ between individuals. [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-limit|What happens to medicine in your body]]

**Excretion.** Excretion clears the drug and its products; elimination determines how quickly concentrations fall, so metabolism and excretion together set how long the exposure lasts, and renal or hepatic impairment changes it. Lithium provides a concrete case: elimination is renal and proportional to serum concentration, which is why kidney function matters. [[F27 DailyMed lithium carbonate labelling#^f27-clearance|Lithium carbonate labelling]]

**Why the model matters.** These processes explain why two people can take the same dose and reach different concentrations, and why some drugs are monitored and others are not. [[F18 FDA Narrow therapeutic index drugs#^f18-window|Narrow therapeutic index drugs]]

**Mechanism and pharmacokinetics are two halves of one prediction.** A target-based claim says what the drug would do if it reached the target, and the ADME profile says how much of it does and for how long, which is why a target-based mechanism claim has to be paired with a pharmacokinetic one before it predicts anything. [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-bioavailability|What happens to medicine in your body]] [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What happens to medicine in your body]] [[F13 NIGMS How do medicines work#^f13-targets|How do medicines work]]

**Reading rule.** Exposure is a time course, not a number: a concentration at one moment says nothing about steady-state behaviour, and statements about a drug's effect should say whether they refer to the drug, its metabolites or the active moiety. [[Half-life and steady state]]

**Cross-domain connection (curation).** What a drug does centrally depends on what reaches the brain, which is where the blood-brain barrier and distribution material in the neuroendocrine-neuroimmune domain becomes relevant - a pharmacokinetics claim without that constraint is incomplete for central-nervous-system effects. [[Blood-brain barrier]] [[Antipsychotic mechanisms]]

## Limitation or common misconception

These are averaged properties: metabolism, interactions and adherence move exposure away from the label value.
