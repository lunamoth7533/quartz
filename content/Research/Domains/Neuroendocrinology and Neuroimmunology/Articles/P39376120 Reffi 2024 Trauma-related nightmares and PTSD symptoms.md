# Understanding nightmares after traumatic events in Detroit (UNiTED): prospective associations with interpersonal violence and posttraumatic stress disorder symptoms.

**Authors.** Reffi AN, Cheng P, et al. (8 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39376120/)

**Published.** 2024 in European journal of psychotraumatology.

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1080/20008066.2024.2409561](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2024.2409561) · PMID [39376120](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39376120/) · PMCID [PMC11463023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11463023/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

Trauma-related nightmares were more common after interpersonal violence than after non-interpersonal trauma such as vehicle collisions (80% against about 49%), with roughly five-fold odds across the three assessments.
^p39376120-violence

Nightmares at one month predicted more severe PTSD symptoms at two months, over and above PTSD status at one week.
^p39376120-prospective

## Scope as reported

Small single-site cohort with attrition from 88 to 59; nightmares were rated by how closely dream content resembled the trauma, and PTSD was assessed by self-report checklist.
^p39376120-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the paper.

A moderate correlation in about 60 people is a signal to replicate, not an estimate of how much nightmares contribute to PTSD in general.
^p39376120-caution-small
