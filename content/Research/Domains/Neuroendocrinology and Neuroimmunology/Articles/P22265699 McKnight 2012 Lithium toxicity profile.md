# Lithium toxicity profile: a systematic review and meta-analysis.

**Authors.** McKnight RF, Adida M, et al. (6 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22265699/)

**Published.** 2012 in Lancet (London, England) (first online 2012-01-20).

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

Clinical hypothyroidism was more common with lithium than with placebo (OR 5.78, 95% CI 2.00-16.67), and thyroid-stimulating hormone was raised by about 4 units on average.
^p22265699-thyroid

Lithium was also linked to higher blood calcium and parathyroid hormone, reduced urinary concentrating ability, and more weight gain than placebo.
^p22265699-other

Average reduction in glomerular filtration was small and not significant, and 0.5% of patients (18 of 3,369) received renal replacement therapy; no significant rise in congenital malformations, hair loss or skin disorders was recorded.
^p22265699-renal

## Scope as reported

Systematic review and meta-analysis of 385 studies using a hierarchy of randomised trials, cohort and case-control studies and case reports in people with mood disorders; the outcomes covered kidney, thyroid and parathyroid function, weight change, skin and hair disorders, and teratogenicity.
^p22265699-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the source.

These are group-level adverse-effect estimates pooled from mixed designs; they describe how often thyroid changes occur with lithium, not what any one person's thyroid will do, and they carry no instruction for monitoring or treatment.
^p22265699-caution-group

Read through the axis, a rise in TSH is the pituitary's feedback response to falling thyroid output, so an average TSH increase signals that the loop is being pushed; it does not mean that every person taking lithium becomes hypothyroid.
^p22265699-caution-tsh
