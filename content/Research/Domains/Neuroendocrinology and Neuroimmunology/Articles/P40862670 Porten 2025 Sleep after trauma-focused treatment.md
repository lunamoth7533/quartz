# Subjective and objective sleep disturbances following trauma-focused treatment.

**Authors.** Porten S, Friedmann F, et al. (13 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40862670/)

**Published.** 2025 in European journal of psychotraumatology.

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1080/20008066.2025.2542044](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2025.2542044) · PMID [40862670](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40862670/) · PMCID [PMC12392437](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12392437/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

The authors note that most people with PTSD report sleep disturbance and that earlier findings on how trauma-focused therapy affects sleep were inconsistent.
^p40862670-background

Subjective sleep improved from before to after treatment on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, rated sleep quality and diary sleep time, with no difference between DBT-PTSD and cognitive processing therapy.
^p40862670-subjective

Actigraphy measures did not change, and 76% of participants still scored above the questionnaire's clinical cut-off after treatment.
^p40862670-objective

## Scope as reported

Secondary analysis of one trial in women with childhood-abuse-related PTSD; sleep was monitored for one week at baseline, 6 months and 12 months.
^p40862670-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the paper.

Sleep disturbance that persists after trauma-focused treatment suggests it is not simply a by-product of PTSD symptoms, although one trial population cannot show whether it is independent or residual.
