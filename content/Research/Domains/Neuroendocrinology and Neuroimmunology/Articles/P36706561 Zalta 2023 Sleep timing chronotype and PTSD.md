# Sleep timing, chronotype, and posttraumatic stress disorder: An individual participant data meta-analysis.

**Authors.** Zalta AK, Vanderboll K, et al. (14 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36706561/)

**Published.** 2023 in Psychiatry research.

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1016/j.psychres.2023.115061](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2023.115061) · PMID [36706561](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36706561/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

When people went to bed and woke up showed no significant link with PTSD severity or diagnosis, while less total sleep time or time in bed was weakly associated with more severe symptoms.
^p36706561-timing

Associations were stronger in some populations and with wrist actigraphy, but few studies fell into those categories and only two measured chronotype, so the authors judge the timing-PTSD relationship weak but under-studied.
^p36706561-gaps

## Scope as reported

Two-step meta-analysis of proxy markers of circadian timing collected in PTSD studies rather than direct circadian phase measures.
^p36706561-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the paper.

Bed and wake times are behavioural proxies shaped by work, family and treatment; a weak association with them does not rule out circadian involvement measured with physiological markers.
^p36706561-caution-proxy
