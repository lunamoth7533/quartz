# Anatomical axes and planes

## Definition

Anatomical directions describe position and orientation in a consistent frame. Anterior means toward the front, posterior toward the back, dorsal toward the back (or top, in the brain), ventral toward the front (or bottom), medial toward the midline and lateral away from it. Sections are cut in transverse, coronal or sagittal planes.

## How it works

**A shared frame.** The nervous system is described through divisions and through sections, with the anatomical axes - rostral to caudal, dorsal to ventral, medial to lateral - providing the shared frame; without it, the same structure has different names in different papers. Structures are learned through the sections in which they appear: transverse sections of the spinal cord, brain stem and diencephalon, coronal sections of the hemispheres. [[F54 Neuroscience Online nervous system overview#^f54-divisions|Overview of the Nervous System]] [[F54 Neuroscience Online nervous system overview#^f54-sections|Overview of the Nervous System]]

**The neuraxis bend is the classic trap.** Because the human neuraxis bends between the spinal cord and the forebrain, the same words shift meaning between structures. In the spinal cord and brainstem, rostral means toward the nose and dorsal toward the back; above the midbrain flexure the forebrain's axes rotate, so dorsal to the cortex is toward the crown and rostral points forward. That historical accident is why structures are named twice (anterior versus ventral), why labelled diagrams are necessary, and why cross-referencing a brainstem diagram against a hemispheric one without checking axes produces real anatomical errors rather than typos. [[F54 Neuroscience Online nervous system overview#^f54-divisions|Overview of the Nervous System]]

**The divisions the axes describe.** The nervous system splits into central and peripheral parts, and the central nervous system is described region by region - cerebral cortex, thalamus and hypothalamus, basal nuclei among them. Grey matter is associated with cell bodies and local processing while white matter carries long-range connections, although real circuits do not observe a neat split. [[F05 OpenStax The central nervous system#^f05-parts|The Central Nervous System]] [[F01 OpenStax Nervous system structure and function#^f01-matter|Nervous System Structure and Function]]

**Planes do diagnostic work.** Localisation depends on the plane: a deficit pattern that crosses a midline or respects a vascular territory looks different depending on which axis is affected, which is why radiological descriptions always specify the plane and level. Structural MRI reconstructs a signal from hydrogen atoms in tissues of different densities, and its axial, coronal and sagittal views map onto the same axes, so a lesion described in one plane has to be localised in the others. The neurological exam is organised by functional divisions - mental status, cranial nerves, motor, sensory, coordination and reflexes - so that a deficit is attributed to a region or pathway rather than to a symptom name. The exam assigns a symptom to a division and the image assigns a lesion to a plane and level; the two assignments have to agree before a localisation claim is made, and neither is powerful alone. [[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-purpose|Overview of the Neurological Exam]] [[F28 OpenStax Brain imaging Psychology 2e#^f28-mri|Brain Imaging]] [[F15 NINDS Neurological diagnostic tests and procedures#^f15-modalities|Neurological diagnostic tests]] [[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-domains|Overview of the Neurological Exam]]

**Reading rule.** State the species, the plane and the orientation convention when describing a location; comparative anatomy renames structures and rotates axes, and most localisation errors in reading begin with an unstated convention. ## Evidence and status

Anatomical convention is standardised terminology rather than an empirical claim. Its usefulness is practical: it is the shared language for describing images, lesions and surgical approaches.

## Uncertainties

- Terminology is not perfectly consistent across atlases and clinical traditions, particularly around the brain stem and diencephalon.
