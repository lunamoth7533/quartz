# Spinal cord

## Definition

The spinal cord is a segmented structure carrying sensory information to the brain and motor commands away from it, and it contains the circuitry for reflexes.

## How it works

**Segments and dermatomes.** The cord is organised into segments corresponding to the level at which their roots enter or leave, each innervating a dermatome. Nerve roots exit in relation to the vertebrae differently in the cervical region than lower down, which is why clinical level estimates follow specific rules and why a clinical level and a radiological level can differ by a segment. [[F59 Neuroscience Online spinal cord#^f59-segments|Anatomy of the Spinal Cord]]

**Horns.** Cross-sections show sensory afferents entering dorsally and motor neurons leaving ventrally, giving the dorsal and ventral horns their functional associations: dorsal structures receive sensory input, ventral structures contain motor neurons, and intermediate zones carry interneurons and autonomic preganglionic cells. Segment and horn together locate a function with unusual precision. [[F59 Neuroscience Online spinal cord#^f59-segments|Anatomy of the Spinal Cord]] [[F59 Neuroscience Online spinal cord#^f59-horns|Anatomy of the Spinal Cord]]

**Two long systems.** Ascending and descending tracts occupy characteristic positions: ascending tracts carry sensory modalities that are segregated by type and crossing point, and descending tracts carry motor commands in lateral and medial groups. [[F59 Neuroscience Online spinal cord#^f59-tracts|Anatomy of the Spinal Cord]] [[F52 OpenStax Central processing#^f52-pathway|Central Processing]] [[F53 OpenStax Motor responses#^f53-descending|Motor Responses]]

**The crossing rules earn their place.** The dorsal-column system crosses near the brainstem while the spinothalamic system crosses within a segment or two of entry, so a hemisection produces loss of fine touch and proprioception below the lesion on the same side and loss of pain and temperature on the opposite side - the classical dissociated pattern. The crossing points explain this dissociation between deficits on one side of the body and the other, which is why the crossing rules are clinically load-bearing, and being able to derive the pattern from them, rather than memorising it, is what makes the localisation rules usable at unusual levels and incomplete lesions. [[F59 Neuroscience Online spinal cord#^f59-tracts|Anatomy of the Spinal Cord]] [[F52 OpenStax Central processing#^f52-pathway|Central Processing]]

**Local circuits do real work.** Some processing happens in the cord and brain stem before information reaches cortex, which is why reflexes are testable and autonomic control has local components. [[F52 OpenStax Central processing#^f52-brainstem|Central Processing]] Reflex arcs are the basic units of motor output, with sensory input, integration and a motor response that can occur with minimal cortical involvement; spinal circuits generate reflexes, coordinate alternating movements and modulate their own excitability, so the cord is a processing structure rather than a cable of tracts. Motor neuron pools integrate descending command, sensory feedback and intrinsic excitability, which is why the same command produces different movements depending on the state of the limb. [[F53 OpenStax Motor responses#^f53-reflex|Motor Responses]] [[F02 OpenStax Nervous tissue#^f02-neuron|Nervous Tissue]]

**Clinical mapping.** Because sensory and motor territories are arranged segmentally and tracts occupy characteristic positions, the level and pattern of a deficit localise the lesion. A spinal lesion produces a pattern of preserved and lost function that maps onto segmental and tract organisation rather than a single symptom, and that pattern lets a clinician estimate the level and distinguish a cord lesion from a root or peripheral lesion - the reasoning that localisation uses, with its known caveats. [[F59 Neuroscience Online spinal cord#^f59-tracts|Anatomy of the Spinal Cord]] [[History and localisation]] [[F59 Neuroscience Online spinal cord#^f59-limit|Anatomy of the Spinal Cord]]

**Reflex testing reads the segmental machinery.** Because reflex arcs close within the cord at a known segment, an absent or exaggerated reflex localises to that segment's circuitry or to the descending control over it. The reflex exam is therefore a map of segmental function layered over the tract exam - one reads the long systems, the other reads the local circuits - and the two together are what make spinal-level diagnosis precise. [[F53 OpenStax Motor responses#^f53-reflex|Motor Responses]] [[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-domains|Overview of the Neurological Exam]]

## Evidence and status

Spinal anatomy is established and clinically validated by examination findings; tract positions are consistent enough to support localisation in most cases.

## Connections

The spinal cord is where [[Sensory and motor systems]] converge with peripheral anatomy, and it is the anatomical basis for the examination described in [[Neurological examination]].

**Cross-domain connection (curation).** The neuroendocrine domain meets the cord at its autonomic preganglionic cells - sympathetic outflow leaves at thoracic and lumbar levels, parasympathetic at cranial and sacral levels - so autonomic findings in systemic illness carry segmental logic too, not only motor and sensory findings. ## Uncertainties

- Individual variation in root and tract anatomy produces occasional atypical presentations.
