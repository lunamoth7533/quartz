# Polygenic scoring accuracy varies across the genetic ancestry continuum.

**Authors.** Ding Y, Hou K, et al. (10 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37198491/)

**Published.** 2023 in Nature.

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1038/s41586-023-06079-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06079-4) · PMID [37198491](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37198491/) · PMCID [PMC10284707](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10284707/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

Polygenic score accuracy fell person by person as genetic distance from the training data grew, including within groups conventionally labelled as one ancestry; averaged over 84 traits, distance and accuracy correlated at about -0.95.
^p37198491-continuum

When scores trained on white British UK Biobank participants were applied to European-ancestry participants in the Los Angeles biobank, the most distant tenth had 14% lower accuracy than the closest tenth.
^p37198491-decile

Genetic distance also correlated with the score values themselves for 82 of 84 traits, and the authors argue for treating ancestry as a continuum rather than as discrete clusters.
^p37198491-scores

## Scope as reported

Individual-level evaluation of polygenic score accuracy against a continuous measure of genetic distance from the training data, using the ATLAS biobank (n = 36,778) and UK Biobank (n = 487,409) across 84 traits.
^p37198491-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the paper.

The abstract does not name psychiatric outcomes among the 84 traits, so the size of the fall-off for any psychiatric score still has to be measured. The mechanism - weights learned in one narrowly sampled population - is shared by psychiatric scores, which is a reason to expect similar limits until shown otherwise.
^p37198491-caution-transfer
