# Screening for bipolar spectrum disorders: A comprehensive meta-analysis of accuracy studies.

**Authors.** Carvalho AF, Takwoingi Y, Sales PM, Soczynska JK, Köhler CA, Freitas TH, Quevedo J, Hyphantis TN, McIntyre RS, Vieta E · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25451435/)

**Published.** 2015 in Journal of affective disorders (first online 2014-10-23).

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1016/j.jad.2014.10.024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2014.10.024) · PMID [25451435](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25451435/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

In psychiatric services at recommended cut-offs, summary sensitivity and specificity were 81% and 67% for the HCL-32, 66% and 79% for the MDQ, and 69% and 86% for the Bipolar Spectrum Diagnostic Scale.
^p25451435-accuracy

The HCL-32 detected bipolar II more accurately than the MDQ in mental health services, although the three tools did not differ consistently overall.
^p25451435-type2

In primary care or general-population samples, the MDQ at a cut-off of 7 missed most cases (sensitivity 43%) but rarely flagged people without the disorder (specificity 95%).
^p25451435-primary

## Scope as reported

Risk of bias was rated with QUADAS-2; most included studies were done in mental health settings and several carried a high risk of bias.
^p25451435-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the source.

With specificity of roughly 60-80% in clinics, a screen flags many people who do not have bipolar disorder, so a positive result starts an interview about past hypomania rather than making the diagnosis; accuracy also shifts with setting and prevalence.
^p25451435-caution-screen
