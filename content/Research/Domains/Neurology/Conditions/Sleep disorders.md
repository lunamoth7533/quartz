# Sleep disorders

## Definition

Sleep disorders are a family of conditions involving difficulty falling or staying asleep, abnormal timing, or unusual behaviour during sleep. Many are secondary to other conditions or to medication.

## How it works

**The family and its categories.** Sleep disorders are grouped as problems with falling asleep, staying asleep, timing and unusual behaviours during sleep. The family includes insomnia, sleep-related breathing disorders, circadian rhythm disorders, hypersomnias and parasomnias, distinguished by the dominant problem. [[F79 MedlinePlus Sleep disorders#^f79-scope|Sleep Disorders]]

**The categories map onto mechanisms.** Insomnia is hyperarousal and maladaptive sleep-related behaviour; breathing disorders are mechanical and physiological; circadian rhythm disorders are misalignment between internal time and allowed schedule; hypersomnias and parasomnias are arousal-state and stage-boundary failures. Because the mechanisms differ, the assessments and treatments differ - which is why "sleep problem" is the start of a differential, not a diagnosis. [[F79 MedlinePlus Sleep disorders#^f79-scope|Sleep Disorders]] [[F14 NINDS Brain basics understanding sleep#^f14-rhythm|Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep]]

**Causes.** Other conditions, medications, stress, shift work and circadian factors are named as causes, which is why assessment looks beyond sleep itself. [[F79 MedlinePlus Sleep disorders#^f79-causes|Sleep Disorders]]

**Normal architecture, so that abnormality has a reference.** Sleep cycles through non-REM and REM stages distinguished by brain-wave patterns, with slow-wave sleep early in the night and longer REM periods later. [[F98 OpenStax Psychology 2e stages of sleep#^f98-stages|Stages of Sleep]] [[F14 NINDS Brain basics understanding sleep#^f14-stages|Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep]] Timing is regulated by circadian rhythms interacting with homeostatic sleep drive - how long you have been awake and what the body clock expects - so timing disorders are not simply insufficient sleep. A disorder can therefore involve the amount, the timing, the architecture or the behaviour, and the four are measured differently. [[F14 NINDS Brain basics understanding sleep#^f14-rhythm|Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep]]

**The clock biology underneath timing problems.** The core molecular clock is a transcriptional-translational feedback loop in which clock proteins repress their own transcription over roughly 24 hours, and most tissues contain their own clocks coordinated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus, so circadian organisation is distributed rather than located in one structure. Clock output influences metabolism, physiology and behaviour, which is why disrupted timing has consequences beyond sleep. [[P27990019 Takahashi 2017 Circadian clock architecture#^p27990019-loop|Circadian clock architecture]] [[P27990019 Takahashi 2017 Circadian clock architecture#^p27990019-tissue|Circadian clock architecture]] [[P27990019 Takahashi 2017 Circadian clock architecture#^p27990019-outputs|Circadian clock architecture]]

**Objective measurement is selective, not routine.** Sleep studies and other evaluations are linked as tools rather than described as routine. Sleep studies are indicated for suspected breathing disorders, parasomnias and diagnostic uncertainty, while diaries and actigraphy - which estimates timing over days or weeks - carry most insomnia and circadian assessment, because the history plus two weeks of timing data outperform a single laboratory night for those presentations. The selectivity is evidence-based rather than economic, and it mirrors the general principle that a test helps only when it changes probabilities. [[F79 MedlinePlus Sleep disorders#^f79-assessment|Sleep Disorders]] [[Differential reasoning in neurology]]

**Reading rule.** Specify which dimension is disordered - initiation, maintenance, timing, architecture or behaviour - because the measurement and the mechanism differ across them, and because a sleep complaint is often a marker of another condition. ## Evidence and status

Diagnostic categories and the physiology of sleep are well established; treatment evidence varies substantially between categories. The reference account has limits: the consumer overview orients readers to categories and assessment without diagnostic criteria, and the sleep-cycle descriptions come from an introductory psychology source, so nothing here supports self-diagnosis or treatment decisions.
