# Rapport in same and mixed neurotype groups of autistic and non-autistic adults.

**Authors.** Foster SJ, Ackerman RA, Wilks CE, et al. (9 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39989258/)

**Published.** 2025 in Autism : the international journal of research and practice (volume 29, issue 7; first online 2025-02-24).

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1177/13623613251320444](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613251320444) · PMID [39989258](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39989258/) · PMCID [PMC12159336](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12159336/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

All-autistic groups reported overall rapport similar to all-non-autistic groups and rated enjoyment and group friendliness higher than both kinds of mixed group.
^p39989258-groups

Autistic participants' rapport declined as more non-autistic members were added, while non-autistic participants' rapport stayed fairly constant whatever the group make-up.
^p39989258-asymmetry

The authors conclude that the findings do not fit a social deficit model, since autistic adults often built rapport and their rapport depended on group composition.
^p39989258-conclusion

## Scope as reported

One brief structured activity followed by a five-item self-report rapport measure, in groups assembled for the study. The authors offer explanations for why group make-up mattered more to autistic participants, but the study does not test them.
^p39989258-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the paper.

Self-rated rapport after five minutes with strangers is one slice of interaction; the asymmetry fits a relational account but could also reflect how comfortable each group was with the task setting, which this design cannot separate.
^p39989258-caution-asymmetry
