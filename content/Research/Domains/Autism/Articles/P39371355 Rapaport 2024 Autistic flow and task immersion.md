# "In a State of Flow": A Qualitative Examination of Autistic Adults' Phenomenological Experiences of Task Immersion.

**Authors.** Rapaport H, Clapham H, et al. (6 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39371355/)

**Published.** 2024 in Autism in adulthood (first online 2024-09-16).

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1089/aut.2023.0032](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2023.0032) · PMID [39371355](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39371355/) · PMCID [PMC11447391](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11447391/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

Autistic adults' descriptions of becoming absorbed in tasks closely matched existing ideas of flow, autistic hyperfocus and monotropism, and the immersion was described as all-encompassing.
^p39371355-immersion

Immersion brought pleasure and a sense of being able to be oneself, but balancing it with the rest of life was hard, with exhaustion and neglected self-care among the costs described.
^p39371355-balance

The authors frame task immersion as potentially very valuable, and as something that may need support if it is to help autistic people flourish.
^p39371355-support

## Scope as reported

A secondary analysis of interviews from a broader study of starting and stopping tasks, designed with autistic community members; most participants were White, well-educated, employed women who used spoken communication, as the authors note.
^p39371355-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the source.

Interviews can show that monotropism's descriptions resonate with autistic experience; with no comparison group they cannot show that monotropic attention explains autism or distinguishes autistic from non-autistic people.
^p39371355-caution-comparison
