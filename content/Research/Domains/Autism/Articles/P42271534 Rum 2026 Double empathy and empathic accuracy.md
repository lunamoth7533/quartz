# Nuances of double empathy in autistic and non-autistic people: examination using the empathic accuracy paradigm.

**Authors.** Rum Y, Feldman N, Genzer S, et al. (7 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42271534/)

**Published.** 2026 in Molecular autism (volume 17, issue 1; first online 2026-06-10).

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1186/s13229-026-00723-2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13229-026-00723-2) · PMID [42271534](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42271534/) · PMCID [PMC13501596](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC13501596/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

Neither rater neurotype nor storyteller neurotype had a significant main effect on empathic accuracy; a trend-level interaction (p = .059, d = 0.13) hinted that autistic raters tracked autistic storytellers' valence relatively better.
^p42271534-accuracy

Non-autistic raters reported more empathy and empathic interest toward both kinds of storyteller.
^p42271534-selfreport

The authors describe partial support for double empathy, possible under-rating by autistic participants of their own empathic ability, and individual variation that cautions against treating neurotypes as uniform categories.
^p42271534-conclusion

## Scope as reported

Each participant viewed two recorded stories in a randomised design. The authors note that video stimuli miss the complexity of spontaneous encounters and that a self-selected autistic sample may not include people who need more support.
^p42271534-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the paper.

A null main effect in a video paradigm is weak evidence against mutual misunderstanding in live interaction, and a trend-level interaction is weak evidence for it; the study mainly shows that any effect on this measure is small.
^p42271534-caution-null
