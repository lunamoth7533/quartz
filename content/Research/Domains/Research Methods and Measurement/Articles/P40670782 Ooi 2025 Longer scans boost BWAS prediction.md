# Longer scans boost prediction and cut costs in brain-wide association studies.

**Authors.** Ooi LQR, Orban C, et al. (29 author entries) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40670782/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

The authors derive a model in which individual-level prediction accuracy rises with total scan duration (sample size multiplied by scan time per person); it fitted observed accuracies for 76 phenotypes in nine datasets closely (r = 0.89).
^p40670782-model

Up to about 20 minutes of scanning, sample size and scan time were roughly interchangeable, although sample size mattered more overall; once per-participant costs were counted, 30-minute scans were on average the most cost-effective, about 22% cheaper than 10-minute scans.
^p40670782-tradeoff

## Scope as reported

Model derivation plus reanalysis of existing resting-state and task fMRI datasets. The outcome is prediction accuracy for research phenotypes, and the most cost-effective scan time differed between task, resting-state and subcortical designs.
^p40670782-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the paper.

Higher prediction accuracy for research phenotypes is a gain in study efficiency; it does not show that fMRI-based predictions are accurate enough to guide decisions about an individual.
^p40670782-caution-prediction
