# The dire disregard of measurement invariance testing in psychological science.

**Authors.** Maassen E, D'Urso ED, et al. (6 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38147039/)

**Published.** 2025 in Psychological methods, volume 30, issue 5, pages 966-979 (first online 2023-12-25).

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1037/met0000624](https://doi.org/10.1037/met0000624) · PMID [38147039](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38147039/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

Of 929 comparisons of self-report scale scores, only 4% were accompanied by a measurement invariance test; the tests that were reported were poorly described, and none could be reproduced.
^p38147039-rarely

When the authors ran 174 sufficiently powered invariance tests themselves, 26% reached scalar invariance and 58% failed completely; in nearly half of the configural failures, the groups differed in their number of factors.
^p38147039-violations

## Scope as reported

Systematic review limited to psychology articles with openly available data; new invariance tests were run only where the data allowed adequately powered testing.
^p38147039-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the paper.

An invariance failure does not show that a reported group difference is false; it shows that the difference cannot be read as a difference in the construct without further modelling.
^p38147039-caution-difference
