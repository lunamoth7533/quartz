# Screening for bipolar spectrum disorders: A comprehensive meta-analysis of accuracy studies.

**Authors.** Carvalho AF, Takwoingi Y, et al. (10 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25451435/)

**Published.** 2015 in Journal of affective disorders (first online 2014-10-23).

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1016/j.jad.2014.10.024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2014.10.024) · PMID [25451435](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25451435/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

In psychiatric services at recommended cut-offs, summary sensitivity and specificity were 81% and 67% for the HCL-32, 66% and 79% for the MDQ, and 69% and 86% for the BSDS.
^p25451435-psychiatric

For bipolar II disorder in mental health settings, the HCL-32 outperformed the MDQ.
^p25451435-typeii

In primary care or general-population samples, the MDQ at a cut-off of 7 had 43% sensitivity and 95% specificity.
^p25451435-primarycare

## Scope as reported

Systematic review and meta-analysis comparing the Hypomania Checklist-32 (HCL-32), Mood Disorder Questionnaire (MDQ) and Bipolar Spectrum Diagnostic Scale (BSDS) with structured DSM or ICD-10 interviews, with bias rated on QUADAS-2; most studies came from mental health settings and several had high risk of bias.
^p25451435-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the source.

The same questionnaire performed differently in specialist and primary-care samples, so a sensitivity or specificity figure belongs to the setting in which it was measured; in low-prevalence settings even high specificity leaves many false positives.
^p25451435-caution-setting
