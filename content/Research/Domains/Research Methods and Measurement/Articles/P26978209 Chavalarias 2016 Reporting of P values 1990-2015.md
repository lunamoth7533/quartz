# Evolution of Reporting P Values in the Biomedical Literature, 1990-2015.

**Authors.** Chavalarias D, Wallach JD, et al. (4 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26978209/)

**Published.** 2016 in JAMA.

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1001/jama.2016.1952](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2016.1952) · PMID [26978209](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26978209/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

The share of MEDLINE abstracts reporting P values rose from 7.3% in 1990 to 15.6% in 2014, and in 2014 reached 54.8% of randomised controlled trial abstracts.
^p26978209-trend

Reported values clustered at .05 and at .001 or below, and 96% of abstracts and articles that gave P values included at least one of .05 or less.
^p26978209-significant

In a manual sample of 796 empirical abstracts, 2.3% gave confidence intervals, 13.9% effect sizes, none Bayes factors, and 1.8% both an effect size and an interval.
^p26978209-alternatives

The authors call for effect sizes and uncertainty measures in place of isolated P values.
^p26978209-recommendation

## Scope as reported

A meta-research study that text-mined MEDLINE abstracts and PubMed Central full texts from 1990-2015, with a manual check of 1,000 abstracts and 100 full texts; it describes reporting practice, not whether the reported results were true.
^p26978209-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the source.

Near-universal reporting of at least one "significant" value fits selective reporting and flexible analysis, but text mining cannot tell which; it is a signal about the literature, not a verdict on any one paper.
^p26978209-caution-selection
