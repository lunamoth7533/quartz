# Replication and publication bias

## Definition

Replication is the reproduction of a finding with new data. Publication bias is the tendency for positive or novel results to be published, reported or emphasised more than null ones.

## How it works

**The large-scale result.** A multi-team project replicated 100 experimental and correlational studies from three psychology journals using high-powered designs and original materials where available. Replication effect sizes averaged about half the original magnitudes, and 36 percent of replications reached significance compared with 97 percent of the originals. [[P26315443 Open Science Collaboration 2015 Reproducibility#^p26315443-design|Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science]] [[P26315443 Open Science Collaboration 2015 Reproducibility#^p26315443-result|Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science]]

**Variation across fields.** Results varied by journal and subfield, so the paper reports a distribution of outcomes rather than a single reproducibility rate for psychology, and a single overall figure should not be generalised to every literature. [[P26315443 Open Science Collaboration 2015 Reproducibility#^p26315443-scope|Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science]] [[P26315443 Open Science Collaboration 2015 Reproducibility#^p26315443-limit|Reproducibility in psychology]]

**Replication is not one thing.** Direct replication repeats the procedure; conceptual replication tests the hypothesis with different procedures; both matter and they fail differently - a failed direct replication challenges the original result, a failed conceptual replication challenges its generality or the conceptual link. Replication crises in psychology and neuroimaging have involved both, plus analytic-flexibility problems that make nominally direct replications indirect. [[P26315443 Open Science Collaboration 2015 Reproducibility#^p26315443-result|Reproducibility]] [[Neuroimaging methods]]

**Why the pattern is systemic rather than a story about fraud.** Low power, analytic flexibility, selective reporting and publication incentives all inflate the published literature without requiring misconduct. The reproducible-science literature argues that reproducibility problems are driven by incentives, publication practices and analysis flexibility rather than by individual misconduct, and it recommends preregistration, registered reports, larger samples, open data and code, and reporting guidelines. The argument is a position paper justified by argument and evidence about research behaviour, not itself an experiment. [[P33954258 Munafo 2017 Reproducible science#^p33954258-metadata|A manifesto for reproducible science]] [[P33954258 Munafo 2017 Reproducible science#^p33954258-practices|A manifesto for reproducible science]] [[P33954258 Munafo 2017 Reproducible science#^p33954258-framing|A manifesto for reproducible science]] [[P33954258 Munafo 2017 Reproducible science#^p33954258-limit|A manifesto for reproducible science]]

**Publication bias is a filter on the input side of everything downstream.** Studies with significant, positive results are more likely to be published; meta-analyses pool the published; therefore pooled estimates inherit the filter even with flawless pooling, and a body of significant findings is not a random sample of the studies that were run. Detection tools - funnel-plot asymmetry, excess-significance tests, registered-report comparisons - quantify parts of the shadow, and the strongest correction is structural: preregistration, registered reports and results-blind review change what gets through. [[P28053326 Poldrack 2017 Neuroimaging reproducibility#^p28053326-practice|Neuroimaging reproducibility]] [[Replication and publication bias]]

**How preregistration changes the inference.** Preregistration separates prediction from postdiction by recording hypotheses, methods and analysis plans before data collection, and practical workflows register analysis plans and report deviations; registered reports reduce publication bias by reviewing methods before results. The visible distinction between confirmatory and exploratory work is what allows a reader to weight a claim appropriately; without it, the same dataset can be made to support several incompatible narratives. [[P29531091 Nosek 2018 Preregistration revolution#^p29531091-distinction|The preregistration revolution]] [[P29531091 Nosek 2018 Preregistration revolution#^p29531091-purpose|The preregistration revolution]]

**Reading rule.** Treat effect sizes as upper bounds until replication is reported, prefer registrations to reconstructions, and read a review's funnel-related checks before treating its pooled estimate as settled. [[P33782057 PRISMA 2020#^p33782057-items|PRISMA 2020]] [[F22 Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions#^f22-certainty|Cochrane Handbook]]

## Evidence and status

The pattern is well documented but field-specific in magnitude; reading guidelines and certainty ratings now routinely incorporate these considerations. Reviews rate certainty partly by publication bias, and GRADE lists it among the domains that lower certainty along with risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness and imprecision. [[P18436948 Guyatt 2008 GRADE#^p18436948-domains|GRADE]] [[P18436948 Guyatt 2008 GRADE#^p18436948-use|GRADE]]

## Uncertainties

- Replication failure can reflect contextual differences rather than error, so a failed replication is information rather than a verdict.
