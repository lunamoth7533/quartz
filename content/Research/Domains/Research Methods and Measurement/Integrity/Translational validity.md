# Translational validity

## Definition

Translational validity concerns whether a finding in one system - an animal model, a laboratory task, a biomarker - supports conclusions about a different system, such as human clinical outcome.

## How it works

**Three kinds of validity.** Animal and cellular models are judged by face validity (does the model resemble the phenomenon), predictive validity (does it forecast drug or treatment response) and construct validity - whether the model's biology matches the human mechanism - which the model literature identifies as the hardest and most important criterion. A model can score well on one and poorly on another, so the three have to be assessed separately. [[P20877280 Nestler 2010 Animal models#^p20877280-validity|Animal models]] [[P20877280 Nestler 2010 Animal models#^p20877280-construct|Animal models]]

**The same split applies to translation claims.** Face, construct and predictive validity have translational analogues: a model can resemble the human phenomenon (face), measure the same construct (construct), or predict the same response to manipulation (predictive). The literature's standard critique is that face validity alone has historically carried more weight than it can bear, while predictive validity - does the model respond to known effective treatments - is the harder and more informative test. The three-way split disciplines an otherwise vague translation claim. [[Translational validity]] [[P21042938 Stiles 2010 Basics of brain development#^p21042938-limit|The basics of brain development]]

**No model reproduces a disorder.** The model literature is explicit that no animal model reproduces a human psychiatric disorder, and that a model's value depends on the specific question it is used to answer. That is a statement about scope rather than about quality: a model selected to test whether a receptor mechanism can produce a behaviour is informative for that question and silent about diagnosis, prognosis or treatment. [[P20877280 Nestler 2010 Animal models#^p20877280-limits|Animal models]] [[P20877280 Nestler 2010 Animal models#^p20877280-limit|Animal models]]

**Species and setting are two translations, often conflated.** Species translation assumes conserved mechanisms across phylogeny; setting translation assumes the laboratory construct captures the real-world phenomenon. A finding can translate across species and not across settings (an animal anxiety model that predicts drug response but not human anxious experience), or the reverse; separating the two axes is what makes the translation argument precise about where it might fail. [[Microbiome-gut-brain axis]] [[Reading a study and matching populations]]

**Biomarkers, regulation and clinical use are separate judgements.** Candidate markers must improve on existing information for individuals, not merely differ between group means, and a polygenic score's possible use in stratification or screening is framed as an area under investigation rather than as settled practice. [[F68 NHGRI Polygenic risk scores#^f68-use|Polygenic risk scores]] Regulatory review weighs the submitted data package against the standard the agency applies, which is a different question from whether a mechanism is well understood; that regulatory judgement is distinct from the fact that a compound was tested at all, and regulatory approval and clinical usefulness are different claims. [[F90 FDA Drug review#^f90-decision|FDA Drug review]]

**Reading rule.** Before importing a mechanism from another species, preparation or population, name what the model measured, what the human measure is, and what the two share. Agreement in direction is not agreement in mechanism. [[P20877280 Nestler 2010 Animal models#^p20877280-validity|Animal models]]

## Evidence and status

Translation is where many well-supported mechanisms fail: effects present in model systems frequently do not survive human trials, which is why the framework emphasises pre-specified criteria over plausibility. Failures happen in stages: a mechanism demonstrated in a reduced preparation must hold in the intact animal, then in humans, then in the clinical population, and then in the outcome that matters, and each hop can fail for reasons unrelated to the original mechanism. The clinical literature in this library contains several cases where the mechanism was solid and clinical translation was incomplete - fear extinction and reconsolidation being the clearest example. [[P22129456 Milad 2012 Fear extinction#^p22129456-translation|Fear extinction]]
