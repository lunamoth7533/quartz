# Neuropeptides and opioids

## Definition

Neuropeptides are short chains of amino acids released as signalling molecules. They are made from larger precursor proteins and processed by successive enzymatic steps, which makes their supply slower to change than that of small-molecule transmitters. [[F107 Neuroscience Online neuropeptides and nitric oxide#^f107-processing|Neuropeptides and nitric oxide]]

## How it works

**Families and precursors.** Neuropeptides are organised into families - the opioid peptides being the largest example - and sequence relationships within a family predict which receptors a peptide can activate. [[F107 Neuroscience Online neuropeptides and nitric oxide#^f107-families|Neuropeptides and nitric oxide]] They are made from larger precursors and processed through successive enzymatic steps before release, so synthesis is a multi-stage process and the precursor, not the final peptide, is what the gene encodes. [[F107 Neuroscience Online neuropeptides and nitric oxide#^f107-processing|Neuropeptides and Nitric Oxide]]

**Why processing makes peptides slow.** A peptide messenger begins as a larger precursor protein that must be transcribed, translated, trafficked and enzymatically cleaved before an active peptide exists in a vesicle. None of those steps can be completed within the second-by-second budget of fast transmission, which is why peptide signalling is recruited by sustained or high-frequency activity and why its effects persist longer than the vesicle-cycle transmitters'. Slowness is not a defect; it suits peptides to state changes rather than moment-to-moment commands. [[F107 Neuroscience Online neuropeptides and nitric oxide#^f107-processing|Neuropeptides and Nitric Oxide]]

**A different signalling geometry.** Peptidergic messengers, together with gaseous messengers, signal in ways that extend beyond classical point-to-point synaptic transmission: release sites are often not aligned with a single postsynaptic density, and the signal can act on nearby targets, producing effects on neighbouring contacts and interactions with other transmitter systems. This is why peptide effects are usually slower, longer-lasting and less spatially precise than a fast amino-acid synapse. [[F107 Neuroscience Online neuropeptides and nitric oxide#^f107-transmission|Neuropeptides and nitric oxide]] [[F04 OpenStax Communication between neurons#^f04-types|Communication Between Neurons]]

**Co-release changes interpretation.** Because peptides are packaged separately from small-molecule transmitters, a single neuron can release a fast transmitter and a peptide from the same terminal under different activity regimes, so the same cell can produce a brief point-to-point signal or a longer modulatory one. Identifying a neuron by its fast transmitter therefore does not predict its peptide effects. [[F107 Neuroscience Online neuropeptides and nitric oxide#^f107-processing|Neuropeptides and Nitric Oxide]] [[F115 Neuroscience Online transmitter release#^f115-requirements|Mechanisms of Neurotransmitter Release]]

**Opioid receptor logic.** Opioid peptides act on receptors that couple to inhibitory intracellular signalling, reducing transmitter release and neuronal excitability, which is the basis of both analgesia and the risks of opioid drugs. The endogenous opioid peptides act at several receptor families that differ in regional distribution and in the effects they mediate, and receptor activation produces both the therapeutic effects that are sought and the tolerance and dependence that limit them. [[P25566076 Allouche 2014 Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance#^p25566076-desensitization|Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance]] [[P25566076 Allouche 2014 Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance#^p25566076-tolerance|Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance]]

**Tolerance is receptor-level and partial.** Repeated opioid exposure produces tolerance to some effects and not others - tolerance that is effect-specific rather than global - and receptor desensitisation, internalisation and trafficking are among the mechanisms studied. These regulatory changes develop over repeated exposure, which is why duration and pattern of exposure matter as much as dose. The effect-specificity is a receptor-level finding with direct bearing on why opioid pharmacology is hard, and it generalises to every peptide system whose receptors adapt. [[P25566076 Allouche 2014 Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance#^p25566076-tolerance|Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance]] [[P25566076 Allouche 2014 Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance#^p25566076-desensitization|Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance]] [[P25566076 Allouche 2014 Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance#^p25566076-mechanism|Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance]]

**Reading rule.** Statements about 'endorphins' in everyday language map loosely onto this system; the reference-level version names the peptide family, the receptor and the preparation, and treats effects on mood as downstream and indirect rather than as a direct readout. [[F107 Neuroscience Online neuropeptides and nitric oxide#^f107-limit|Neuropeptides and Nitric Oxide]]

## Evidence and status

Peptide anatomy and receptor pharmacology are well established; the behavioural role of most peptides is established in animals rather than humans. Tolerance mechanisms come largely from cell systems and animal models. ## Uncertainties

- Peptide signalling is difficult to measure dynamically in humans, so most mechanistic knowledge comes from animal work.
- The relationship between receptor-level adaptation and clinical tolerance is not one-to-one. [[P25566076 Allouche 2014 Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance#^p25566076-caution-scope|AI appraisal: Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance]]
