# Adenosine signalling

## Definition

Adenosine is a nucleoside that acts as a signalling molecule throughout the body, including the brain, where it links cellular metabolic state to excitability. Unlike transmitters stored in vesicles, its extracellular concentration tracks activity and metabolism.

## How it works

**A transmitter that reports metabolic state.** Adenosine is not packaged into vesicles and released by calcium-triggered fusion like classical transmitters; it is a breakdown product of adenosine triphosphate, released through transporters and by enzymatic breakdown of released ATP. Its extracellular concentration rises when energy use outpaces supply - during sustained activity, metabolic stress or sleep deprivation - so its level is not held constant but reports what the tissue has been doing, which makes it a candidate mediator of use-dependent changes in excitability. [[F62 Neuroscience Online biogenic amines#^f62-classes|Biogenic Amines]] [[F115 Neuroscience Online transmitter release#^f115-calcium|Mechanisms of Neurotransmitter Release]] [[F98 OpenStax Psychology 2e stages of sleep#^f98-stages|Stages of Sleep]] [[F98 OpenStax Psychology 2e stages of sleep#^f98-rem|Stages of Sleep]]

**Receptors with opposite couplings.** Adenosine acts on G-protein coupled receptors that typically reduce neuronal excitability and modulate transmitter release, which is why it is described as a homeostatic brake rather than a fast transmitter. A1 receptors are generally inhibitory: they reduce transmitter release and hyperpolarise postsynaptic cells, damping activity where metabolic demand is high. A2A receptors are concentrated in striatal circuits and interact with dopamine signalling there, which is why adenosine appears in research on movement, reward and the behavioural effects of caffeine. One molecule, opposite couplings, different circuits - the same lesson receptor diversity teaches elsewhere in this domain. [[F04 OpenStax Communication between neurons#^f04-metabotropic|Communication Between Neurons]] [[F62 Neuroscience Online biogenic amines#^f62-dopamine|Biogenic Amines]] [[F87 NIDA Drugs and the brain#^f87-reward|Drugs and the Brain]]

**Sleep pressure.** Sleep timing is regulated by circadian rhythms interacting with a homeostatic sleep drive - how long you have been awake matters independently of the time of day - and adenosine accumulation is the best-supported molecular candidate for that homeostatic component. [[F14 NINDS Brain basics understanding sleep#^f14-rhythm|Brain basics: understanding sleep]]

**Caffeine as an experiment in nature.** Caffeine's main action is antagonism at adenosine receptors: it blocks a brake rather than adding an excitatory signal, which is why it delays sleepiness rather than removing the need for sleep, and why the same dose feels different at different times of day and after different amounts of sleep loss - a clean illustration of state-dependent pharmacology. It shows that the brake is behaviourally potent; it does not show that sleep reverses its effects by clearing adenosine alone, nor that adenosine is the whole homeostatic signal. The caffeine argument is best read as pharmacological evidence that adenosine is one input to a system that has more than one. ## Evidence and status

The metabolic relationship and receptor pharmacology are established experimentally; sleep-pressure models combine this with behavioural and electrophysiological measures. Attribution of sleep pressure to adenosine alone is a simplification, since other signals also accumulate with wakefulness and chronic caffeine use produces adaptive changes. [[F98 OpenStax Psychology 2e stages of sleep#^f98-stages|Stages of Sleep]] [[F14 NINDS Brain basics understanding sleep#^f14-rhythm|Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep]] Adenosine's role in sleep is supported mainly at the level of receptor pharmacology and animal work; claims that an intervention "clears adenosine" or "restores receptor sensitivity" in people are marketing rather than evidence, and the general sleep source used here is an educational overview rather than a trial. ## Uncertainties

- Human adenosine dynamics are inferred from indirect measures and from pharmacological probes such as caffeine.
- Chronic caffeine use produces adaptive changes that complicate the simple 'blocks sleep pressure' description.
