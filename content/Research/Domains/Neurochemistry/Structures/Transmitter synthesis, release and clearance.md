# Transmitter synthesis, release and clearance

## Definition

Every small-molecule transmitter passes through the same functional stages: it is synthesised from precursors by specific enzymes, concentrated into vesicles, released on calcium entry, and then removed from the synaptic cleft. Drugs can act at any of these stages.

## How it works

**Synthesis.** Transmitter identity depends on which enzymes a neuron expresses, and small-molecule transmitters are built locally in the terminal from precursors: acetylcholine from choline and acetyl-CoA, the catecholamines from tyrosine through successive steps, and serotonin from tryptophan. Biogenic amines are grouped into catecholamines and indolamines by synthetic pathway, and because the pathways differ by class, pharmacological manipulation can be transmitter-selective. Because each step is enzymatic, precursor availability and enzyme activity together set the ceiling on how much transmitter a terminal can supply. [[F62 Neuroscience Online biogenic amines#^f62-classes|Biogenic amines]] [[F108 Neuroscience Online acetylcholine#^f108-inactivation|Acetylcholine transmission]] Amino acid transmitters are made from ordinary metabolic intermediates: glutamate from the tricarboxylic acid cycle and GABA from glutamate. [[P12467378 Petroff 2002 GABA and glutamate#^p12467378-metabolism|GABA and glutamate]]

**Packaging and release.** Transmitter is concentrated into vesicles, and calcium entry into the terminal - established as the trigger in classic experiments at the skeletal neuromuscular junction - causes docked vesicles to fuse with the presynaptic membrane, with release occurring in quantal units. [[F04 OpenStax Communication between neurons#^f04-release|Communication Between Neurons]] [[F115 Neuroscience Online transmitter release#^f115-calcium|Transmitter release]] [[F115 Neuroscience Online transmitter release#^f115-quanta|Transmitter release]] Release depends jointly on calcium availability and the state of the release machinery, and only a fraction of vesicles is immediately available, so the same spike can release different amounts and release probability depends on what has happened recently as much as on the number of release sites - a synapse's output is history-dependent. [[F115 Neuroscience Online transmitter release#^f115-requirements|Mechanisms of Neurotransmitter Release]] Peptides differ here: they are processed from larger precursors through successive enzymatic steps, making their supply slower to replenish. [[F107 Neuroscience Online neuropeptides and nitric oxide#^f107-processing|Neuropeptides and nitric oxide]]

**Clearance.** Reuptake transporters and degrading enzymes terminate the signal, and the balance between them differs by transmitter: reuptake returns transmitter to the terminal for repackaging while enzymatic degradation handles what escapes, so monoamines are largely transported back into the terminal, whereas for acetylcholine the enzyme route dominates and the recovered choline is reused. [[F108 Neuroscience Online acetylcholine#^f108-inactivation|Acetylcholine transmission]] [[F06 OpenStax Cells of the nervous system#^f06-receptor|Cells of the Nervous System]] Clearance is not cleanup but the reset step that makes the next signal interpretable: it determines how long a signal lasts, and slowing it changes both the amplitude and the duration of the postsynaptic response. [[F108 Neuroscience Online acetylcholine#^f108-inactivation|Acetylcholine transmission]] [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What happens to medicine in your body]]

**Timescales.** Fast amino-acid transmission operates on millisecond timescales, while monoamines and neuropeptides act as modulators over seconds to minutes. [[F106 Neuroscience Online amino acid transmitters#^f106-excitatory|Amino Acid Transmitters]] [[F107 Neuroscience Online neuropeptides and nitric oxide#^f107-transmission|Neuropeptides and Nitric Oxide]]

**Turnover as a measurable quantity.** Because synthesis, release and clearance run continuously, transmitter content in a terminal reflects a balance rather than a store size, and measurements of turnover (through metabolites or labelled precursors) capture that balance over time rather than a single moment. This is why peripheral metabolite measurements are only indirect indices of central signalling. [[F62 Neuroscience Online biogenic amines#^f62-limit|Biogenic Amines]] [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What happens to medicine in your body]]

**Where drugs enter the cycle.** A drug can act at any step: precursor supply, synthetic enzymes, vesicular packaging, release machinery, receptors, reuptake transporters or degradation enzymes. Clearance is the target of many psychotropic drugs, and blocking a transporter prolongs the signal rather than increasing release, which matters when an effect is attributed to 'more transmitter'. [[F108 Neuroscience Online acetylcholine#^f108-inactivation|Acetylcholine transmission]] [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What happens to medicine in your body]] Knowing which step a drug touches explains its onset, its duration and much of its side-effect profile, and it is the minimum needed for a mechanistic statement that goes beyond a drug's class name. [[F13 NIGMS How do medicines work#^f13-targets|How do medicines work]] [[Monoamine reuptake and degradation]]

## Evidence and status

Each stage is established biochemically and electrophysiologically in identified preparations. Stage-level knowledge explains why drugs have specific molecular targets; it does not predict clinical effect sizes. ## Uncertainties

- Quantifying release and clearance in the intact human brain is not possible with current methods; estimates come from animals, tissue and indirect measures.
- Transmitter levels measured in blood or cerebrospinal fluid are not direct indices of synaptic activity.
