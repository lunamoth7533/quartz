## Reported findings (checked abstract)

Both pituitary peptides are best known for peripheral actions (oxytocin in birth and nursing, vasopressin in blood pressure and urine concentration), yet each is also secreted inside the brain and acts there on social behaviour.
^p35863332-roles

In the work reviewed, oxytocin supports maternal care and bonding and raises the reward value and salience of social cues; vasopressin influences social communication, investigation, territoriality and aggression, chiefly in males.
^p35863332-effects

Both peptides aid social memory and, in monogamous species, pair bonding; effects differ from species to species.
^p35863332-species

It closes on translational prospects for conditions with social difficulties, such as autism.
^p35863332-translation

## Scope as reported

A narrative review of circuit-level research, including studies across several animal species that use molecular genetic tools to map, monitor and manipulate peptide circuits. It does not pool human trial data.
^p35863332-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the source.

Circuit findings come largely from non-human species, and the review itself stresses species differences, so a behavioural role shown in an animal model is a hypothesis for people rather than an established human mechanism; the popular "social hormone" label overstates what transfers.
^p35863332-caution-species

Peripheral and central release are distinct routes, so a blood level of either peptide reflects pituitary secretion and is not a direct measure of signalling inside the brain.
^p35863332-caution-routes
