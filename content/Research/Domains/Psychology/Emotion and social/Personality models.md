# Personality models

## Definition

Personality models describe enduring patterns of thinking, feeling and behaving. Trait approaches measure these as continuous dimensions; typological approaches assign categories, which the evidence supports less well.

## How it works

**Traits are dimensional.** Historical perspectives on personality are traced from temperament ideas through trait and psychodynamic traditions. Contemporary trait models summarise personality with a few broad dimensions derived from covariation among many descriptors rather than with types, which is why modern work uses continuous scores and personality is normally reported as a profile rather than a type. [[F45 OpenStax Psychology 2e personality#^f45-historical|Personality]] [[F45 OpenStax Psychology 2e personality#^f45-traits|What Is Personality?]]

**What a trait score promises, precisely.** A dimension score locates a person in a population distribution and predicts average tendencies with modest accuracy; it does not describe internal causation, and its predictive value decays as the outcome moves away from the samples used to derive it. Rank order is the defensible claim - people's relative standing is fairly stable in adulthood. [[F45 OpenStax Psychology 2e personality#^f45-traits|What Is Personality?]]

**Situational debate.** The consistency of specific behaviours across situations is limited, and the field resolved this by treating traits as tendencies that interact with context rather than as fixed dispositions that override it. Disagreements about consistency across situations nonetheless remain live, and they are the same question raised by dimensional accounts of psychopathology. [[F45 OpenStax Psychology 2e personality#^f45-debate|What Is Personality?]] The situational emphasis in social psychology is the same argument from another direction. [[F46 OpenStax Psychology 2e social psychology#^f46-situational|What Is Social Psychology?]]

**Clinical categories persist for practical reasons.** Personality constructs appear in clinical classification as patterns of inner experience and behaviour that deviate markedly from expectations and are inflexible. Borderline personality disorder is the worked example: it is described through long-standing instability in mood, self-image and relationships with marked sensitivity to rejection and abandonment; features often emerge in adolescence or early adulthood and diagnosis is possible when criteria are met. [[F84 NIMH Borderline personality disorder#^f84-pattern|Borderline Personality Disorder]] [[F84 NIMH Borderline personality disorder#^f84-onset|Borderline Personality Disorder]] Evidence-based psychotherapies are the primary approach, with medication reserved for specific symptoms rather than the disorder. [[F84 NIMH Borderline personality disorder#^f84-treatment|Borderline Personality Disorder]] [[F84 NIMH Borderline personality disorder#^f84-limit|Borderline Personality Disorder]] It is a categorical diagnosis whose criteria describe a dimensional construct at its extreme. The hybrid is not a compromise pending better taxonomy; it is what practice looks like when categories carry service and communication functions while dimensions carry measurement. [[P28333488 Kotov 2017 HiTOP#^p28333488-structure|HiTOP]]

**The dimensional critique is mainstream here.** Categorical taxonomies suffer from arbitrary thresholds, unclear boundaries, frequent co-occurrence, within-disorder heterogeneity and diagnostic instability, and the proposed response is to organise covarying syndromes into dimensional spectra as a research effort, which is why dimensional models are increasingly used in research. Personality pathology is where that critique is least contested, and the critique supplies the reason the clinical hybrid is contested. [[P28333488 Kotov 2017 HiTOP#^p28333488-problem|HiTOP]] [[P28333488 Kotov 2017 HiTOP#^p28333488-structure|HiTOP]]

**Measurement before comparison.** Self-report personality measures are vulnerable to reference-group effects and to invariance problems when compared across cultures. Comparing trait or pathology scores across groups or time assumes invariance, so invariance testing is the prerequisite for any group comparison, and absent invariance changes what a difference means. Reliability is not validity: an instrument can produce consistent scores that consistently miss the construct. [[P27942093 Putnick 2016 Measurement invariance#^p27942093-practice|Measurement invariance]] [[P29942800 Boateng 2018 Developing and validating scales#^p29942800-caution-score|AI appraisal: Developing and validating scales]]

**Reading rule.** Cultural expectations shape what counts as disordered, so criteria are not culture-free, and harm or dysfunction is part of the definition rather than an add-on. A trait score is a position in a distribution, not a verdict. [[F47 OpenStax Psychology 2e psychological disorders#^f47-cultural|Psychological Disorders]] [[F47 OpenStax Psychology 2e psychological disorders#^f47-harm|Psychological Disorders]]

## Evidence and status

Trait structure replicates across cultures; the number of dimensions and the boundary with psychopathology remain debated, which is the same disagreement that appears in the classification articles.
