# Can we enhance working memory? Bias and effectiveness in cognitive training studies.

**Authors.** Rodas JA, Asimakopoulou AA, Greene CM (3 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38366265/)

**Published.** 2024 in Psychonomic bulletin & review.

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.3758/s13423-024-02466-8](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-024-02466-8) · PMID [38366265](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38366265/) · PMCID [PMC11543728](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11543728/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

Training produced only a small working-memory gain overall (SMD 0.18), but a much larger one (SMD 1.15) when the outcome tasks resembled the training tasks.
^p38366265-effect

Fluid intelligence did not improve, and working-memory gains were unrelated to changes in it; some specific executive functions did improve, and training dose and type did not moderate effectiveness.
^p38366265-transfer

Risk-of-bias ratings raised concerns about randomisation and possible selective reporting, and the authors name the choice of assessment task as the largest source of bias.
^p38366265-bias

## Scope as reported

A meta-analysis that assessed risk of bias and took care to control for practice effects, pooling training aimed at different cognitive functions.
^p38366265-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the paper.

When the outcome task resembles the trained task, a gain may reflect practice with that task format rather than a change in capacity, so the small overall estimate is the better guide to generalisable change.
^p38366265-caution-similarity
