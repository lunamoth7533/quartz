# Efficacy of cognitive behavioral therapy in treating repetitive negative thinking, rumination, and worry - a transdiagnostic meta-analysis.

**Authors.** Stenzel KL, Keller J, et al. (5 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39916353/)

**Published.** 2025 in Psychological medicine (first online 2025-02-07).

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1017/S0033291725000017](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291725000017) · PMID [39916353](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39916353/) · PMCID [PMC12017360](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12017360/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

The authors frame repetitive negative thinking as a transdiagnostic process linked to the start, persistence and relapse of several mental disorders, with rumination and worry as its best-known forms.
^p39916353-definition

After treatment, CBT reduced repetitive negative thinking more than control conditions (g = -0.67), and the size of the effect did not differ significantly between rumination, worry and general repetitive thinking.
^p39916353-effect

Treatments built to target repetitive thinking reduced it more (g = -0.99) than less specific CBT (g = -0.56), while individual or in-person delivery added no significant benefit.
^p39916353-specific

## Scope as reported

Randomised trials only, pooled at post-treatment across diagnoses, intervention types and constructs.
^p39916353-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the source.

Post-treatment questionnaire scores for the very process a therapy targets are the outcome most exposed to expectancy effects, and pooling across diagnoses says little about any single condition such as bipolar disorder or ADHD.
^p39916353-caution-outcome
