# Interactive relationship between alexithymia, psychological distress and posttraumatic stress disorder symptomology across time.

**Authors.** Putica A, Van Dam NT, Felmingham K, Lawrence-Wood E, McFarlane A, O'Donnell M (6 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37987839/)

**Published.** 2024 in Cognition & emotion.

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1080/02699931.2023.2283934](https://doi.org/10.1080/02699931.2023.2283934) · PMID [37987839](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37987839/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

Alexithymia predicted more severe PTSD symptoms and hyperarousal across three assessments spanning pre-deployment to several years after return, which the authors read as a persistent risk factor.
^p37987839-risk

General psychological distress partly mediated the links between alexithymia and total PTSD severity and hyperarousal, and fully mediated the links with re-experiencing and avoidance, at all three time points.
^p37987839-distress

## Scope as reported

Self-report measures in a military cohort: the Toronto Alexithymia Scale, the Kessler 10 and a PTSD checklist; the design responds to what the authors describe as over-reliance on cross-sectional research.
^p37987839-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the paper.

Because the alexithymia questionnaire itself shares variance with distress, a mediation by distress is hard to separate from overlapping measurement; a military cohort also limits transfer to civilian or complex-trauma populations.
^p37987839-caution-overlap
