# EMDR therapy vs. supportive therapy as adjunctive treatment in trauma-exposed bipolar patients: A randomised controlled trial.

**Authors.** Hogg B, Radua J, et al. (26 authors) · [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38061553/)

**Published.** 2024 in Spanish journal of psychiatry and mental health (first online 2023-12-05).

**Identifiers.** DOI [10.1016/j.sjpmh.2023.11.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sjpmh.2023.11.005) · PMID [38061553](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38061553/)

**Verification.** Abstract-level check via PubMed on 2026-09-27 (metadata matched by PMID). Full text was not read; read status is queued.

## Reported findings (checked abstract)

Affective relapse over 24 months, the primary outcome, did not differ between EMDR and supportive therapy, whether or not relapses needing hospital admission were counted.
^p38061553-relapse

At the 12-month follow-up EMDR was ahead of supportive therapy on depressive symptoms (d 0.97), manic symptoms (d 0.51) and functioning (d 0.49); trauma symptoms fell from baseline in both arms.
^p38061553-symptoms

Dropout did not differ between arms, and working on traumatic memories was not followed by more relapses.
^p38061553-safety

## Scope as reported

Registered trial (NCT02634372) reported to CONSORT standards, with 20 sessions in each arm and assessments at baseline, after treatment and at 12 and 24 months; described by the authors as the first multicentre trial of a trauma-focused adjunctive therapy aimed at bipolar relapse.
^p38061553-limit

## Library appraisal

These paragraphs are this library's own methodological appraisal, not statements from the source.

The primary outcome was negative, so the symptom advantages are secondary results in a sample of 77 that need replication before they can be read as a change in the course of bipolar disorder.
^p38061553-caution-primary
