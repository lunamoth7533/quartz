# Cerebral cortex and lobes

## Definition

The cerebral cortex is the folded sheet of grey matter covering the hemispheres, conventionally divided into frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital lobes plus insular and limbic regions.

## How it works

**Primary areas.** Primary sensory and motor areas sit adjacent to the relevant input or output. The postcentral gyrus is the cortical target for discriminative touch and proprioception, and primary motor cortex lies immediately anterior to the central sulcus. [[F52 OpenStax Central processing#^f52-cortex|Central Processing]] [[F53 OpenStax Motor responses#^f53-cortical|Motor Responses]]

**Maps.** Body representations in sensory and motor cortex are ordered but not proportional to body size in a simple way, and the maps are plastic rather than fixed. [[F103 Neuroscience Online somatosensory systems#^f103-receptors|Somatosensory systems]]

**Association cortex.** Anterior, posterior and limbic association regions integrate across modalities; posterior association damage and frontal executive syndromes are used as the standard illustrations that association cortex is not spare capacity. [[F109 Neuroscience Online association and executive processing#^f109-posterior|Association and executive processing]] [[F109 Neuroscience Online association and executive processing#^f109-executive|Association and executive processing]]

**Cortical processing.** Visual cortex is the most thoroughly mapped case: striate cortex receives the thalamic relay, and extrastriate areas build on its analysis with properties such as orientation and motion sensitivity. [[F104 Neuroscience Online visual cortex#^f104-pathway|Visual Processing: Cortical Pathways]] [[F104 Neuroscience Online visual cortex#^f104-properties|Visual Processing: Cortical Pathways]]

## Evidence and status

Lobar anatomy and primary areas are established by anatomy, stimulation and lesion work. Functional localisations of complex functions are model-dependent and have been repeatedly revised. [[F110 Neuroscience Online language#^f110-limits|Language]]

> [!info]- In depth: lamination, territories and the limits of lobe labels
> **The laminar plan.** Neocortex is built in six layers with characteristic input and output rules: thalamic afferents terminate mainly in the middle layers, corticocortical connections concentrate in superficial and deep layers, and the principal output neurons send their axons to subcortical targets from the deep layers. Layer composition predicts connectivity, which is why cytoarchitectonic maps are used as a first approximation of functional territory. [[F05 OpenStax The central nervous system#^f05-parts|The Central Nervous System]] [[F102 Neuroscience Online cell types#^f102-compartments|Organization of Cell Types]]
>
> **Columnar organisation.** Vertical groupings of cells share input and output preferences, and this columnar motif is the standard unit of cortical description. The motif is a statistical regularity rather than a rigid module: column boundaries shift with the method used to define them, and the degree of modularity differs between areas and species. [[F102 Neuroscience Online cell types#^f102-dendrites|Organization of Cell Types]]
>
> **Lobes as coarse addresses.** Frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital labels describe position, and each contains multiple functionally distinct areas. The classical associations - occipital with vision, temporal with audition and memory, parietal with somatosensation and spatial processing, frontal with motor and executive function - are useful navigation aids and poor mechanism statements. [[F05 OpenStax The central nervous system#^f05-parts|The Central Nervous System]] [[F109 Neuroscience Online association and executive processing#^f109-areas|Association and Executive Processing]]
>
> **Association cortex is where the integration story lives.** Areas that receive input from several modalities integrate across them, and this is where the language, attention and executive literature concentrates; the same region participates in several networks, which is why naming a function after a region invites confusion. [[F109 Neuroscience Online association and executive processing#^f109-areas|Association and Executive Processing]] [[Association cortex and networks]]
>
> **Reading rule.** Cortex is best described by the level that the evidence addresses: laminar and columnar organisation for local circuit claims, area maps for connectivity claims, and networks for statistical claims about coordinated activity. Mixing those levels produces claims that cannot be tested. [[F52 OpenStax Central processing#^f52-cortex|Central Processing]]

> [!info]- Further depth: macroscopic map, functional map and the difference
> The central nervous system is described region by region, and the cerebral cortex's lobes take their names from the overlying skull rather than from function; functional assignments are empirical findings rather than definitions. The neurological exam's domains - mental status, cranial nerves, motor, sensory, coordination and reflex testing - correspond to functional divisions that cut across the lobar map, so a deficit is localised by function and only then mapped onto anatomy. [[F05 OpenStax The central nervous system#^f05-parts|The Central Nervous System]] [[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-domains|Overview of the Neurological Exam]] [[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-purpose|Overview of the Neurological Exam]]
>
> Large cortical territories support distributed functions, which is why damage produces syndromes rather than single deficits: posterior association cortex integration and the parietal syndrome are the illustrative case. Structural MRI renders the map by reconstructing a signal from tissue properties, and cortical-thickness comparisons are anatomical measurements that require a separate argument before function can be inferred. ## Uncertainties

- Lobar boundaries are anatomical conventions; function does not respect them, and many processes span two or more lobes.
