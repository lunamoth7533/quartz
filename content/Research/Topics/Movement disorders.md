# Movement disorders

## Definition

Movement disorders are conditions of abnormal movement without primary weakness, arising mainly from basal ganglia and cerebellar dysfunction. They are classified by the phenomenology of the movement itself.

## How it works

**Phenomenology.** Resting tremor at about four to seven hertz, chorea, dystonia, rigidity and bradykinesia have different patterns and different typical associations, and the pattern is the first localising clue. [[F112 Neuroscience Online motor disorders#^f112-dyskinesias|Disorders of the Motor System]]

**Circuit logic.** Signs are mapped onto basal ganglia and cerebellar circuits rather than treated as isolated symptoms. [[F56 Neuroscience Online basal ganglia#^f56-pathways|Basal ganglia]] [[F57 Neuroscience Online cerebellum#^f57-layers|Cerebellum]]

**Parkinson's disease.** Degeneration of dopamine neurons produces the cardinal motor features, and treatment targets dopaminergic transmission. [[F76 MedlinePlus Parkinson's disease#^f76-mechanism|Parkinson's Disease]] Diagnosis rests on history and examination rather than a single test. [[F76 MedlinePlus Parkinson's disease#^f76-diagnosis|Parkinson's Disease]]

**Drug-induced movement disorders.** Dopamine-blocking drugs can produce extrapyramidal effects, which is why receptor occupancy and side effects are linked in antipsychotic pharmacology. [[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-d2|Antipsychotics mechanisms]]

## Evidence and status

Phenomenological classification and the association with specific circuits are well established; treatment effects vary by condition and stage.

> [!info]- In depth: circuits, sign patterns and what the exam can distinguish
> **The organising idea.** Symptoms arising from basal ganglia dysfunction are described as extrapyramidal disorders, distinguishing them from pyramidal-tract weakness; the underlying logic is that the basal ganglia modulate cortical output through direct and indirect pathways rather than generating movement themselves. The motor-disorders literature therefore maps signs to circuits instead of treating them as isolated symptoms. [[F112 Neuroscience Online motor disorders#^f112-extrapyramidal|Motor Disorders]] [[F56 Neuroscience Online basal ganglia#^f56-pathways|Basal Ganglia]] [[F112 Neuroscience Online motor disorders#^f112-mapping|Motor Disorders]]
>
> **Sign patterns carry localising information.** Resting tremor at about four to seven hertz is most often associated with Parkinson's disease, and tremor, chorea and dystonia are distinguished by pattern - when the movement occurs, how it is recruited, and whether it is suppressible - which is why the clinical description precedes any test. The cerebellum contributes a separate layer: its cortex has three layers with Purkinje cells forming a single-cell-thick layer, and cerebellar signs are grouped as coordination rather than movement-initiation problems. [[F112 Neuroscience Online motor disorders#^f112-dyskinesias|Motor Disorders]] [[F57 Neuroscience Online cerebellum#^f57-layers|Cerebellum]]
>
> **The Parkinson's example.** Parkinson's disease is described as a progressive disorder in which dopamine-producing neurons degenerate, producing movement symptoms, and there is no single definitive test as described: diagnosis rests on history and examination, with tests used to support or exclude alternatives. That combination - a well-understood neurotransmitter loss and a clinical diagnosis - is the clearest illustration of mechanism not equalling test. [[F76 MedlinePlus Parkinson's disease#^f76-mechanism|Parkinson's Disease]] [[F76 MedlinePlus Parkinson's disease#^f76-diagnosis|Parkinson's Disease]]
>
> **Drug-induced movement signs share the mechanism.** Striatal dopamine D2 receptor blockade is the shared mechanism behind antipsychotic clinical response and also behind endocrine and motor side effects, which implies a therapeutic window rather than a simple dose logic. A movement sign in someone taking an antipsychotic is therefore a mechanistic prediction, not an unrelated event. [[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-d2|Antipsychotics mechanisms]] [[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-offtarget|Antipsychotics mechanisms]]
>
> **Reading rule.** Name the sign, the circuit it implicates, and the evidence class - examination pattern, pharmacological response, imaging - before quoting a diagnosis. Movement disorders are the domain where the classical pathway model has been most refined since, so the textbook account is a starting frame rather than the final description. [[F56 Neuroscience Online basal ganglia#^f56-limit|Basal Ganglia]] [[F112 Neuroscience Online motor disorders#^f112-limit|Motor Disorders]]
>
> **Hypokinetic versus hyperkinetic organises the family.** Parkinsonism - bradykinesia with rigidity and resting tremor - is the hypokinetic pole; chorea, dystonia and tics are hyperkinetic phenomena. The two poles map onto different circuit disruptions in the basal ganglia's direct and indirect pathways, which is why the phenomenological distinction is not descriptive bookkeeping but the first circuit-level inference. The pattern also predicts pharmacology: dopaminergic loss and dopaminergic overstimulation produce opposite poles. [[F112 Neuroscience Online motor disorders#^f112-dyskinesias|Disorders of the Motor System]] [[F56 Neuroscience Online basal ganglia#^f56-pathways|Basal Ganglia]]
>
> **Diagnosis remains clinical, and that is informative.** Parkinson's disease diagnosis rests on history and examination rather than a single test, because the degeneration is not directly observable in life and imaging modalities measure structure and metabolism rather than the specific nigrostriatal loss. That keeps the movement-disorder examination - the phenomenology described above - central to diagnosis rather than a preliminary to technology.
