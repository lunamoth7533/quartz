# Neurodegeneration and cognitive syndromes

## Definition

Neurodegeneration is progressive loss of neuronal structure and function. Cognitive syndromes such as dementia describe the clinical consequence; the pathological diagnosis refers to tissue findings that may only be established definitively after death.

## How it works

**Age-related change.** Several measures increase with age, including ventricular volume, glial number and size, myelin thickness and the presence of plaques and tangles, so ageing and disease overlap in measured features. [[F113 Neuroscience Online CNS aging and Alzheimer's disease#^f113-aging|CNS Aging and Alzheimer's Disease]]

**Alzheimer's disease.** The commonest neurodegenerative disorder, beginning with mild memory deficits and progressing; clinical criteria distinguish probable from definite diagnosis, with definite requiring pathological confirmation. [[F113 Neuroscience Online CNS aging and Alzheimer's disease#^f113-ad|CNS Aging and Alzheimer's Disease]] [[F113 Neuroscience Online CNS aging and Alzheimer's disease#^f113-diagnosis|CNS Aging and Alzheimer's Disease]]

**Heterogeneity.** Different diseases produce overlapping syndromes, and mixed pathology is common, which is why clinical labels and tissue diagnoses do not map one to one.

**Cholinergic link.** Degeneration of the basal forebrain cholinergic projection is one pathology associated with Alzheimer's disease, which motivated cholinergic treatments. [[F108 Neuroscience Online acetylcholine#^f108-alzheimer|Acetylcholine transmission]]

## Evidence and status

Pathological descriptions are well established; the accuracy of clinical diagnosis against pathology is good but imperfect, and treatment effects on symptoms are modest relative to disease progression.

> [!info]- In depth: progressive loss, cognitive syndromes and the limits of a general account
> **The category.** Neurodegeneration covers progressive disorders in which neurons are lost, with different protein and circuit targets producing different clinical syndromes. The Alzheimer's material in this library describes the disease pattern - progressive cognitive decline with characteristic pathological changes - at an introductory level, which is enough to establish the shape of the category without supporting specific claims about staging or treatment. [[F113 Neuroscience Online CNS aging and Alzheimer's disease#^f113-aging|CNS Aging and Alzheimer's Disease]] [[F113 Neuroscience Online CNS aging and Alzheimer's disease#^f113-limit|CNS Aging and Alzheimer's Disease]]
>
> **Coordination and cognitive concerns are part of care.** Guidance for a progressive neurological condition addresses coordination of care, information and support alongside disease-modifying treatment, and treats symptom management - including cognitive concerns - as standard care rather than an afterthought. That structure applies to neurodegenerative care generally: the disease process and the functional consequences are managed in parallel. [[F96 NICE Multiple sclerosis NG220#^f96-care|Multiple sclerosis NG220]] [[F96 NICE Multiple sclerosis NG220#^f96-symptoms|Multiple sclerosis NG220]]
>
> **Functioning, not just test scores.** The ICF describes functioning through activities, participation and environmental factors, with the linked WHODAS instrument covering cognition, mobility, self-care, getting along, life activities and participation. A functioning score summarises reported activity and participation rather than symptom severity or capacity, which is why dementia assessment and disability assessment answer different questions. [[F23 WHO International Classification of Functioning#^f23-domains|International Classification of Functioning]] [[F23 WHO International Classification of Functioning#^f23-caution-impairment|AI appraisal: International Classification of Functioning]]
>
> **Why the exam still matters.** The neurological exam is a structured set of tests whose purpose is to identify where function is lost so that a deficit can be attributed to a region or pathway rather than to a symptom name, with domains corresponding to functional divisions of the nervous system. In a progressive condition the exam's value is longitudinal: change over time localises where the process is advancing. [[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-purpose|Overview of the Neurological Exam]] [[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-domains|Overview of the Neurological Exam]]
>
> **Reading rule.** Name the syndrome, the stage and the measurement, and keep pathological diagnosis separate from clinical syndrome. Group-level findings in this area describe samples; they do not diagnose or prognosticate for an individual. [[P28461699 Hibar 2018 ENIGMA cortical abnormalities in bipolar disorder#^p28461699-caution-groups|AI appraisal: ENIGMA cortical abnormalities in bipolar disorder]]
>
> **Ageing and disease share measured features, which is the interpretive trap.** Ventricular enlargement, gliosis, myelin change, plaques and tangles all increase with age in people without dementia, so a cross-sectional imaging or pathological finding must be interpreted against age expectations rather than treated as disease evidence on its own. The clinical criteria handle this by requiring functional decline - the syndrome is clinical before it is radiological or pathological. [[F113 Neuroscience Online CNS aging and Alzheimer's disease#^f113-aging|CNS Aging and Alzheimer's Disease]] [[F113 Neuroscience Online CNS aging and Alzheimer's disease#^f113-diagnosis|CNS Aging and Alzheimer's Disease]]
>
> **The clinical-pathological mismatch is structural, not an error.** Different proteinopathies produce overlapping syndromes, mixed pathology is common in the elderly, and the same disease produces different syndromes depending on distribution - so clinical labels (amnestic dementia, behavioural-variant syndrome) and tissue diagnoses name different kinds of kind. Research that needs pathological certainty uses different categories from clinical care, and neither is wrong.
