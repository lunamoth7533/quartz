# Transmitter synthesis, release and clearance

## Definition

Every small-molecule transmitter passes through the same functional stages: it is synthesised from precursors by specific enzymes, concentrated into vesicles, released on calcium entry, and then removed from the synaptic cleft. Drugs can act at any of these stages.

## How it works

**Synthesis.** Transmitter identity depends on which enzymes a neuron expresses. Biogenic amines are grouped into catecholamines and indolamines by synthetic pathway, and acetylcholine is made by a distinct route from choline and acetyl groups. [[F62 Neuroscience Online biogenic amines#^f62-classes|Biogenic amines]] [[F108 Neuroscience Online acetylcholine#^f108-inactivation|Acetylcholine transmission]] Amino acid transmitters are made from ordinary metabolic intermediates: glutamate from the tricarboxylic acid cycle and GABA from glutamate. [[P12467378 Petroff 2002 GABA and glutamate#^p12467378-metabolism|GABA and glutamate]]

**Release.** Calcium entry into the terminal triggers fusion of docked vesicles, and release occurs in quantal units. [[F115 Neuroscience Online transmitter release#^f115-calcium|Transmitter release]] [[F115 Neuroscience Online transmitter release#^f115-quanta|Transmitter release]] Peptides differ here: they are processed from larger precursors through successive enzymatic steps, making their supply slower to replenish. [[F107 Neuroscience Online neuropeptides and nitric oxide#^f107-processing|Neuropeptides and nitric oxide]]

**Clearance.** Reuptake transporters and degrading enzymes terminate the signal; the balance between them differs by transmitter. Acetylcholine is primarily degraded enzymatically, whereas monoamines are largely transported back into the terminal. [[F108 Neuroscience Online acetylcholine#^f108-inactivation|Acetylcholine transmission]]

## Evidence and status

Each stage is established biochemically and electrophysiologically in identified preparations. Stage-level knowledge explains why drugs have specific molecular targets; it does not predict clinical effect sizes. [[P28153641 Harmer 2017 How do antidepressants work#^p28153641-monoamine|How do antidepressants work?]]

> [!info]- In depth: the life cycle of a transmitter, step by step
> **Synthesis.** Small-molecule transmitters are built locally from precursors by enzymes in the terminal: acetylcholine from choline and acetyl-CoA, the catecholamines from tyrosine through successive steps, serotonin from tryptophan, and GABA from glutamate. Because each step is enzymatic, precursor availability and enzyme activity together set the ceiling on how much transmitter a terminal can supply. [[F62 Neuroscience Online biogenic amines#^f62-classes|Biogenic Amines]] [[F108 Neuroscience Online acetylcholine#^f108-inactivation|Acetylcholine]]
>
> **Packaging and release.** Transmitter is concentrated into vesicles, and release occurs when calcium entry triggers fusion with the presynaptic membrane. Because only a fraction of vesicles is immediately available, release probability depends on what has happened recently as much as on the number of release sites, which is why a synapse's output is history-dependent. [[F04 OpenStax Communication between neurons#^f04-release|Communication Between Neurons]] [[F115 Neuroscience Online transmitter release#^f115-requirements|Mechanisms of Neurotransmitter Release]]
>
> **Clearance.** Reuptake transporters return transmitter to the terminal for repackaging, while enzymatic degradation handles what escapes; for acetylcholine the enzyme route dominates and the recovered choline is reused. Clearance is not cleanup but the reset step that makes the next signal interpretable, and slowing it changes both amplitude and duration of the postsynaptic response. [[F06 OpenStax Cells of the nervous system#^f06-receptor|Cells of the Nervous System]] [[F108 Neuroscience Online acetylcholine#^f108-inactivation|Acetylcholine]]
>
> **Turnover as a measurable quantity.** Because synthesis, release and clearance run continuously, transmitter content in a terminal reflects a balance rather than a store size, and measurements of turnover (through metabolites or labelled precursors) capture that balance over time rather than a single moment. This is why peripheral metabolite measurements are only indirect indices of central signalling. [[F62 Neuroscience Online biogenic amines#^f62-limit|Biogenic Amines]] [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What happens to medicine in your body]]
>
> **Where drugs enter the cycle.** A drug can act at any step: precursor supply, synthetic enzymes, vesicular packaging, release machinery, receptors, reuptake transporters or degradation enzymes. Knowing which step a drug touches explains its onset, its duration and much of its side-effect profile, and it is the minimum needed for a mechanistic statement that goes beyond a drug's class name. [[F13 NIGMS How do medicines work#^f13-targets|How do medicines work]] [[Monoamine reuptake and degradation]]

> [!info]- Further depth: the life cycle that sets the timescale
> Transmitters are made in the terminal from precursors by specific enzymes, so synthesis depends on precursor availability and enzyme activity, and for biogenic amines the pathways differ by class - the reason pharmacological manipulation can be transmitter-selective. Release is calcium-triggered, established in classic experiments at the skeletal neuromuscular junction, and depends jointly on calcium availability and the state of the release machinery, so the same spike can release different amounts. [[F62 Neuroscience Online biogenic amines#^f62-classes|Biogenic Amines]] [[F115 Neuroscience Online transmitter release#^f115-calcium|Mechanisms of Neurotransmitter Release]] [[F115 Neuroscience Online transmitter release#^f115-requirements|Mechanisms of Neurotransmitter Release]]
>
> Clearance by reuptake and enzymatic degradation determines how long a signal lasts and is the target of many psychotropic drugs: blocking a transporter prolongs the signal rather than increasing release, which matters when an effect is attributed to 'more transmitter'. Fast amino-acid transmission operates on millisecond timescales while monoamines and neuropeptides act as modulators over seconds to minutes. ## Uncertainties

- Quantifying release and clearance in the intact human brain is not possible with current methods; estimates come from animals, tissue and indirect measures.
- Transmitter levels measured in blood or cerebrospinal fluid are not direct indices of synaptic activity.
