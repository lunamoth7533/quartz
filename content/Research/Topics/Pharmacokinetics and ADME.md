# Pharmacokinetics and ADME

**Definition.** Pharmacokinetics describes what the body does to a drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion. [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What Happens to Medicine in Your Body?]]

## Supported claims

- ADME describes the journey of a drug through the body from absorption to elimination. [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What Happens to Medicine in Your Body?]]
- Bioavailability is the fraction of a dose that reaches the bloodstream in active form and varies with route and formulation. [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-bioavailability|What Happens to Medicine in Your Body?]]

> [!info]- In depth: four processes that decide how much drug reaches the target
> **Absorption and bioavailability.** Bioavailability is the fraction of a dose that reaches the bloodstream in active form, and it varies with route, formulation and first-pass metabolism. Two products with the same nominal dose can differ in exposure, which is why formulation and route belong to the mechanism description rather than to logistics. [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-bioavailability|What happens to medicine in your body]]
>
> **Distribution.** Once absorbed, a drug distributes according to blood flow, tissue affinity and protein binding; the fraction bound to plasma proteins is inactive, and the blood-brain barrier restricts entry for many compounds, so central and peripheral effects can diverge in both magnitude and timing. [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What happens to medicine in your body]] [[F105 Neuroscience Online blood-brain barrier#^f105-transport|Blood Brain Barrier]]
>
> **Metabolism and excretion.** Metabolism usually terminates activity, but for prodrugs or active metabolites it can generate it; excretion clears the drug and its products. Because metabolism depends on enzyme populations that vary between people, the same dose can produce different exposure - the mechanistic reason dose-response curves differ between individuals. [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-limit|What happens to medicine in your body]]
>
> **Reading rule.** Exposure is a time course, not a number: a concentration at one moment says nothing about steady-state behaviour, and statements about a drug's effect should say whether they refer to the drug, its metabolites or the active moiety. [[Half-life and steady state]]

> [!info]- Reference: the four processes and what each determines
> **Absorption.** Absorption determines how much of a dose reaches the circulation and how quickly. Bioavailability is the fraction of a dose that reaches the bloodstream in active form and depends on route and formulation. [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What happens to medicine in your body]] [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-bioavailability|What happens to medicine in your body]]
>
> **Distribution.** Distribution depends on blood flow, tissue affinity and protein binding, which is why the same plasma concentration can correspond to different tissue concentrations.
>
> **Metabolism.** Most metabolism occurs in the liver and can convert a drug into active, inactive or toxic products; genetic variation in metabolising enzymes changes exposure for a given dose. [[F21 OpenStax Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics#^f21-model|Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics]]
>
> **Excretion.** Elimination determines how quickly concentrations fall, and renal or hepatic impairment changes it. Lithium provides a concrete case: elimination is renal and proportional to serum concentration, which is why kidney function matters. [[F27 DailyMed lithium carbonate labelling#^f27-clearance|Lithium carbonate labelling]]
>
> **Why the model matters.** These processes explain why two people can take the same dose and reach different concentrations, and why some drugs are monitored and others are not. [[F18 FDA Narrow therapeutic index drugs#^f18-window|Narrow therapeutic index drugs]]

> [!info]- Further depth: the four processes and what they decide
> Absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion determine how much drug reaches its target and for how long; bioavailability is the fraction of a dose that reaches the bloodstream in active form and varies with route and formulation, so the same molecule given two ways is two different exposures. Distribution determines which compartments see the drug, and metabolism and excretion set the time course - which is why a target-based mechanism claim has to be paired with a pharmacokinetic one before it predicts anything. [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What happens to medicine in your body]] [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-bioavailability|What happens to medicine in your body]] [[F13 NIGMS How do medicines work#^f13-targets|How do medicines work]]

> [!info]- Further depth: what the four processes mean for a claim
> Route and formulation change bioavailability, so the same dose can produce different exposures; distribution determines which compartments see the drug, and metabolism and excretion set how long the exposure lasts. That is why mechanism and pharmacokinetics are separate halves of a single prediction: a target-based claim says what the drug would do if it reached the target, and the ADME profile says how much of it does and for how long. [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-bioavailability|What happens to medicine in your body]] [[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What happens to medicine in your body]] [[F13 NIGMS How do medicines work#^f13-targets|How do medicines work]]
>
> **Cross-domain connection (curation).** What a drug does centrally depends on what reaches the brain, which is where the blood-brain barrier and distribution material in the neuroendocrine-neuroimmune domain becomes relevant - a pharmacokinetics claim without that constraint is incomplete for central-nervous-system effects. ## Limitation or common misconception

These are averaged properties: metabolism, interactions and adherence move exposure away from the label value.
