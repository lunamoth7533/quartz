# Cerebrovascular disease

## Definition

Cerebrovascular disease includes stroke - interruption of blood supply by blockage or bleeding - and the chronic vascular changes that raise its risk. Brain tissue depends on continuous perfusion and has limited storage.

## How it works

**Mechanism.** A stroke occurs when blood flow to part of the brain is interrupted, and cells begin to die within minutes; the affected function corresponds to the territory involved. [[F74 MedlinePlus Stroke#^f74-mechanism|Stroke]]

**Risk.** High blood pressure, diabetes, heart disease, smoking and age are named as factors, which is why prevention is largely cardiovascular. [[F74 MedlinePlus Stroke#^f74-risk|Stroke]]

**Urgency.** Immediate emergency care and subsequent rehabilitation are emphasised, because time matters for tissue survival. [[F74 MedlinePlus Stroke#^f74-urgency|Stroke]]

**Vascular anatomy.** Because arterial supply has multiple routes and territories, the pattern of a deficit carries localising information, which is why vascular anatomy is taught alongside functional anatomy. [[F30 OpenStax Circulation and the central nervous system#^f30-stroke|Circulation and the Central Nervous System]]

## Evidence and status

Stroke mechanism, risk factors and time-dependence are well established; outcomes depend heavily on lesion size, location and time to care.

> [!info]- In depth: perfusion, time and the logic of focal deficits
> **Why interruption is so damaging.** Neural tissue depends on continuous perfusion and has limited storage, so interrupted supply produces focal deficits; the circulation section frames stroke in exactly those terms. A stroke occurs when blood flow to part of the brain is interrupted, either by a blockage or by bleeding, and brain cells begin to die within minutes. [[F30 OpenStax Circulation and the central nervous system#^f30-stroke|Circulation and the Central Nervous System]] [[F74 MedlinePlus Stroke#^f74-mechanism|Stroke]]
>
> **Risk factors are population-level, and one of them is age.** Named risk factors include high blood pressure, diabetes, heart disease and smoking, with higher risk in some population groups and age as a strong factor. Because these are distributed risk factors rather than causes in an individual, a person can have none of them and still have a stroke, and the clinical question is about the specific mechanism in that person. [[F74 MedlinePlus Stroke#^f74-risk|Stroke]]
>
> **Time and rehabilitation structure care.** The consumer overview emphasises immediate emergency care and rehabilitation afterwards and directs individual decisions to clinicians - which is the correct division of labour between a reference library and a clinical pathway. In the localisation framework, the level and pattern of a deficit carry information about where the interruption occurred; spinal cord organisation, where ascending and descending tracts occupy characteristic positions, is the same principle at a different level. > **Where the reference account stops.** The sources here describe presentation, risk and urgency at a general level without trial detail, so nothing in this note supports statements about thrombolysis windows, secondary prevention choices or prognosis. Mechanism and urgency are what a reference treatment can carry. >
> **Reading rule.** Separate vascular territory, mechanism (ischaemic versus haemorrhagic), time course and deficit pattern; the four together are what a localisation argument needs. [[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-causes|Overview of the Neurological Exam]]
>
> **Territory is the localising principle.** Arterial territories are anatomically regular, so a deficit pattern that respects a territory (a full middle-cerebral-arm-and-face pattern, a posterior-circulation visual field loss) points to the blocked vessel, while patterns that cross territories suggest different mechanisms. This is the clearest case in neurology of vascular anatomy doing diagnostic work, and it is why stroke examination is taught territory-first. [[F30 OpenStax Circulation and the central nervous system#^f30-stroke|Circulation and the Central Nervous System]] [[F74 MedlinePlus Stroke#^f74-mechanism|Stroke]]
>
> **The psychiatric overlap runs both ways.** Post-stroke depression is common enough to be part of routine post-stroke care conversations, and vascular risk factors are shared with the small-vessel disease that contributes to late-life cognitive decline - so cerebrovascular disease belongs in psychiatric differentials for mood and cognitive change, not only in neurology. The direction of causation in any individual case is an assessment question, not an assumption. [[Neurodegeneration and cognitive syndromes]] [[Mood disorders]]
>
> **Cross-domain connection (curation).** The time-critical nature of stroke treatment is the clinical expression of the metabolic dependency described in the neurobiology domain: interrupted perfusion produces deficits in seconds to minutes because signalling and maintenance are continuously energy-paid. [[Neuronal cell biology and energetics]] [[History and localisation]]
>
> **Prevention is where the population impact lives.** Because the named risk factors - blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, atrial fibrillation, age - are measurable and partly modifiable, most stroke burden reduction happens before any acute event, through risk-factor management rather than acute care. That makes the epidemiological literature on these factors the highest-impact part of the cerebrovascular story, alongside the acute-treatment literature that the urgency framing emphasises.
