# Membrane transport and ion channels

## Definition

Neuronal excitability depends on proteins embedded in the membrane: pumps that establish concentration gradients, channels that let ions cross down those gradients, and transporters that move transmitters and ions against them.

## How it works

**Gradients.** Pumps maintain sodium, potassium, calcium and chloride gradients at the cost of adenosine triphosphate; the resulting separation of charge produces the resting potential. [[F03 OpenStax The action potential#^f03-gradient|The Action Potential]] **Selectivity.** Channels are selective because of their pore structure, and gating decides when they open. Voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels open in sequence during a spike. [[F03 OpenStax The action potential#^f03-threshold|The Action Potential]] **Leak and modulation.** Leak conductances set resting excitability, and neuromodulators alter channel properties to change how a cell responds to the same input. [[P23040802 Marder 2012 Neuromodulation#^p23040802-reconfigure|Neuromodulation]] **Transporters.** Reuptake transporters clear transmitter from the synaptic cleft, and vesicular transporters load it into vesicles. [[F108 Neuroscience Online acetylcholine#^f108-inactivation|Acetylcholine transmission]]

**Why molecular detail matters clinically.** Most psychoactive drugs act on this machinery: receptors, transporters, channels or the enzymes that degrade transmitter. [[F13 NIGMS How do medicines work|How do medicines work]] Knowing that a drug blocks a transporter says nothing about clinical effect size, which has to be measured separately. [[P28153641 Harmer 2017 How do antidepressants work#^p28153641-monoamine|How do antidepressants work]]

## Evidence and status

Channel biophysics is one of the most quantitatively established areas of neuroscience, with single-channel recording and structure determination. Behavioural consequences of specific channels are established for some mutations and inferred for most common variation. [[F117 Neuroscience Online genetics and neuronal disease#^f117-disease|Genetics and neuronal disease]]

> [!info]- In depth: channels, carriers and pumps as three different machines
> **Three mechanisms, three jobs.** Passive movement through channels follows the electrochemical gradient and requires no energy; carrier-mediated transport moves solutes with or against their gradient depending on the carrier; and active pumps use ATP to move ions against their gradients, which is what makes the gradients exist in the first place. Every signalling event on the membrane is one of these three mechanisms either opening, closing or being consumed. [[F03 OpenStax The action potential#^f03-gradient|The Action Potential]] [[F02 OpenStax Nervous tissue#^f02-myelin|Nervous Tissue]]
>
> **Channels are gated, and gating is the biology.** The conductances that produce a spike open and close in a defined order: sodium channels open at threshold and then inactivate, while potassium channels open more slowly and close slowly, producing the undershoot. Inactivation is a distinct state from closing, which is why a channel that has just opened cannot immediately reopen and why the refractory period exists as a real physiological limit rather than an artefact. [[F03 OpenStax The action potential#^f03-threshold|The Action Potential]] [[F03 OpenStax The action potential#^f03-signals|The Action Potential]]
>
> **Selectivity and distribution set the cell's repertoire.** A channel permeable to one ion family, placed in a particular membrane domain, determines whether a cell responds to a signal with depolarisation, hyperpolarisation or a change in second messengers. Where channels sit matters as much as which ones are expressed: dense sodium channel clustering at the initial segment is what makes that region the trigger zone, and receptor-specific effects at the synapse are the same principle at a smaller scale. [[F04 OpenStax Communication between neurons#^f04-initial-segment|Communication Between Neurons]] [[F04 OpenStax Communication between neurons#^f04-receptor|Communication Between Neurons]]
>
> **Transporters recycle what release spends.** Terminating a signal requires removing the transmitter from the cleft, and for the monoamines that is largely the job of reuptake transporters; enzymatic breakdown provides a second route, and recovered precursors are reused. Pharmacologically this is a leverage point: reuptake inhibitors keep transmitters active in the cleft longer, which is a transport effect rather than a receptor effect, and the two are often confused in informal descriptions. [[F06 OpenStax Cells of the nervous system#^f06-receptor|Cells of the Nervous System]] [[F108 Neuroscience Online acetylcholine#^f108-inactivation|Acetylcholine]]
>
> **Water and ions across the barrier.** Barrier function is selective rather than absolute: gases diffuse freely while ions, nutrients and larger molecules depend on specific transporters. That selectivity means the extracellular ionic environment is actively maintained rather than inherited from plasma, which is why disturbances in transport show up as excitability changes. [[F105 Neuroscience Online blood-brain barrier#^f105-transport|Blood Brain Barrier]] [[F105 Neuroscience Online blood-brain barrier#^f105-tight|Blood Brain Barrier]]
>
> **Where pharmacology enters.** Most psychoactive drugs act on receptors, transporters, channels or the enzymes that degrade transmitter, and the same target appears in many tissues, which is why effects are rarely single. Reading a drug's mechanism therefore requires the target list plus the tissue distribution, not the target name alone. ## Uncertainties

- Receptor and channel subtypes are numerous, and assigning a behavioural role to one subtype usually requires selective tools that do not exist for every target.
- Human variation in these genes is common, and inferring function from sequence alone is unreliable. [[P29844615 Schaid 2018 Fine-mapping#^p29844615-limits|Fine-mapping]]
