# Headache and pain

## Definition

Headache disorders include primary conditions such as migraine and secondary headaches caused by another process. Pain is a subjective experience produced by nociceptive signalling and central processing, not simply a peripheral signal.

## How it works

**Nociception.** Nociceptors are free nerve endings responding to damaging or potentially damaging stimuli and to chemicals released by damaged tissue. [[F111 Neuroscience Online pain principles#^f111-nociceptors|Pain Principles]]

**Sensitisation.** Tissue damage releases substances that sensitise nociceptors, which is why the same stimulus becomes more painful after injury. [[F111 Neuroscience Online pain principles#^f111-inflammation|Pain Principles]] [[F111 Neuroscience Online pain principles#^f111-transmission|Pain Principles]]

**Migraine.** A recurring headache disorder with attacks that can include nausea and sensory sensitivity, varying between people; assessment may include imaging to exclude other causes in specific presentations. [[F77 MedlinePlus Migraine#^f77-attack|Migraine]] [[F77 MedlinePlus Migraine#^f77-patterns|Migraine]] [[F77 MedlinePlus Migraine#^f77-care|Migraine]]

## Evidence and status

Migraine mechanisms involve trigeminovascular and cortical processes; treatment effects are established in randomised trials, and this note does not cover them. [[F111 Neuroscience Online pain principles#^f111-limit|Pain Principles]]

> [!info]- In depth: nociception, sensitisation and the migraine example
> **The peripheral machinery.** Nociceptors are free nerve endings that respond to damaging or potentially damaging stimuli and to chemicals released by damaged tissue. Tissue damage triggers release of substances that sensitise nociceptors, which is how the same stimulus can become painful after injury - the mechanism behind the everyday observation that a healed area can remain tender. [[F111 Neuroscience Online pain principles#^f111-nociceptors|Pain Principles]] [[F111 Neuroscience Online pain principles#^f111-inflammation|Pain Principles]]
>
> **Modulation happens at several levels.** Pain information is transmitted to the central nervous system through defined pathways, and modulation occurs at several levels rather than only at the periphery. That is why pain intensity is not a simple readout of tissue damage, and why psychological and contextual factors change reported pain without implying that the pain is unreal. [[F111 Neuroscience Online pain principles#^f111-transmission|Pain Principles]] [[F111 Neuroscience Online pain principles#^f111-limit|Pain Principles]]
>
> **Migraine as a recurring disorder.** Migraine is described as a recurring headache disorder with attacks that can include nausea, sensitivity to light and sound, and visual or sensory changes, and the overview distinguishes migraine with and without aura and notes that attack frequency and triggers vary between people. Assessment may include imaging to exclude other causes in specific presentations, and management is described as individualised and clinician-led. [[F77 MedlinePlus Migraine#^f77-attack|Migraine]] [[F77 MedlinePlus Migraine#^f77-patterns|Migraine]] [[F77 MedlinePlus Migraine#^f77-care|Migraine]] [[F77 MedlinePlus Migraine#^f77-limit|Migraine]]
>
> **Why the reference account stops at physiology.** The pain chapter covers nociceptive mechanisms and does not address chronic pain management, and the migraine page describes care at a general level. Nothing here supports statements about specific acute or preventive treatments, which live in the clinical literature and in guidelines rather than in a physiology reference. [[F111 Neuroscience Online pain principles#^f111-limit|Pain Principles]] [[F77 MedlinePlus Migraine#^f77-limit|Migraine]]
>
> **Reading rule.** Separate nociception, the perception of pain, and the suffering and disability around it; then state which of the three a claim is about. The three are related but not interchangeable, and most overstatement in this area comes from treating one as a proxy for another. [[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-domains|Overview of the Neurological Exam]]
>
> **Primary versus secondary is the assessment's first fork.** Headache disorders are classified as primary - migraine, tension-type, cluster - when the headache is the disorder itself, and secondary when it signals another cause; the assessment task is distinguishing them, because red-flag patterns (sudden onset, progressive course, systemic signs, neurological deficits) change the workup rather than the presumed diagnosis. The migraine article's imaging caveat fits here: imaging is for specific presentations, not routine confirmation of a typical primary headache. [[F77 MedlinePlus Migraine#^f77-patterns|Migraine]] [[F77 MedlinePlus Migraine#^f77-care|Migraine]]
>
> **Sensitisation explains the chronicification pattern.** Peripheral sensitisation lowers nociceptor thresholds after tissue injury; central sensitisation amplifies processing further up, so pain can persist after healing and spread beyond the original territory. This is the mechanism-level reason chronic pain is a disorder of the nervous system rather than a measure of tissue damage, and why pain assessment asks about function and sleep, not only intensity. [[F111 Neuroscience Online pain principles#^f111-inflammation|Pain Principles]] [[F111 Neuroscience Online pain principles#^f111-transmission|Pain Principles]]
>
> **Cross-domain connection (curation).** The psychology domain's emotion-regulation and attention material meets pain through the modulation systems - attention and context change what is felt because the pathway is built to be modulated - which is also why psychological pain management has a mechanism-level rationale rather than being merely supportive.
