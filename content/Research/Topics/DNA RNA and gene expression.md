# DNA, RNA and gene expression

## Definition

DNA stores hereditary information in a nucleotide sequence; RNA carries and regulates that information; proteins do most of the work in cells. The central dogma describes the flow from DNA to RNA to protein, with replication, transcription and translation as distinct processes. [[F71 OpenStax The genetic code#^f71-dogma|The Genetic Code]]

## How it works

**The code.** Nucleotides are read in three-base codons; most amino acids are specified by more than one codon, and stop codons terminate translation. Ribosomes, transfer RNAs and the reading frame together determine which protein is produced. [[F71 OpenStax The genetic code#^f71-codons|The Genetic Code]] [[F71 OpenStax The genetic code#^f71-machinery|The Genetic Code]]

**Expression is regulated.** Which genes are transcribed, when and in which cells determines cell identity, so the same genome produces liver and cortex. Epigenomic marks change how cells use DNA without altering the sequence. [[F69 NHGRI Epigenomics fact sheet#^f69-definition|Epigenomics fact sheet]]

**Historical method.** The identification of DNA as the carrier of inherited information came from indirect experiments, including the transforming principle in bacteria - a useful reminder that genetic inference is inferential. [[F70 OpenStax Historical basis of DNA#^f70-transforming|Historical Basis of Modern Understanding]]

## Evidence and status

Molecular biology is among the most quantitatively established areas of biology; structure-function claims for individual genes in behaviour are far less certain. [[F117 Neuroscience Online genetics and neuronal disease#^f117-inference|Genetics and neuronal disease]]

> [!info]- In depth: the pipeline, its regulation and why regulation is where the interest is
> **The central pipeline.** DNA is transcribed into RNA and translated into protein, with the genetic code defining how nucleotide triplets map to amino acids; the code is degenerate, so several codons specify the same residue. That degeneracy is one reason a sequence change does not automatically change a protein. [[F71 OpenStax The genetic code#^f71-dogma|The Genetic Code]] [[F71 OpenStax The genetic code#^f71-codons|The Genetic Code]]
>
> **Regulation is the interesting layer.** Expression is controlled by promoters, enhancers and chromatin state, so the same genome produces different proteins in different cells, and regulatory variation is therefore a major route from genotype to phenotype. Epigenomic marks change how cells use DNA rather than altering the sequence, and they are part of how different cell types maintain different states. [[F70 OpenStax Historical basis of DNA#^f70-identity|Historical Basis of DNA]] [[F07 OpenStax Human genetics#^f07-expression|Human Genetics]] [[F69 NHGRI Epigenomics fact sheet#^f69-definition|Epigenomics fact sheet]]
>
> **Mapping marks is a research programme.** The epigenomics literature describes mapping marks such as DNA methylation and chromatin features, including large reference projects that catalogue them across tissues, and presents epigenomic changes in disease as ongoing work rather than as established mechanisms for specific disorders. [[F69 NHGRI Epigenomics fact sheet#^f69-methods|Epigenomics fact sheet]] [[F69 NHGRI Epigenomics fact sheet#^f69-disease|Epigenomics fact sheet]] [[F69 NHGRI Epigenomics fact sheet#^f69-limit|Epigenomics fact sheet]]
>
> **Why this matters for psychiatry.** Because most associated variants lie in non-coding regions, the mechanistic question is usually about regulation - which gene, which tissue, when - rather than about protein structure, which is why fine-mapping and functional annotation are central. [[P29844615 Schaid 2018 Fine-mapping#^p29844615-problem|Fine-mapping]] [[P29844615 Schaid 2018 Fine-mapping#^p29844615-limits|Fine-mapping]]
>
> **Reading rule.** Distinguish sequence, expression, mark and function; a variant near a gene is not a variant that changes that gene's product, and a methylation difference in blood is not a measurement of brain state. [[F69 NHGRI Epigenomics fact sheet#^f69-disease|Epigenomics fact sheet]] [[P29844615 Schaid 2018 Fine-mapping#^p29844615-limits|Fine-mapping]]
>
> **Why the code's degeneracy and regulation both blunt determinism.** Because several codons specify the same amino acid, many sequence changes change nothing at the protein level; and because expression is controlled by promoters, enhancers and chromatin state, most changes that do something do so by changing where, when or how much a gene is used. Both facts push the genotype-to-phenotype chain through regulation rather than through protein replacement, which is why regulatory annotation dominates post-GWAS interpretation. [[F71 OpenStax The genetic code#^f71-codons|The Genetic Code]] [[F69 NHGRI Epigenomics fact sheet#^f69-definition|Epigenomics fact sheet]]
>
> **Cell identity is maintained by marks, which is their promise and their measurement problem.** The marks that keep a neuron a neuron - methylation patterns, chromatin states - are stable across cell division and tunable by experience; the same properties make them attractive as records of exposure and hard to read, because marks differ by tissue and a accessible-tissue sample is not the tissue of interest unless cross-tissue correspondence has been shown. The gene-environment article carries that inference chain in detail. [[F69 NHGRI Epigenomics fact sheet#^f69-methods|Epigenomics fact sheet]] [[Gene-environment interplay and epigenetics]]
>
> **Cross-domain connection (curation).** Computational-neuroscience readers meet this material where genetic influence on circuits is modelled - gene-expression programs shaping ion-channel and receptor complements - and the honest bridge is that those models inherit the same regulatory complexity described here rather than simplifying it away. [[Genome-wide association studies]] [[Gene-environment interplay and epigenetics]]
