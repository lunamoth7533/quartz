# Neuronal cell biology and energetics

## Definition

A neuron is a highly polarised cell whose compartments are separated by large distances, so the cell must solve logistics problems that most cells do not: making proteins in the soma, delivering them to distal processes, and supplying adenosine triphosphate where the work happens.

## How it works

**Compartments and synthesis.** The soma carries the nucleus and most protein synthesis; dendrites, axons and terminals receive material by transport. Dendrites and their spines contain most of the organelles found in the cell body, including ordered arrays of microtubules, which is how local structure is maintained far from the nucleus. [[F102 Neuroscience Online cell types#^f102-dendrites|Cell types]] [[F102 Neuroscience Online cell types#^f102-compartments|Cell types]]

**Transport.** Axonal transport moves cargo along microtubules in both directions, and the machinery for secretion is carried to terminals along the same system. [[F108 Neuroscience Online acetylcholine#^f108-inactivation|Acetylcholine transmission]] Any process that disrupts transport therefore affects terminals before it affects the soma.

**Energy demand.** Restoring ion gradients after signalling consumes adenosine triphosphate, and most of the brain's energy budget is spent on that restoration rather than on the spike itself. Cerebral metabolism is tightly coupled to activity, which is the physiological fact that functional imaging relies on. [[F105 Neuroscience Online blood-brain barrier#^f105-transport|Blood Brain Barrier]]

## Evidence and status

Cellular anatomy and transport are established by microscopy, tracing and live imaging; the energy budget is established by metabolic measurement. [[F113 Neuroscience Online CNS aging and Alzheimer's disease#^f113-aging|CNS aging and Alzheimer's disease]] Mitochondrial and cellular-energy hypotheses of psychiatric conditions build on this base, but they are hypotheses about disease rather than established facts about the cell. [[F94 NICE Bipolar disorder CG185#^f94-assessment|Bipolar disorder CG185]]

> [!info]- In depth: the cell as a factory with a power budget
> **Compartments and their logistics.** A prototypical neuron has dendrites as input sites, a cell body containing the nucleus, and an axon carrying output to terminals; the compartments differ in protein composition and in what they contain. Because almost all protein synthesis happens in the soma while most of the membrane is far away, the cell depends on continuous transport along the cytoskeleton to keep distant terminals supplied. [[F02 OpenStax Nervous tissue#^f02-neuron|Nervous Tissue]] [[F102 Neuroscience Online cell types#^f102-compartments|Organization of Cell Types]]
>
> **Why the energy demand is structural, not incidental.** Restoring the ion gradients after signalling is the dominant cost of neuronal activity, and it is paid continuously, because the membrane leaks even at rest. Active transport therefore consumes ATP at a rate set by the resting conductance plus whatever signalling the cell has just done - which is why an interruption of supply produces deficits within seconds to minutes rather than hours. [[F03 OpenStax The action potential#^f03-gradient|The Action Potential]] [[F30 OpenStax Circulation and the central nervous system#^f30-stroke|Circulation and the Central Nervous System]]
>
> **Metabolic support is a tissue function.** Neural tissue holds limited storage and depends on continuous perfusion of glucose and oxygen, and the barrier restricts what can enter from blood, so the substrates must be transported rather than simply diffusing. The vascular arrangement around the central nervous system is correspondingly extensive and tightly regulated. [[F30 OpenStax Circulation and the central nervous system#^f30-privilege|Circulation and the Central Nervous System]] [[F105 Neuroscience Online blood-brain barrier#^f105-transport|Blood Brain Barrier]]
>
> **Glia participate in the metabolic arrangement.** Astrocytes contact both synapses and vessels, influence the extracellular environment, and are required for barrier properties, which places them in the position of coupling local activity to supply. That coupling is the basis for the blood-flow signals used in functional imaging, and its indirectness is exactly why such signals are read as estimates rather than as neural activity itself. [[F105 Neuroscience Online blood-brain barrier#^f105-support|Blood Brain Barrier]] [[F28 OpenStax Brain imaging Psychology 2e#^f28-fmri|Brain imaging]]
>
> **Maintenance, ageing and clearance.** With age, the tissue shows increases in ventricular volume, glial number and size, myelin thickness, and the presence of plaques and tangles in some proportion of people, and these changes are common enough that their mere presence does not diagnose disease. Distinguishing ageing from pathology requires distribution, quantity and clinical context, not a single marker. [[F113 Neuroscience Online CNS aging and Alzheimer's disease#^f113-aging|CNS Aging and Alzheimer's Disease]] [[F113 Neuroscience Online CNS aging and Alzheimer's disease#^f113-diagnosis|CNS Aging and Alzheimer's Disease]]

> [!info]- Further depth: why the brain's energy budget is unusual
> Restoring ion gradients after signalling consumes most of the brain's energy budget, so neurons are unusually dependent on continuous supply and mitochondrial function; the barrier between blood and brain is selective, with ions, nutrients and larger molecules depending on specific transporters rather than diffusing freely. That coupling explains both the vulnerability to interruption - brain cells begin to die within minutes of ischaemia - and the reason peripheral energy measurements are a long way from the relevant compartment. ## Uncertainties

- How local translation in dendrites contributes to plasticity in the human brain is difficult to measure directly.
- Energy measurements in animals and tissue preparations do not translate directly into rates in intact human cortex.
