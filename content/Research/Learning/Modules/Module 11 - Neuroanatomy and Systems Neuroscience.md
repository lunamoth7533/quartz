# Module 11 - Neuroanatomy and Systems Neuroscience

**Lessons.** 17 · **Entry point.** [[Learning Path]]

## What this module is for

Physical organisation of the nervous system, from shared directional vocabulary and the methods that
establish structure, through spinal, brainstem, cerebellar, diencephalic, cortical and subcortical regional
anatomy, to the systems - sensory, motor, interoceptive and large-scale network - that cut across regions.

Neuroanatomy is the most secure layer of neuroscience: where a structure sits and what it connects to are
observable, while what it "does" is an inference that depends on method, species and task. This module builds
the discipline of keeping those two apart, and of naming the loop, the pathway or the network rather than the
whole region, before crediting anatomy with a function.

## Learning objectives

1. Use a consistent directional and planar vocabulary, and recognise where the neuraxis bend changes what a
   direction word means.
2. State which method (tracing, lesion, staining, or a named imaging modality) produced a given connectivity
   or functional claim, and what that method does and does not establish.
3. Trace how segmental, tract and loop organisation in the spinal cord, brainstem, cerebellum, basal ganglia
   and thalamus predicts a testable pattern of deficit or function.
4. Explain why a lobe, a cortical layer or column, or a named large-scale network is a coarse address or a
   statistical summary rather than a function in itself.
5. Read condition-linked findings in the prefrontal cortex, insula, and default mode, salience and executive
   networks for their sector, measure, population, state and effect size before generalising them.
6. Distinguish an anatomical tract from a statistical correlation and from a modelled effective influence when
   any of the three is called "connectivity."

## Core and advanced branches

**Core sequence**

- Read the vocabulary and methods lessons first, then the regions bottom-up along the neuraxis (cord,
  brainstem and cerebellum, diencephalon, cortex), then the subcortical and systems lessons.
- For every structure-function claim, name the region, nucleus, pathway or loop, and the method that produced
  the claim, before judging it.

**Advanced branch**

- Compare how the same reading rule (name the sector, the measure, the population and the effect size)
  applies across the three condition-linked lessons - prefrontal cortex, insula, and default mode, salience
  and executive networks - and note what changes and what stays the same.
- Design the anatomical detail (region, method, population, effect size) you would want stated before
  accepting a popular "brain region does X" headline.

## Common confusions

- A lobe, sector or network name is treated as a function in itself, rather than as a coarse address or a
  statistical summary.
- A finding about one loop or subregion of a structure is generalised to the whole structure.
- An observational or resting-state association is read as proof that one thing caused another.
- A finding shared across many diagnoses is read as evidence specific to one of them.

## Module assessment

Answer from memory first; the parent lesson holds the supporting detail.

1. Why does naming "the amygdala" or "the basal ganglia" without a subregion or pathway usually fail to say
   anything testable?
   - *Working answer.* Both are hubs made of functionally distinct subregions, pathways and loops rather than
     single-purpose units, so a general claim does not say which part or circuit is involved.
   - *Answer sources.* [[F60 Neuroscience Online amygdala#^f60-connections|Amygdala]]; [[F56 Neuroscience Online basal ganglia#^f56-pathways|Basal ganglia]]

2. Why do the ADHD, bipolar and PTSD findings tied to the prefrontal cortex, insula and default mode network
   need to be read differently from each other, even though they involve some of the same regions?
   - *Working answer.* Their effect sizes, populations and even directions differ - small dimensional ADHD
     effects, mood-state-reversing bipolar effects, trauma-exposure-versus-PTSD-specific effects - and some
     findings are shared across many diagnoses rather than being specific to one.
   - *Answer sources.* [[P38476041 Norman 2024 Subcortico-cortical connectivity in ADHD#^p38476041-caution-effect|Appraisal: Norman 2024]]; [[P32259712 Wang 2020 Network dysfunction across bipolar mood states#^p32259712-caution-state|Appraisal: Wang 2020]]; [[P34688728 Bao 2021 Large-scale networks in adult PTSD#^p34688728-caution-controls|Appraisal: Bao 2021]]

3. Why is a diffusion-imaging or resting-state "connectivity" claim weaker evidence than a tracer-based
   connectivity claim in an animal?
   - *Working answer.* Diffusion and resting-state estimates are indirect and model-dependent, and inherit
     documented analytic-flexibility and power problems, while a tracer is transported directly along an axon
     and reveals a real anatomical connection.
   - *Answer sources.* [[P28053326 Poldrack 2017 Neuroimaging reproducibility#^p28053326-flexibility|Neuroimaging reproducibility]]; [[F101 Neuroscience Online synapse formation and elimination#^f101-elimination|Synapse Formation, Survival, and Elimination]]

## Source boundaries

Every lesson names the access level and reading rule for its sources. Several lessons draw on classic
mechanistic reviews built mainly from animal work, with translation to humans explicitly incomplete
([[Lesson - Amygdala]], [[Lesson - Hippocampal system]]). Three lessons carry the condition-linked imaging
cautions that recur across this vault's ADHD, bipolar and CPTSD domains - small effect sizes, mood-state
reversal, and findings shared across diagnoses rather than specific to one ([[Lesson - Prefrontal cortex]],
[[Lesson - Insula]], [[Lesson - Default mode, salience and executive networks]]). Human connectional and
network claims throughout the module are estimates that inherit the neuroimaging methods literature's
documented problems with analytic flexibility and statistical power, not measured wiring diagrams.
