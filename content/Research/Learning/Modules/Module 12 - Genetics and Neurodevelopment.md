# Module 12 - Genetics and Neurodevelopment

**Lessons.** 11 · **Entry point.** [[Learning Path]]

## What this module is for

How genetic variation is measured and interpreted, and how a genome becomes a nervous system.

This module covers two linked subjects. The first half - DNA through polygenic scores - builds the vocabulary for reading any genetic claim in this library: what heritability means, how common and rare variants are found and what each finding does and does not establish, and why a polygenic score built in one population does not automatically transfer to another. The second half - neurogenesis through sensitive periods - follows the developmental sequence that builds a nervous system from that genetic material, in overlapping, experience-shaped stages rather than a fixed schedule.

## Learning objectives

1. Explain how information flows from DNA to protein, and why regulation rather than sequence change is the main route from genotype to phenotype.
2. Distinguish Mendelian from complex, polygenic inheritance, and state precisely what a heritability estimate does and does not claim.
3. Explain how twin and family designs estimate heritability, with worked figures for bipolar disorder, ADHD, autism and PTSD, and why molecular estimates run lower than twin estimates.
4. Contrast common and rare genetic architectures and the study designs - association versus sequencing - built to detect each.
5. Read a genome-wide association or polygenic-score claim by separating association, fine-mapping, mechanism and prediction, and explain why scores lose accuracy across and within ancestry groups.
6. Trace the developmental sequence from neurogenesis through migration, axon guidance, synaptogenesis, pruning, myelination and sensitive periods, and explain why timing differs by system.

## Core and advanced branches

**Core sequence**

- Read the genetics lessons in order first (DNA through polygenic scores), then the development lessons in order (neurogenesis through sensitive periods); each half builds on the one before it.
- For every genetic claim, name the level it occupies - association, fine-mapping, mechanism or prediction - before judging it; for every developmental claim, name the process, region, species and whether it is mechanism or intervention.

**Advanced branch**

- Compare the heritability figures for bipolar disorder, ADHD, autism and PTSD side by side and explain why they are not directly comparable across designs.
- Design the ancestry-matching and validation steps you would want before treating a polygenic score as informative for one person, and justify each step.

## Common confusions

- A heritability estimate describes a population, not a person.
- A statistical association is not a mechanism, and a mechanism is not yet a prediction.
- Development runs as overlapping processes on different schedules, not one clean timeline.

## Module assessment

Answer from memory first; the parent lesson holds the supporting detail.

1. Why doesn't a high heritability estimate tell you how "fixed" a trait is for any one person?
   - *Working answer.* Heritability is a statistic describing how much of the variation among people in a population is associated with genetic differences; it says nothing about the causal story for any individual or about whether the trait would respond to a changed environment.
   - *Answer sources.* [[F07 OpenStax Human genetics#^f07-heritability|Human genetics]]; [[P26709141 Tick 2016 Heritability of autism twin meta-analysis#^p26709141-estimates|Tick 2016]]

2. What has to happen between a genome-wide association signal and a claim about mechanism?
   - *Working answer.* The broad signal has to be fine-mapped to a smaller credible set of candidate variants, and then those candidates need functional evidence showing how they act; association alone establishes neither the causal variant nor the mechanism.
   - *Answer sources.* [[P29844615 Schaid 2018 Fine-mapping#^p29844615-methods|Fine-mapping]]; [[F67 NHGRI GWAS fact sheet#^f67-meaning|GWAS fact sheet]]

3. Why does the same developmental disruption produce different outcomes at different ages?
   - *Working answer.* Development proceeds through overlapping processes with system-specific schedules - for cortical migration, an inside-out pattern means the earliest-migrating cells build the deepest layers - so what is being built, and therefore what a disruption damages, differs by developmental timing.
   - *Answer sources.* [[P21042938 Stiles 2010 Basics of brain development#^p21042938-processes|The basics of brain development]]; [[P19794405 Tau 2010 Normal development of brain circuits#^p19794405-sequence|Normal development of brain circuits]]

## Source boundaries

Every lesson names the access level and evidence limits of its sources. Heritability and polygenic-score lessons are explicit about population and ancestry scope; epigenetics and gene-environment lessons are explicit about which tissue and which design produced a given claim; developmental lessons are explicit about species and about which parts of the sequence are established in humans versus inferred from animal models.
