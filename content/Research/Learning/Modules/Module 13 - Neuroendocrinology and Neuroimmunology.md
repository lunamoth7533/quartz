# Module 13 - Neuroendocrinology and Neuroimmunology

**Lessons.** 11 · **Entry point.** [[Learning Path]]

## What this module is for

Neuroendocrinology and neuroimmunology covers the hormonal axes, circadian timing, and immune-brain signalling that connect peripheral physiology to brain function and to psychiatric symptoms.

The module keeps every claim tied to what was actually measured - a hormone in a feedback loop, a marker in a group comparison, a mechanism shown in an animal preparation - rather than letting a single number or a popular phrase stand in for a diagnosis. It opens with the HPA axis and its sibling hormonal loops, moves through circadian timing and sleep, and closes with the barrier, immune and microbial systems that route peripheral state into the brain.

## Learning objectives

1. Explain the HPA axis as a feedback loop with two receptor populations and more than one timescale, and why a single cortisol value cannot be read as "the stress level."
2. Distinguish exposure, appraisal, physiological mediators and cumulative allostatic load as four separately measured constructs.
3. Read thyroid, sex-hormone and autonomic findings for what population associations, trial evidence and measurement conditions actually establish.
4. Explain the circadian clock as a self-sustaining molecular oscillator, distinct from homeostatic sleep drive, and apply that distinction across four conditions.
5. Trace how the blood-brain barrier, microglia, circulating cytokines and gut microbiota connect peripheral state to brain function, and what each literature does and does not establish.

## Core and advanced branches

**Core sequence**

- Read the HPA axis first, since it supplies the loop-and-feedback logic the thyroid, sex-hormone and allostasis lessons all reuse.
- For every axis or marker claim, name what was actually measured, at what timescale, and what population it came from before judging it.

**Advanced branch**

- Compare the HPA, thyroid and sex-hormone axes side by side and list what evidence would be needed to move any of their mood associations from correlational to causal.
- Trace one claim - "inflammation," "leaky barrier," or "gut-brain imbalance" - from an animal mechanism through to a popular health claim, and mark exactly where the evidence runs out.

## Common confusions

- A single hormone or marker value is not a diagnosis; every axis in this module is read through feedback, rhythm and multiple timescales, not a single number.
- Group-level associations (thyroid and depression, cytokines and mood, gut microbiota and behaviour) are not individual tests or causal proof.
- Animal mechanism and human evidence are different rungs of the same ladder; a mechanism shown in mice does not by itself establish a human treatment claim.

## Module assessment

Answer from memory first; the parent lesson holds the supporting detail.

1. Why can't a single cortisol or thyroid-hormone value be read as a diagnosis of axis dysfunction?
   - *Working answer.* Both are hormones in feedback loops with daily rhythms and more than one receptor population or timescale of action; a static value cannot separate altered drive, altered feedback sensitivity or altered output, or distinguish rhythm and momentary state from true dysregulation.
   - *Answer sources.* [[P2998738 Reul 1985 Two receptor systems for corticosterone in rat brain#^p2998738-occupation|Two receptor systems for corticosterone in rat brain]]; [[P34524390 Bode 2021 Hypothyroidism and clinical depression#^p34524390-caution-direction|Appraisal: Bode 2021]]

2. What separates the "inflammation contributes to depression in a subset" claim this module supports from the "inflammation causes depression" claim it does not?
   - *Working answer.* The supported claim is narrower and marker-defined: elevated inflammatory markers are reproducible at the group level in some conditions, and experimental challenges can produce depressive-like changes in that identifiable subset; the broader claim would require establishing direction, specificity and applicability to depression in general, which the evidence does not do.
   - *Answer sources.* [[P26711676 Miller 2016 Inflammation in depression#^p26711676-subset|The role of inflammation in depression]]; [[P22749156 Munkholm 2013 Cytokines in bipolar disorder#^p22749156-caution|Cytokines in bipolar disorder]]

3. Why does a germ-free mouse study showing gut bacteria affect brain development not, by itself, support a human probiotic's mental-health claim?
   - *Working answer.* The mouse study can establish necessity or sufficiency for a specific microbe under tightly controlled conditions in that species; a human product's claim needs its own separate evidence that a candidate mediator changes, reaches the relevant compartment, and produces the claimed effect when manipulated in a properly controlled human trial - the animal and human literatures sit on different rungs of the same causal ladder.
   - *Answer sources.* [[P31460832 Cryan 2019 Microbiota-gut-brain axis#^p31460832-status|The Microbiota-Gut-Brain Axis]]
