# Sleep and circadian disruption across conditions

**Module.** M13 Neuroendocrinology and Neuroimmunology · **Topic.** [[Sleep and circadian disruption across conditions]]

## Why this matters

Sleep complaints turn up in bipolar disorder, ADHD, autism and PTSD alike, and it is tempting to treat that overlap as proof of one shared cause. This lesson resists that shortcut. It asks, for each condition, whether disturbed sleep looks like a cause of symptoms, a consequence of them, or a marker of some shared underlying vulnerability - and shows that the honest answer, most of the time, is that the evidence cannot yet say.

## The core model

"Disrupted sleep" is not one variable. Because circadian timing and homeostatic sleep pressure are separate systems, and because sleep itself cycles through distinct stages, disruption can mean altered timing, shorter duration, broken continuity, or changed internal structure - each measured with a different tool, from self-report to actigraphy to full polysomnography. [[F14 NINDS Brain basics understanding sleep#^f14-rhythm|Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep]]

In bipolar disorder, a recent review reports sleep changes in every mood state, not only during episodes, linked with relapse, suicide risk and impaired cognition; most people in a current episode recall sleep disturbance beforehand once asked specifically, with earlier onset before depression than before mania. In long wearable records spanning more than 40,000 person-days, modelled circadian phase disturbance preceded mood symptoms in bipolar I and major depression, but sleep-phase disturbance itself showed no such lead - a genuine time-order finding, though still a single design testing one plausible mechanism rather than a settled account. [[P39210713 Tonon 2024 Sleep and circadian disruption in bipolar disorders#^p39210713-allstates|Tonon 2024]]

In ADHD, the pattern is different: objective measures and complaints partly diverge. Actigraphy - a wrist-worn movement sensor - shows longer sleep latency and lower sleep efficiency in children and adolescents with ADHD, but full polysomnography does not show significant differences on the same dimensions, and medication, co-occurring conditions and how many nights were recorded all move these estimates. [[P37657199 Liang 2023 Objective sleep continuity in ADHD#^p37657199-actigraphy|Liang 2023]]

In autism, insomnia in particular is much more common than in the general population, and medication-naive autistic children show longer sleep latency, lower efficiency and shorter total sleep on objective measures, with moderate-sized effects - a more consistently objective picture than the ADHD literature shows. [[P37469173 Kim 2023 Sleep in medication-naive autistic youth#^p37469173-objective|Kim 2023]]

In PTSD, trauma-related nightmares were more common after interpersonal violence specifically, and predicted more severe PTSD symptoms a month later - a genuine prospective, time-ordered finding, though from a small study. [[P39376120 Reffi 2024 Trauma-related nightmares and PTSD symptoms#^p39376120-prospective|Reffi 2024]]

On the question of direction, the field's honest position is a reciprocal model: disturbed sleep can worsen or trigger symptoms, and symptoms can disturb sleep, in both directions at once rather than one clean arrow. Genetic designs offer a different kind of leverage on direction than observational timing does; one Mendelian randomization study linked a genetically later activity peak to both autism and ADHD, while a separate, nominal association ran the other way for ADHD - from the condition toward more fragmented sleep - though this is one analysis awaiting replication, not a settled direction for either condition. [[P38869021 Dai 2025 Mendelian randomization circadian traits autism ADHD#^p38869021-chronotype|Dai 2025]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A researcher observes that children with more severe ADHD symptoms also report worse sleep, and a summary states, "poor sleep causes ADHD symptoms to worsen."

Apply the reciprocal-model check. A cross-sectional correlation between symptom severity and sleep complaints is consistent with sleep problems worsening ADHD symptoms, with ADHD symptoms worsening sleep, with both influencing each other, or with a third factor affecting both - and this design cannot distinguish between them. Testing direction properly needs either a prospective design that times which change comes first, as the bipolar wearable study attempted, or a genetic design like Mendelian randomization that does not rely on observed timing at all.

The accurate statement: "ADHD symptom severity and sleep complaints are correlated in this sample; this cross-sectional design cannot establish which caused which, and this domain's own reciprocal model treats both directions as live possibilities pending a study built to test direction."

## Common confusions

- "Sleep problems across these four conditions share one cause." Each condition is studied with different emphases and different measures, and shared vulnerability is one candidate explanation among several, not an established finding.
- "A correlation between symptoms and sleep complaints shows direction." Only prospective time-ordered designs or genetic designs such as Mendelian randomization can speak to direction, and even those carry their own assumptions.
- "Actigraphy and polysomnography always agree." In ADHD specifically, actigraphy shows differences that polysomnography does not, which is itself informative about what each tool captures.

## What the sources do not establish

Descriptive evidence - that objective sleep differences exist in these conditions - is the strongest layer here; directional evidence is thinner and rests on single designs with their own known gaps, such as shared-rater bias, a single time-series study, one Mendelian randomization analysis, and a small prospective sample, so none of them yet settles cause, consequence or shared vulnerability for any one condition.

## Answer notes

1. Altered timing, shorter duration, broken continuity, and changed internal sleep architecture.
2. Actigraphy shows longer latency and lower efficiency in ADHD, while polysomnography does not show significant differences on the same measures - a reminder that different measurement tools can give different pictures of the same claimed disruption.
3. Cause, consequence, or shared vulnerability; only prospective time-ordered designs or genetic designs such as Mendelian randomization, rather than simple cross-sectional correlation, can offer leverage on which explanation fits.
