# Cytokines and inflammation in psychiatric conditions

**Module.** M13 Neuroendocrinology and Neuroimmunology · **Topic.** [[Cytokines and inflammation in psychiatric conditions]]

## Why this matters

"Inflammation causes depression" is a headline claim that has outrun the evidence behind it more than once. This lesson gives the more careful version the field itself has converged on: not that inflammation explains depression in general, but that inflammatory signalling may contribute to symptoms in a subset of people identifiable by markers - a narrower, better-supported claim that this lesson teaches how to tell apart from the broader one.

## The core model

Cytokines are immune signalling molecules that act on the central nervous system as well as on peripheral immune function. Prolonged inflammatory signalling is proposed to sustain depressive symptoms in vulnerable individuals, with inflammatory responses themselves framed as potentially adaptive in short bursts and costly when sustained - a distinction between the acute, useful version of the response and the chronic, potentially harmful one that echoes the allostasis framework from earlier in this module. [[P18073775 Dantzer 2008 Inflammation to sickness and depression#^p18073775-depression|From inflammation to sickness and depression]] [[P26711676 Miller 2016 Inflammation in depression#^p26711676-adaptive|The role of inflammation in depression]]

The field's current framing - inflammation as relevant to a subgroup identifiable by elevated markers, rather than to depression as a whole - is treated in this domain as an honesty move rather than a retreat. Early inflammation-in-depression claims failed when tested as claims about the general population; the narrower, subgroup-based framing that replaced them is better supported, in part because experimental medicine studies show that deliberately triggering an inflammatory challenge can produce depressive-like changes in that identifiable subset. [[P26711676 Miller 2016 Inflammation in depression#^p26711676-subset|The role of inflammation in depression]]

Group-level marker differences are, in fact, reproducible in some conditions. A systematic review pooling studies of cytokine concentrations in bipolar disorder across mood states reported elevated TNF-alpha and two related soluble receptor markers, and a 2025 update confirmed those elevations, narrowing them further: the elevated markers appeared during mania and depression but not during euthymia (the stable, symptom-free state) - a pattern that fits a marker tracking the current episode as well as it fits a marker driving the illness. [[P22749156 Munkholm 2013 Cytokines in bipolar disorder#^p22749156-finding|Cytokines in bipolar disorder]] [[P41082830 McIntyre 2025 TNF-alpha in bipolar disorder meta-analysis#^p41082830-state|McIntyre 2025]]

These reproducible group differences do not, on their own, establish either direction or an individual-level test. Circulating cytokine measures are affected by infection, sleep, medication, body composition and current illness state - all of which differ systematically between people with and without an episode - so a group difference in a peripheral marker is not automatically usable to predict or diagnose anything in one person, and the chain from marker to mechanism needs direction (cause or consequence), specificity (which signalling pathway) and timing (state or trait) established separately, none of which a pooled average supplies by itself. [[P22749156 Munkholm 2013 Cytokines in bipolar disorder#^p22749156-caution|Cytokines in bipolar disorder]]

Anti-inflammatory treatment strategies follow the same narrowing logic rather than skipping past it: they are discussed as experimental directions that require identifying and targeting the marker-defined subgroup, not as established general antidepressants to be tried broadly. [[P26711676 Miller 2016 Inflammation in depression#^p26711676-treatment|The role of inflammation in depression]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A wellness article states, "a new study proves inflammation causes depression, so anti-inflammatory supplements should help anyone who's depressed."

Separate the three layers the reading rule asks for: marker findings, mechanistic models, and treatment claims. A marker finding - cytokines differing between a patient group and controls - is empirical and can be real; a mechanistic model proposing inflammation contributes to symptoms in some people is a testable proposal, not a demonstrated general cause; and a treatment claim for "anyone who's depressed" skips past the subset framing entirely, since the field's own evidence supports investigating anti-inflammatory approaches only in marker-identified subgroups, not as a universal recommendation.

The accurate statement: "this study reports a group-level cytokine difference associated with depression in some participants; it does not establish that inflammation causes depression generally, and no source here supports anti-inflammatory supplements as a treatment for depression broadly."

## Common confusions

- "Inflammation causes depression." The current evidence supports a role for inflammatory signalling in a marker-identified subset, not a general causal claim about depression as a whole.
- "A group difference in cytokines is a diagnostic test." Circulating markers are affected by infection, sleep, medication and illness state, so a group average is not a usable individual-level test.
- "An elevated marker during an episode proves inflammation caused the episode." The same pattern - elevated in episodes, normal in remission - fits a state marker (a passenger) just as well as a causal driver.

## What the sources do not establish

Elevated group-level markers in bipolar disorder are reproducible across two independent meta-analyses, but individual-level prediction and causal direction are not established, and anti-inflammatory treatment remains an experimental direction requiring patient selection by marker, not a general practice supported by current evidence.
