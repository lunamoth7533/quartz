# Sex hormones and mood across the lifespan

**Module.** M13 Neuroendocrinology and Neuroimmunology · **Topic.** [[Sex hormones and mood across the lifespan]]

## Why this matters

Reproductive hormone transitions - the menstrual cycle, pregnancy and the postpartum period, perimenopause - are among the most consistent, best-documented windows of psychiatric symptom change in this library, and they are also a window into how easy it is to slide from "symptoms change around this transition" to "this hormone causes the symptoms" without the evidence to support the jump. This lesson keeps those two claims separate.

## The core model

Sex hormones sit under the same hypothalamic-pituitary architecture as the axes in the earlier lessons of this module: the pituitary releases FSH and LH under hypothalamic control, and the resulting gonadal hormones feed back on the hypothalamus and pituitary. In mood research the emphasis falls less on absolute hormone levels and more on transitions - points where hormonal state changes relatively quickly. [[F34 OpenStax Pituitary gland and hypothalamus#^f34-connection|Pituitary Gland and Hypothalamus]]

The menstrual cycle shows the most consistent pattern across conditions: a review found the premenstrual and menstrual phases to be the times of most reliable worsening, with strong evidence for increases in psychotic, manic and depressive symptoms, suicide attempts and drinking, while anxiety, general stress and binge eating tended to run high throughout the whole luteal phase rather than being sharply timed to it. Findings for panic disorder, PTSD symptoms and borderline personality disorder were less consistent. Because cycle phase in most of this research is estimated from reported dates rather than measured hormones, "premenstrual worsening" ties a symptom to a point in the calendar without identifying which hormonal change, if any, is actually responsible. [[P35267252 Handy 2022 Psychiatric symptoms across the menstrual cycle#^p35267252-phases|Handy 2022]]

The postpartum period carries the largest, best-quantified risk in this lesson. A meta-analysis covering 5,700 deliveries in women with bipolar disorder, previous postpartum psychosis, or both, found that roughly 35% of deliveries were followed by a postpartum relapse, with severe episodes in 17% of women with bipolar disorder and 29% of those with a prior postpartum psychosis. Relapse was reported in 66% of women who were medication-free during pregnancy against 23% of those on prophylactic medication - a striking contrast, but one drawn from observational groups that differ from each other in more than medication status alone, so it shows an association rather than the effect of a treatment decision, and it is not a basis for any individual's treatment choice. [[P26514657 Wesseloo 2016 Postpartum relapse in bipolar disorder#^p26514657-overall|Wesseloo 2016]]

An expert review of ADHD in women concluded that puberty, the menstrual cycle, pregnancy and perimenopause each tend to make ADHD symptoms and mood problems worse, proposed interactions between oestrogen, progesterone and dopamine pathways as a mechanism, and flagged that drug studies and treatments tailored to women are lacking. Much of this evidence is cross-sectional and self-reported rather than drawn from within-person studies with measured hormones, so the hormonal mechanism remains a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated cause. [[P40692967 Kooij 2025 Female ADHD and hormonal fluctuations#^p40692967-transitions|Kooij 2025]]

Across all three windows - cycle, postpartum, perimenopause - the pattern is the same: the research times symptoms to a change of hormonal state rather than measuring hormone concentrations in each person, which is a real and useful kind of evidence but a different, weaker kind than a study that measures the hormone directly and follows the same person through the change.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A magazine article states, "the postpartum relapse statistics prove that stopping medication during pregnancy causes bipolar relapse after birth."

Check what the comparison in the meta-analysis can and cannot show. The 66% versus 23% relapse contrast compares two observational groups - women who stopped medication in pregnancy and women who continued it - that likely differ in illness severity, personal history and clinical circumstances well beyond the medication decision itself; that is exactly the kind of comparison that shows association without showing the effect of the decision in isolation.

The accurate statement: "in this meta-analysis, postpartum relapse was more common among women who were medication-free during pregnancy than among those on prophylactic medication; because the groups were not randomly assigned, this comparison shows an association between two observational groups and is not a controlled test of medication's causal effect."

## Common confusions

- "Premenstrual symptom worsening proves a hormonal cause." Most studies time symptoms to a reported cycle phase rather than to a measured hormone change, so the specific hormonal driver is not identified.
- "The postpartum relapse-medication contrast shows what stopping medication does." It compares two different observational groups, not a randomised comparison, so it cannot isolate the effect of the decision alone.
- "The ADHD-hormone link in women is an established mechanism." It rests mainly on cross-sectional and self-reported evidence and is described by its own reviewers as a hypothesis awaiting within-person studies with measured hormones.

## What the sources do not establish

None of the three literatures measures hormone concentrations continuously in the same people across a transition, so which specific hormonal change drives any reported worsening remains unidentified; the postpartum relapse figures come from observational, not randomised, comparisons, and the ADHD hormonal-transition account is an expert narrative synthesis rather than a pooled quantitative one.
