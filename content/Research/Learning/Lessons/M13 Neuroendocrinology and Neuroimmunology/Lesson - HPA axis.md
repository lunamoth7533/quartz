# HPA axis

**Module.** M13 Neuroendocrinology and Neuroimmunology · **Topic.** [[HPA axis]]

## Why this matters

"Cortisol levels" is one of the most overused phrases in popular writing about stress, treated as if it were a single dial that goes up when someone is stressed and down when they are calm. This lesson opens the module by building the actual control loop behind that word - a loop with feedback, multiple receptor populations and more than one timescale - so a single cortisol number can be read for what it actually is rather than for what a headline says it is.

## The core model

The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis is a hormonal loop, not a one-way chain. Hypothalamic corticotropin-releasing hormone drives the pituitary to release adrenocorticotropic hormone, which in turn drives cortisol release from the adrenal cortex - and cortisol then feeds back on the hypothalamus and pituitary to restrain further release. That feedback is what stops a stress response rather than merely slowing it down. [[F34 OpenStax Pituitary gland and hypothalamus#^f34-feedback|Pituitary Gland and Hypothalamus]]

The adrenal cortex does not put out one signal. It produces mineralocorticoids, glucocorticoids and androgens from separate zones, so "adrenal output" is really a family of signals whose relative mix depends on what triggered the response; a statement about "the stress hormone" is really a statement about only the glucocorticoid arm of this one axis. [[F64 OpenStax The adrenal glands#^f64-cortex|The Adrenal Glands]]

Cortisol is read by two different receptor populations, and this is where the loop gets genuinely interesting. Detailed mapping work in animal brain tissue found a high-affinity receptor population concentrated in the hippocampus and a lower-affinity population spread more widely, including in the hypothalamus itself. Because of that difference in affinity, the high-affinity population is heavily occupied even at the daily low point of the axis, while the lower-affinity population only gets substantially engaged near the daily peak or during a stress response. The same circulating cortisol level therefore recruits different receptors at different times of day - a mechanism about receptor location and affinity, not a claim that the hormone has two different identities. [[P2998738 Reul 1985 Two receptor systems for corticosterone in rat brain#^p2998738-systems|Two receptor systems for corticosterone in rat brain]]

Cortisol also runs on two timescales at once: fast, membrane-associated effects within minutes, and slower, gene-level effects over hours. A review of the hormone's actions found this range too broad for one label, and proposed sorting its effects into permissive, suppressive, stimulatory and preparative categories depending on context - explicitly a set of working criteria built from varied findings, not a settled, closed taxonomy. [[P10696570 Sapolsky 2000 Glucocorticoids and stress#^p10696570-timing|Glucocorticoids and stress]]

Finally, the HPA axis is only one arm of the acute stress response. The sympathetic nervous system reacts within seconds, while the hormonal axis takes minutes to hours, so the two run on different clocks, handle different phases of a demand, and can be active or quiet somewhat independently of one another in the same person at the same time.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A fictional wellness coach tells a client, "your one morning cortisol test came back high, so your HPA axis is broken and needs fixing."

Check the claim against the loop. A single cortisol value is a snapshot of a pulsatile hormone with a daily rhythm, shaped by which receptor population is currently most engaged, sitting downstream of at least three possible sources of change: altered drive from the hypothalamus, altered feedback sensitivity, or altered adrenal output itself - and a static number cannot distinguish between them. One elevated morning reading is also consistent with the time it was drawn, recent waking, food, exercise or simple day-to-day variation.

The defensible statement is: "one cortisol measurement was higher than a reference range; this does not identify which part of the loop changed, or whether the axis is dysregulated at all, since a single sample cannot separate rhythm, momentary state and true dysregulation."

## Common confusions

- "Cortisol is the stress hormone, full stop." It is one arm of one axis, itself a family of hormones from different adrenal zones, with a rhythm and more than one timescale of action.
- "A single cortisol value shows whether the axis is working." A static number cannot distinguish altered drive, altered feedback, or altered adrenal output from one another.
- "The high- and low-affinity receptor systems mean cortisol is two different hormones." It is one hormone read by two receptor populations with different affinities, engaged to different degrees depending on the time of day.

## What the sources do not establish

The receptor mapping is animal work and shows where the two receptor systems sit and how they bind, not what a particular cortisol value means for a particular person; axis anatomy and feedback are well established, but the functional interpretation of cortisol in behaviour and psychiatric conditions is considerably less settled, and single measurements remain weak proxies for anything beyond that one moment.

## Answer notes

1. The hypothalamus (corticotropin-releasing hormone), the pituitary (adrenocorticotropic hormone) and the adrenal cortex (cortisol), with cortisol feeding back on the first two.
2. Because a high-affinity receptor population is already heavily occupied at low, resting cortisol levels, while a lower-affinity population only engages substantially near the daily peak or during a stress response.
3. Because a change measured in circulating cortisol could reflect altered hypothalamic drive, altered feedback sensitivity, or altered adrenal output, and a single static value cannot distinguish between those sources.
