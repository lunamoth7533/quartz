# Circadian clock biology

**Module.** M13 Neuroendocrinology and Neuroimmunology · **Topic.** [[Circadian clock biology]]

## Why this matters

Jet lag and shift-work exhaustion are not simply sleep deprivation, and the reason lies in the molecular clock this lesson introduces. Once the clock is understood as a genuine biological oscillator - not just a passive response to light - it becomes clear why realigning it takes days rather than minutes, and why "just get some sleep" does not fix a circadian problem.

## The core model

At the core of the clock is a transcriptional feedback loop: clock proteins build up inside cells and repress their own production, and that repression-and-rebuild cycle takes roughly 24 hours to complete on its own, generating a self-sustaining rhythm from gene expression rather than from any rhythmic input the cell receives. The period of this cycle is set by the kinetics of the feedback loop itself, and variants in the genes involved shift how long the cycle takes - the mechanistic basis for inherited differences in timing and for animal models with an altered period. Because the rhythm persists even without external cues and is only adjusted by them, this is what makes the clock a genuine internal oscillator rather than a passive echo of the light-dark cycle. [[P27990019 Takahashi 2017 Circadian clock architecture#^p27990019-loop|Circadian clock architecture]]

The clock's own intrinsic period runs slightly off 24 hours, so it needs daily correction to stay locked to the external day. Light reaching the brain's master clock, the suprachiasmatic nucleus, advances or delays the clock's phase each day to keep it aligned. Because that daily adjustment is limited in size, a large or repeated shift - crossing time zones, or a switched work schedule - cannot be absorbed in one step; it takes several cycles, and the period of internal-external mismatch during that stretch is the biological content of jet lag and of shift-work maladaptation, not simply a lack of hours slept. [[P27990019 Takahashi 2017 Circadian clock architecture#^p27990019-outputs|Circadian clock architecture]]

The clock is not confined to one brain structure. Most tissues in the body run their own local clocks, coordinated - but not commanded moment to moment - by the suprachiasmatic nucleus, so timing information can be measured in peripheral tissue as well as in the brain. That distributed architecture is exactly why peripheral clocks can fall out of step with the central one when light, feeding and activity cues conflict, which is the suspected mechanism behind the metabolic problems linked to shift work. [[P27990019 Takahashi 2017 Circadian clock architecture#^p27990019-tissue|Circadian clock architecture]]

Clock output reaches well beyond sleep timing: it influences metabolism, hormone release and immune function, which is why disrupted circadian timing has consequences that are not limited to feeling sleepy. Sleep itself results from this circadian system interacting with a separate homeostatic sleep drive that builds the longer a person has been awake; the two are dissociable systems, which is exactly why shift work and jet lag are not simply sleep deprivation by another name. [[P27990019 Takahashi 2017 Circadian clock architecture#^p27990019-outputs|Circadian clock architecture]] [[F14 NINDS Brain basics understanding sleep#^f14-rhythm|Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep]]

A reading rule follows directly: the molecular loop is established mainly in animal models, and its human application is about phase, amplitude and their association with outcomes rather than a direct read of someone's clock genes. The proposed link from disrupted circadian timing to mood specifically is a good example of a claim that outruns its support - a widely cited theory connecting rhythm disruption to mood episodes reports only limited direct evidence for its causal version, with an internal-pacemaker alternative still on the table. [[P27990019 Takahashi 2017 Circadian clock architecture#^p27990019-limit|Circadian clock architecture]] [[P16904251 Grandin 2006 Social zeitgeber theory#^p16904251-evaluation|The social zeitgeber theory]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A traveller crosses eight time zones and argues, "I slept eight hours on the plane, so I shouldn't have jet lag."

Separate the two systems. Sleep duration addresses homeostatic sleep drive - how long the traveller has been awake - but it does nothing to move the circadian clock's phase, which can only shift by a limited amount per day even with well-timed light exposure. Eight hours of sleep at the wrong circadian phase does not resolve the mismatch between the internal clock and the new local time; it addresses a different variable entirely.

The accurate statement: "eight hours of sleep reduces homeostatic sleep pressure but does not shift circadian phase by more than a small amount per day; several days of appropriately timed light exposure are what the clock actually needs to re-align, and until then, internal and external time genuinely disagree."

## Common confusions

- "Jet lag is just tiredness from lost sleep." It is a mismatch between internal clock phase and local time, which sleep duration alone does not fix.
- "The whole body runs on one central clock." Most tissues have their own local clocks coordinated by, but not identical to, the central one.
- "Disrupted circadian rhythm is a proven cause of mood episodes." The leading theory connecting the two has only limited direct support for its causal claim.

## What the sources do not establish

The molecular clock is established in model organisms and human tissue, but its practical effects on psychiatric outcomes rest on observational, mostly occupational literature with its own confounds, and the causal link from circadian disruption to mood specifically remains a proposal with limited direct evidence rather than a settled mechanism.

2. Why does correcting a large time-zone shift take several days rather than one?
3. Why are jet lag and shift-work fatigue not simply "sleep deprivation"?

## Answer notes

1. Its rhythm is generated by an internal transcriptional feedback loop that continues on roughly a 24-hour period even without external cues; light and behaviour only reset its phase, they do not generate the rhythm.
2. Because the clock can only be advanced or delayed by a limited amount each day, so a large shift has to be absorbed over several cycles rather than in one correction.
3. Because circadian phase and homeostatic sleep drive are separate, dissociable systems; sleeping enough hours addresses the homeostatic component but does not, by itself, realign the circadian clock to a new time zone or schedule.
