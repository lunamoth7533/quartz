# Thyroid axis and mood

**Module.** M13 Neuroendocrinology and Neuroimmunology · **Topic.** [[Thyroid axis and mood]]

## Why this matters

The thyroid axis is built on the same loop logic as the HPA axis from the first lesson - a releasing hormone, a pituitary hormone, and feedback from the target gland - which makes it a useful second example of that architecture. It also matters clinically for a specific reason in this vault: lithium, a mainstay bipolar medication, measurably changes thyroid function, and separating "the axis" from "what a drug does to the axis" from "whether thyroid hormone treats mood" is a discipline worth practising here.

## The core model

Like the HPA axis, the thyroid axis is a loop rather than a one-way chain: hypothalamic releasing hormones drive the pituitary to release thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), and thyroid hormone then feeds back on the hypothalamus and pituitary. Because of that feedback, a rise in TSH is the pituitary's response to falling thyroid output, which makes TSH the loop's most sensitive signal that something is pushing on it - not a direct measure of thyroid hormone itself. [[F34 OpenStax Pituitary gland and hypothalamus#^f34-feedback|Pituitary Gland and Hypothalamus]]

The association between thyroid dysfunction and depression is real, but considerably more modest than popular accounts suggest. A meta-analysis of 25 population studies covering 348,014 participants found hypothyroidism associated with clinical depression, and the association was clearly stronger for overt hypothyroidism than for subclinical cases, and present in women but not men in a post hoc analysis - while thyroid autoimmunity alone gave an inconclusive result. Crucially, this kind of study cannot show direction: fatigue and low mood overlap with hypothyroid symptoms, and people who are already depressed may simply be tested for thyroid problems more often. [[P34524390 Bode 2021 Hypothyroidism and clinical depression#^p34524390-association|Bode 2021]]

Lithium's relationship with the thyroid is one of the better-documented drug-endocrine interactions in this vault. A systematic review of 385 studies found clinical hypothyroidism substantially more common with lithium than with placebo, with TSH rising by around 4 units on average - alongside other changes including higher calcium and parathyroid hormone and reduced urinary concentrating ability. These are group averages drawn from mixed study designs, not a prediction of what will happen to any one person's thyroid, and how lithium stabilises mood in the first place is itself still unresolved, so its thyroid effects are best read as a well-documented side effect rather than as a clue to its therapeutic mechanism. [[P22265699 McKnight 2012 Lithium toxicity profile#^p22265699-thyroid|McKnight 2012]]

Thyroid hormone has also been studied as an add-on treatment for bipolar depression, motivated by a hypothesis linking rapid mood cycling to low thyroid hormone availability in the brain. Open-label studies reported better outcomes and fewer relapses in rapid cycling, but a randomised trial found no advantage over placebo overall, and benefit turned up only in a subgroup - women with rapid cycling - in a separate three-arm trial. The available studies were too different from one another to pool into a single estimate, and a review judged the overall evidence mixed and low quality: open-label promise did not consistently hold up once trials added a proper control group, which is exactly the pattern that expectation and natural improvement over time would also produce. [[P36421864 Seshadri 2022 Thyroid hormone augmentation in bipolar disorder#^p36421864-trials|Seshadri 2022]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A patient forum post claims, "my depression cleared up after I started thyroid medication, so undiagnosed thyroid problems must be behind a lot of depression that doctors miss."

Separate the loop, the association, and the treatment record. The reported improvement is a single anecdote, not a controlled comparison, and even the pooled evidence on hypothyroidism and depression - itself a real but modest association from observational data - cannot establish which way the arrow points for any one person. Whether thyroid hormone helps depression as an added treatment has been tested directly, and the trial record there is mixed and rated low quality, with the clearest randomised benefit confined to one subgroup rather than showing up broadly.

The accurate statement: "one person's mood improved after starting thyroid medication; population-level hypothyroidism-depression associations are real but modest and cannot establish causation for an individual, and controlled trials of thyroid hormone as an added treatment for depression have given a mixed, low-quality record rather than consistent benefit."

## Common confusions

- "Low thyroid function is a common hidden cause of depression." The association is real but modest, stronger for overt than subclinical dysfunction, and direction is not established.
- "Lithium's thyroid effects explain how it stabilises mood." They are a documented side effect; lithium's mood mechanism is separately unresolved.
- "Open-label improvement on thyroid hormone proves it works." Randomised, placebo-controlled evidence has been mixed, with a positive signal confined mostly to one subgroup.

## What the sources do not establish

The feedback architecture of the axis is textbook physiology, but whether a particular symptom in one person is caused by axis dysfunction cannot be read from population associations; the hypothyroidism-depression link's direction is unresolved, and evidence for thyroid hormone as an added bipolar-depression treatment is explicitly rated mixed and low quality rather than established.

2. Why can't the hypothyroidism-depression association studies establish which caused which?
3. Why does the open-label promise for thyroid hormone in bipolar depression not settle the question?

## Answer notes

1. Both are hypothalamic-pituitary loops: a releasing hormone drives a pituitary hormone, which drives a target-gland hormone that feeds back to restrain the loop.
2. Because the studies are observational: depression symptoms overlap with hypothyroid symptoms, and people with depression may be tested for thyroid problems more often, so the design cannot distinguish cause from effect or from a shared underlying factor.
3. Because open-label studies lack a control group, so improvement consistent with expectation or natural symptom change over time cannot be ruled out; the one randomised trial with a clear positive result found it only in a subgroup.
