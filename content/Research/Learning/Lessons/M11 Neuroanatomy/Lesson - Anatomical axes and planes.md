# Anatomical axes and planes

**Module.** M11 Neuroanatomy and Systems Neuroscience · **Topic.** [[Anatomical axes and planes]]

## Why this matters

Every other lesson in this module, and every clinical or research claim about where something sits in the
nervous system, depends on a shared set of directional words. Without that shared frame, "dorsal" or
"anterior" means something different depending on which structure is under discussion. Learning the
vocabulary first is what makes every later lesson readable.

## The core model

The nervous system is described with three paired axes - anterior/posterior, dorsal/ventral, and
medial/lateral - and cut in transverse, coronal or sagittal planes. These words give a consistent frame for
position and section, which is why the nervous system is taught divisions-first and sections-first rather
than structure-by-structure. [[F54 Neuroscience Online nervous system overview#^f54-divisions|Overview of the Nervous System]] [[F54 Neuroscience Online nervous system overview#^f54-sections|Overview of the Nervous System]]

The trap is that the human neuraxis bends between the spinal cord and the forebrain. In the cord and
brainstem, rostral points toward the nose and dorsal toward the back. Above the midbrain flexure, the
forebrain's axes rotate: dorsal to the cortex means toward the crown of the head, and rostral points forward.
That historical accident is why comparing a brainstem diagram to a hemispheric one without checking axes
produces genuine anatomical errors rather than typos. [[F54 Neuroscience Online nervous system overview#^f54-divisions|Overview of the Nervous System]] The same axes describe how the nervous system divides into
central and peripheral parts, and how the central nervous system is divided into named regions such as
cerebral cortex, thalamus, hypothalamus and the basal nuclei. [[F05 OpenStax The central nervous system#^f05-parts|The Central Nervous System]]

Planes also do real diagnostic work. A deficit that crosses the midline, or that respects a particular
blood-vessel territory, looks different depending on which axis is affected, which is why radiological and
surgical descriptions always state the plane and the level. The neurological exam is organised by functional
domains - mental status, cranial nerves, motor, sensory, coordination and reflexes - so a deficit is assigned
to a region or pathway, while a lesion seen on imaging is assigned to a plane and a level; those two
assignments have to agree before a location claim can be trusted. [[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-purpose|Overview of the Neurological Exam]] [[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-domains|Overview of the Neurological Exam]] Structural imaging itself reconstructs a signal from
hydrogen atoms in tissues of different densities, and its axial, coronal and sagittal views map onto the same
axes. [[F28 OpenStax Brain imaging Psychology 2e#^f28-mri|Brain Imaging]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

Imagine two readers looking at figures from an invented animal study. One shows a coronal section of the
spinal cord labelled "dorsal horn, superior part." The other shows a coronal section of the cerebral
hemispheres labelled "dorsal cortex, superior part." A student assumes "dorsal" means the same direction in
both and plots the two structures as if stacked vertically on one straight body diagram.

Applying the reading rule fixes the error: state the species, the plane and the orientation convention before
comparing locations. Below the midbrain flexure, "dorsal" points toward the back; above it, in the cerebral
hemispheres, "dorsal" points toward the top of the head. The two labels are not contradictory, but they are
not interchangeable either - each is correct within its own segment of the neuraxis, joined by the bend
rather than lying on one straight line.

## Common confusions

- "Dorsal always means toward the top of the head." Only above the midbrain flexure; in the cord and
  brainstem, dorsal means toward the back.
- "One imaging plane is enough to localise a lesion." A location claim needs the exam's functional finding
  and the image's plane-and-level finding to agree.
- "Anatomical terminology is fixed and universal." Convention varies across atlases and clinical traditions,
  especially around the brainstem and diencephalon.

## What the sources do not establish

These sources describe a naming convention, not a testable claim about how the nervous system works - the
axes are standardised terminology whose value is practical rather than empirical. They do not resolve every
terminology conflict between atlases.

## Answer notes

1. The human neuraxis bends at the midbrain flexure, so the axes rotate: below it, dorsal points toward the
   back; above it, dorsal points toward the top of the head.
2. The neurological exam's functional-domain assignment and the image's plane-and-level assignment.
3. Because a deficit's appearance depends on which axis is affected, and stating the plane and level is what
   lets different readers localise the same lesion consistently.
