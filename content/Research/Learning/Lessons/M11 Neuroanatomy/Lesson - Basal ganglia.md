# Basal ganglia

**Module.** M11 Neuroanatomy and Systems Neuroscience · **Topic.** [[Basal ganglia]]

## Why this matters

The basal ganglia give this module one of its cleanest examples of anatomy predicting a testable pattern -
naming the loop, the pathway and the output nucleus lets a claim be checked, the way naming the segment and
horn did for the spinal cord. They also offer a clean warning about mistaking an observational association
for a caused effect.

## The core model

The basal ganglia are forebrain nuclei - the striatum (caudate, putamen and nucleus accumbens), the globus
pallidus, the subthalamic nucleus, and the midbrain substantia nigra - that receive input mainly from cortex
and thalamus into the striatum, process it through pallidal and nigral nuclei, and return output to cortex
through the thalamus. Because the circuit loops back to cortex rather than driving muscles directly, it
modulates cortical output - the initiation, scaling and selection of movement - rather than generating
movement itself, which is why damage here changes how movement is selected rather than causing paralysis. [[F56 Neuroscience Online basal ganglia#^f56-structures|Basal ganglia]] [[F56 Neuroscience Online basal ganglia#^f56-pathways|Basal ganglia]]

The textbook circuit has two pathways with opposite effects on the same output. In the direct pathway,
striatal neurons inhibit the output nuclei, and the output nuclei inhibit the thalamus - two inhibitory links
in series produce net facilitation, releasing thalamocortical activity. In the indirect pathway, an extra
excitatory step through the subthalamic nucleus increases output-nucleus activity and suppresses the thalamus
instead. Dopamine shifts the balance between them. [[F56 Neuroscience Online basal ganglia#^f56-pathways|Basal ganglia]] The same circuit architecture repeats across different
functional loops - sensorimotor, associative and limbic - distinguished by which cortical areas feed in and
which thalamic nuclei receive the output, which is why basal ganglia dysfunction can produce cognitive and
motivational changes alongside movement signs.

The clearest evidence that this pathway account tracks something real is clinical: loss of dopamine neurons
in the substantia nigra is the characteristic pathology behind the resting tremor, rigidity and slowed
movement of Parkinson's disease, directly linking a specific cell loss to a specific circuit-level prediction
and a specific clinical pattern. [[F76 MedlinePlus Parkinson's disease#^f76-mechanism|Parkinson's Disease]] The reading rule that follows is to name the territory, the pathway and the
output nucleus rather than attributing a finding to "the basal ganglia" in general - and to remember that the
textbook two-pathway picture is a refined framework rather than a complete wiring diagram: real recordings
show patterned activity rather than one simple output rate, and additional routes and feedback loops
complicate the clean direct/indirect split.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

Suppose a hypothetical two-year observational study follows children with ADHD and reports that those who
took stimulant medication during the study showed lower caudate-to-frontoparietal-cortex coupling at the end,
closer to values typical of children without ADHD - and a summary concludes that "the medication normalised
the circuit." Because treatment was not randomly assigned, that causal reading goes beyond what the study can
show: children who received medication may have differed from those who did not in ways connected to symptom
severity, family decisions or access to care, and any of those differences could also relate to caudate-cortex
coupling on their own. [[P39505862 Kaminski 2024 Striatal connectivity and stimulant exposure#^p39505862-networks|Kaminski 2024]] The finding still supports something useful - that medication history has to be
accounted for when interpreting striatal connectivity results - but it does not, by itself, show the
medication caused the change. A randomised comparison, with medication assignment controlled rather than
observed, is what the causal version of the claim would require.

## Common confusions

- "The basal ganglia generate movement." The circuit modulates cortical output through a loop; it does not
  drive muscles directly.
- "The two-pathway model is the complete wiring diagram." It is a well-supported but simplified framework;
  real activity is more patterned and includes routes the classic diagram omits.
- "An observed association between treatment and a brain measure proves the treatment caused the change."
  Without random assignment, other differences between groups can produce the same pattern.

## What the sources do not establish

These sources do not establish how basal ganglia circuits produce non-motor conditions such as tic disorders
in mechanistic detail, and the observational connectivity finding used above does not establish that any
specific treatment causes a specific circuit change.

## Check yourself

1. Why does the direct pathway's two inhibitory links in series produce net facilitation?
2. Why is Parkinson's disease described as strong evidence for the basal ganglia pathway account?
3. Why can't the caudate-coupling finding in the worked example be read as proof that medication caused the
   circuit change?

## Answer notes

1. Because an inhibition of an inhibition releases the target from suppression - striatal inhibition of the
   output nuclei reduces how much those nuclei inhibit the thalamus, increasing thalamocortical activity.
2. Because a specific, well-documented cell loss predicts a specific circuit-level change and a specific,
   observable clinical pattern, tying anatomy to signs directly.
3. Because the study was observational rather than randomised, so factors other than the medication could
   explain why the medicated group's connectivity looked different.
