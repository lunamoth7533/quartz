# Cortical layers and columns

**Module.** M11 Neuroanatomy and Systems Neuroscience · **Topic.** [[Cortical layers and columns]]

## Why this matters

The previous lesson treated layer composition as something that "predicts connectivity" without explaining
why. This lesson supplies the mechanism: lamination is a wiring diagram, not decoration, and the columnar
motif built on top of it needs an area, a species and a method attached before it means anything specific.

## The core model

Cell types are distributed non-randomly across the six cortical layers, and the orderly arrangement inside
dendrites supports this compartmental structure. [[F102 Neuroscience Online cell types#^f102-compartments|Organization of Cell Types]] Lamination predicts where axons terminate and where they
originate: thalamic input arrives mainly in the middle layers, corticocortical connections concentrate in the
superficial and deep layers, and output neurons projecting to subcortical targets leave from the deep layers.
A region's layer profile therefore summarises its connectivity - heavy middle layers mean strong thalamic
drive, prominent deep layers mean strong subcortical output - so a stain revealing layers is really revealing
wiring, not just appearance. [[F05 OpenStax The central nervous system#^f05-parts|The Central Nervous System]]

This wiring pattern is built during development through inside-out migration, as neurons migrate past
earlier-arriving neurons to take their layer-specific positions. The receptors expressed at a given layer
change with age, so the same microcircuit can have different molecular properties at different points in
development - which is why developmental findings and adult circuit findings need to be stated as claims
about different ages rather than merged into one. [[P21042938 Stiles 2010 Basics of brain development#^p21042938-processes|The basics of brain development]]

Columns are the vertical counterpart to layers: cells stacked on top of each other tend to share input
sources and response preferences. The clearest demonstration comes from vision: signals reach cortex through
the lateral geniculate nucleus and arrive first in striate cortex, where neurons are described by properties
such as orientation and direction sensitivity - the findings that established an ordered functional map
arising from a layered circuit. [[F104 Neuroscience Online visual cortex#^f104-pathway|Visual Cortex]] [[F104 Neuroscience Online visual cortex#^f104-properties|Visual Processing: Cortical Pathways]]

But the columnar motif is a statistical regularity, not a fixed universal module, and its strength varies by
area. In primary sensory cortex, vertically stacked cells share response properties strongly enough that
columns behave like real functional units; outside primary areas the evidence for sharply bounded columns
weakens, and the column becomes a tendency rather than a module. That distinction matters because a "modular"
claim predicts sharp boundaries and reproducible maps that a "tendency" claim does not, and the method used
to map boundaries shapes what boundaries appear. The reading rule: require an area, a species and a method
before accepting a general statement that "cortex is organised in columns" - one of the places a genuine
textbook motif gets overextended into a theory it cannot support everywhere.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

Suppose a hypothetical popular-science claim states: "A new study shows the human brain is organised into
functional columns, so thought itself is built column by column." Which cortical area was studied - primary
sensory cortex, where columnar organisation is best supported, or association cortex, where it is weaker?
Which species - the strongest evidence comes from specific well-studied systems and does not automatically
transfer across species? Which method defined the boundaries - an anatomical stain, or a functional map that
could shift with the analysis choices?

If the study used response-property mapping in primary visual cortex, the defensible claim is narrow: that
area, in that species, shows vertically stacked cells with shared response properties. That does not license
"thought itself is column-based," which extends a tendency found reliably in one primary sensory area into a
universal architecture for cognition - exactly the overextension the reading rule warns against.

## Common confusions

- "Cortical columns are identical rigid modules everywhere." The regularity is strongest in primary sensory
  cortex and becomes a looser tendency elsewhere.
- "A stain that shows cortical layers is just showing texture." It is showing a wiring diagram: which layers
  receive input and which send output.
- "A developmental finding about a layer's receptors applies unchanged to the adult circuit." Receptor
  expression at a given layer changes with age.

## What the sources do not establish

These sources establish lamination and cell-type distribution histologically and describe columnar
organisation as well supported in some systems and weaker in others; they do not settle how far canonical
cortical microcircuits generalise across all cortical areas.

## Check yourself

1. Why does a region's layer profile "summarise its connectivity"?
2. Why should a developmental claim about a cortical layer be kept separate from an adult circuit claim about
   the same layer?
3. What three things should a reader ask for before accepting a general claim that "cortex is organised in
   columns"?

## Answer notes

1. Because specific layers have characteristic inputs and outputs, so knowing the layer profile tells you
   what the region mostly connects to.
2. Because the receptors expressed at a given layer change with age, so the same physical layer can have
   different molecular properties in a developing brain than in an adult one.
3. The area studied, the species, and the method used to define the column boundaries.
