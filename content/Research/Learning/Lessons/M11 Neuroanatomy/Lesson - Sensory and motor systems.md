# Sensory and motor systems

**Module.** M11 Neuroanatomy and Systems Neuroscience · **Topic.** [[Sensory and motor systems]]

## Why this matters

This lesson names the pattern earlier lessons have already used - crossing points, multi-stage relays, and
separate lateral and medial systems - as a general framework. Sensory and motor pathways are chains of
processing stages, not single relays, and the reading rule here (name the level and the modality or muscle
group) is the same discipline applied to every region so far.

## The core model

Each sensory modality travels through its own dedicated series of relay neurons with a characteristic
crossing point and thalamic relay before reaching cortex, and the parallelism is functional: fine touch and
pain are carried by different pathways because they need different speeds and different central processing.
Discriminative touch and proprioception travel a three-neuron chain - primary afferent, a second-order neuron
crossing the midline, and a thalamic neuron projecting to the postcentral gyrus - while pain and temperature
travel a separate channel with different receptors and dynamics. [[F52 OpenStax Central processing#^f52-pathway|Central Processing]]

Pain is where that parallelism pays off most visibly. Nociceptors feed a channel that crosses early, ascends
separately from discriminative touch, and is modulated at both spinal and higher levels before it reaches
cortex - built into the pathway's anatomy, which is why attention and context change what is felt, and why
pain intensity can come apart from injury severity. [[F111 Neuroscience Online pain principles#^f111-nociceptors|Pain Principles]] Because most pathways cross, a deficit on one side of the
body usually points to a lesion on the opposite side above the crossing point - the same logic the spinal
cord lesson used to explain the hemisection pattern.

Motor output runs the chain in reverse: descending commands originate in cortex and brain stem and converge
on lower motor neurons, and the exam distinguishes upper from lower motor neuron lesions by the pattern of
tone, reflex and wasting changes each produces. [[F53 OpenStax Motor responses#^f53-descending|Motor Responses]] [[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-domains|Overview of the Neurological Exam]] Descending systems also split by target: lateral
systems project to distal limb circuitry and control fine movement, while medial systems project to proximal
and axial circuitry and control posture - lateral-system damage costs skilled hand use, medial-system damage
costs balance and trunk control. [[F53 OpenStax Motor responses#^f53-descending|Motor Responses]]

Motor control as a whole is hierarchical and recursive: cortex and brainstem issue commands refined at the
spinal level by reflex circuits and sensory feedback, the basal ganglia modulate selection, and the
cerebellum calibrates. Because feedback runs through every level, a movement is continuously adjusted rather
than executed open-loop, which is why deafferentation produces deficits in accuracy and timing rather than
paralysis: the command still exists, but its calibration does not. A motor deficit can therefore arise at the
cortical, pyramidal, extrapyramidal, cerebellar or lower motor neuron level, which is why the exam interprets
motor findings jointly with coordination and reflex findings. The reading rule: specify the level and the
modality or muscle group; a claim that stops at "sensory" or "motor" cannot be tested.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

Suppose a hypothetical teaching case states only: "The patient had weakness, which proves damage to the motor
system." Weakness could reflect an upper motor neuron pattern - typically increased tone and brisk reflexes,
pointing toward cortex or the pyramidal tract - or a lower motor neuron pattern, typically wasting and reduced
reflexes, pointing toward the cord, root or peripheral nerve. It could involve mainly distal weakness,
suggesting the lateral descending system, or mainly proximal weakness, suggesting the medial system. Without
naming the pattern of tone, reflex and wasting change, and the muscle groups affected, "damage to the motor
system" could describe several different levels, each with a different explanation. The exercise is to list
the missing details a complete description would need before the vague phrase can become a specific,
checkable claim.

## Common confusions

- "Touch and pain travel the same pathway at different speeds." They are anatomically separate channels with
  different crossing points and different levels of modulation.
- "A motor deficit means 'the motor system' failed as a single unit." It can arise at several distinct
  levels, each with its own pattern.
- "Losing sensory feedback causes paralysis." It impairs accuracy and timing, because the motor command still
  exists; only its calibration is lost.

## What the sources do not establish

Pathway organisation here is established from anatomy and clinical correlation, but the simplified chain
described in this lesson omits the parallel and recurrent connections that carry much of the real
information, so a textbook diagram should be read as a teaching simplification.

## Check yourself

1. Why do fine touch and pain travel in anatomically separate pathways rather than one shared channel?
2. Why does deafferentation impair movement accuracy rather than causing paralysis?
3. What two things should a sensory or motor claim specify to be testable?

## Answer notes

1. Because they require different speeds and different kinds of central processing, and pain in particular is
   modulated at spinal and supraspinal levels in ways discriminative touch is not.
2. Because the descending motor command is unaffected; only the sensory feedback that calibrates the ongoing
   movement is lost, so the movement becomes inaccurate and poorly timed rather than absent.
3. The level (receptor, pathway, nucleus, cortical area or loop) and the specific modality or muscle group
   involved.
