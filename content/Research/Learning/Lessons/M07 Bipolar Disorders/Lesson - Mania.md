# Mania

**Module.** M07 Bipolar Disorders · **Topic.** [[Mania]]

## Why this matters

Mania is the episode that defines bipolar I disorder: one manic episode alone is enough for the diagnosis, and depression is never required. [[F16 NIMH Bipolar disorder#^f16-mania|NIMH]] Knowing what counts as mania, and what its treatment evidence covers, is the base the rest of this module builds on - hypomania, psychosis and phase-specific treatment all measure themselves against this episode.

## The core model

Mania needs two things together: an elevated or irritable mood, and a rise in activity or energy, lasting at least a week (or any length if hospital care is needed) and severely disrupting functioning. [[P29630220 Dailey 2023 Mania reference chapter#^p29630220-criteria|Dailey 2023]] Requiring the activity change alongside the mood change is a deliberate, relatively recent tightening of the definition. The everyday picture includes less need for sleep, rapid speech, racing thoughts, distractibility and more goal-directed activity, often with little insight that anything has changed. [[P29630220 Dailey 2023 Mania reference chapter#^p29630220-features|Dailey 2023]] Before any of this is called mania, a substance or medical illness producing the activation has to be ruled out. [[P29630220 Dailey 2023 Mania reference chapter#^p29630220-exclusion|Dailey 2023]]

Acute-episode treatment evidence is the strongest evidence in this condition, because a short trial with a symptom count is easy to run. A network meta-analysis of 72 trials found nine antipsychotics, lithium, two anticonvulsants and tamoxifen all beat placebo on response - but only four antipsychotics also beat placebo on dropouts. [[P34642461 Kishi 2022 Drug treatment for acute mania#^p34642461-efficacy|Kishi 2022]] [[P34642461 Kishi 2022 Drug treatment for acute mania#^p34642461-acceptability|Kishi 2022]] A drug can beat placebo on symptoms while still being one people are more likely to quit. These trials averaged about four weeks, so they answer only the acute question, with no reason to prefer one option for a given person. [[P34642461 Kishi 2022 Drug treatment for acute mania#^p34642461-limit|Kishi 2022]]

Mania also has a proposed biological signature: during the manic state, imaging and pharmacology studies link it to higher striatal D2/3 dopamine receptor availability and an overactive reward network. [[P28289283 Ashok 2017 Dopamine hypothesis of bipolar disorder#^p28289283-state|Ashok 2017]] This is a state-dependent finding, detected during mania rather than as a stable trait between episodes, and its own authors call it speculative rather than settled. [[P28289283 Ashok 2017 Dopamine hypothesis of bipolar disorder#^p28289283-speculative|Ashok 2017]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

A magazine article claims: "New trial proves antipsychotic X keeps mania away for a whole year." Before accepting that, ask what the trial measured. If it comes from the acute-mania evidence above, the study ran about four weeks and measured symptom response, not one-year relapse prevention - a different question needing a much longer design. The headline describes evidence the study never collected. A more honest version: "Drug X reduced manic symptoms more than placebo over about a month, and was harder for some patients to stay on than a better-tolerated comparator." That keeps outcome, timeframe and tolerability intact - exactly what "four-week trial" should prompt a careful reader to ask.

## Common confusions

- "Bipolar I needs both a manic and a depressive episode." One manic episode is sufficient; depression is common but not required. [[F16 NIMH Bipolar disorder#^f16-mania|NIMH]]
- "Elevated mood always means euphoria." Mania can present as irritability alongside the required rise in activity. [[P29630220 Dailey 2023 Mania reference chapter#^p29630220-features|Dailey 2023]]
- "The strongest mania evidence proves long-term benefit." It shows acute, roughly four-week benefit only. [[P34642461 Kishi 2022 Drug treatment for acute mania#^p34642461-limit|Kishi 2022]]
- "Raised dopamine receptors explain why mania happens." The finding is state-dependent, and its authors call it speculative, not an established cause. [[P28289283 Ashok 2017 Dopamine hypothesis of bipolar disorder#^p28289283-speculative|Ashok 2017]]

## What the sources do not establish

The acute-mania trials cannot show what happens after they end, whether response predicts long-term course, or which option suits a given person. The dopamine account is a correlation offered as a hypothesis to test further, not a demonstrated cause. Every group-level finding here describes trial averages, not a forecast for one person.

## Check yourself

1. Besides mood change, what else must be present for an episode to count as mania?
2. Why does "more responders than placebo" not automatically mean "well tolerated"?
3. Why is the raised-dopamine finding in mania called state-dependent?

## Answer notes

1. A rise in activity or energy alongside the mood change, sustained for the required duration, with marked impairment or hospitalisation.
2. Efficacy and acceptability are measured separately; only four of nine effective antipsychotics also beat placebo on dropouts, so some effective options were harder to tolerate.
3. The raised receptor availability and reward-network activity were observed during the episode itself, not between episodes, so the finding describes the state, not a stable trait.
