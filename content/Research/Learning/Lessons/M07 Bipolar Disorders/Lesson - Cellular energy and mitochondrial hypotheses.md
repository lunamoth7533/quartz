# Cellular energy and mitochondrial hypotheses

**Module.** M07 Bipolar Disorders · **Topic.** [[Cellular energy and mitochondrial hypotheses]]

## Why this matters

The cellular-energy hypothesis is built almost entirely from indirect evidence - peripheral blood cells, post-mortem tissue, suggestive genetic overlap - and none of it directly measures the one thing the hypothesis is actually about: energy metabolism in a living, working brain. Separating a measured difference from a brain inference from a causal claim is the skill this lesson teaches.

## The core model

Neurons are unusually dependent on mitochondrial function because restoring ion gradients after signalling consumes most of the brain's energy budget. Cerebral metabolism is coupled to transport across a selective barrier rather than bulk flow: gases diffuse freely, but ions, nutrients and larger molecules depend on specific transporters. [[F105 Neuroscience Online blood-brain barrier#^f105-transport|Blood Brain Barrier]] [[F105 Neuroscience Online blood-brain barrier#^f105-tight|Blood-brain barrier]] That selectivity is exactly why a measurement taken in blood is a long way from the compartment the hypothesis is actually about. [[F105 Neuroscience Online blood-brain barrier#^f105-limit|Blood-brain barrier]]

The claim itself - that altered mitochondrial function or energy metabolism contributes to mood episodes - draws on indirect sources: altered enzyme activity or gene expression in peripheral samples, post-mortem tissue work, and suggestive genetic overlaps. Each measures something other than mitochondrial function in a living brain. [[P20877280 Nestler 2010 Animal models#^p20877280-construct|Nestler 2010]] The model literature judges animal and cellular models chiefly by construct validity - whether their biology actually matches the human mechanism - and calls it the hardest and most important criterion to meet. [[P20877280 Nestler 2010 Animal models#^p20877280-validity|Nestler 2010]] [[P20877280 Nestler 2010 Animal models#^p20877280-limit|Nestler 2010]]

Lithium keeps the question open without answering it. It acts on named cellular nodes such as GSK3, CREB and Na+/K+ ATPase, generally enhancing basal and inhibiting stimulated activity, and these effects remain incompletely understood - one reason interest in cellular mechanisms persists. [[P25687772 Alda 2015 Lithium pharmacology and pharmacogenetics#^p25687772-signalling|Alda 2015]] The mechanism that actually prevents recurrences is only partially understood: competing hypotheses have not been conclusively supported or rejected, research is limited by the absence of suitable animal models, and pharmacogenetic findings are limited by small samples. [[P25687772 Alda 2015 Lithium pharmacology and pharmacogenetics#^p25687772-unknown|Alda 2015]] [[P25687772 Alda 2015 Lithium pharmacology and pharmacogenetics#^p25687772-pharmacogenetics|Alda 2015]] A drug that acts on cellular signalling and works clinically is a reason to keep studying that pathway, not evidence that the pathway explains the disorder.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

A hypothetical science-news headline reads: "Blood test finds 'brain energy defect' in bipolar patients, explaining the disorder." Separate this into three steps. First, a measured difference in a peripheral blood sample - potentially real on its own terms. Second, an inference that mitochondrial function in the brain itself differs - this requires assuming a blood measurement reflects brain biology, despite the blood-brain barrier's selective transport standing in the way. Third, a causal role in mood episodes - not established by either of the first two steps. The headline collapses all three into one settled fact, when each step needs its own separate evidence.

## Common confusions

- "A blood finding tells you directly about brain mitochondria." The blood-brain barrier's selective transport is exactly why peripheral measurements sit far from the relevant compartment. [[F105 Neuroscience Online blood-brain barrier#^f105-limit|Blood-brain barrier]]
- "Lithium's cellular effects prove the energy hypothesis is correct." A drug acting on cellular signalling and working clinically shows the pathway is druggable, not that a defect there caused the illness. [[P25687772 Alda 2015 Lithium pharmacology and pharmacogenetics#^p25687772-unknown|Alda 2015]]
- "Animal models have already validated this pathway." Research is limited by the absence of suitable animal models, and construct validity is often the unmet criterion. [[P20877280 Nestler 2010 Animal models#^p20877280-limit|Nestler 2010]]
- "A finding shared across many conditions confirms this mechanism is specifically about bipolar disorder." A finding common across conditions is weak evidence of any one specific mechanism. [[P20877280 Nestler 2010 Animal models#^p20877280-construct|Nestler 2010]]

## What the sources do not establish

Whether any peripheral or post-mortem marker of mitochondrial function correlates with, causes, or is a consequence of bipolar illness course or its treatment is not settled here. Whether a suitably construct-valid animal model for this hypothesis yet exists is also unresolved.

## Check yourself

1. Why does the blood-brain barrier matter specifically when evaluating this hypothesis's evidence?
2. What is "construct validity" in an animal or cellular model, and why is it called the hardest, most important criterion?
3. Why doesn't lithium's action on cellular signalling pathways count as evidence that a cellular-energy defect causes bipolar disorder?

## Answer notes

1. The barrier is selective - gases diffuse freely, but ions, nutrients and larger molecules depend on transporters - so a peripheral measurement sits a long way from the living brain the hypothesis is actually about.
2. Whether a model's biology actually matches the human mechanism it represents, rather than just producing a superficially similar measurement; it is hardest because a model can look convincing while still testing the wrong biological process.
3. A drug that acts at certain cellular nodes and also works clinically shows the pathway is relevant to treatment response, not that a defect there caused the illness - the mechanism behind lithium's own recurrence-prevention effect is itself only partially understood.
