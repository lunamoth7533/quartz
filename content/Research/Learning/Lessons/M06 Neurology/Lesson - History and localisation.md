# History and localisation

**Module.** M06 Neurology · **Topic.** [[History and localisation]]

## Why this matters

A claim like "this points to the brainstem" or "that finding localises to the left hemisphere" is only as
good as the reasoning behind it. Localisation is the step that turns a vague complaint into a specific,
checkable claim about where in the nervous system something has gone wrong, and every other lesson in this
module leans on it.

## The core model

**What the exam is for.** A neurological exam identifies where function has been lost, so a complaint can be
attributed to a region or pathway rather than left as a symptom name. History comes first because it
generates the hypotheses the exam then tests.
[[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-purpose|Overview of the Neurological Exam]]

**Timing narrows the mechanism.** Sudden, progressive or fluctuating onset points toward different causes -
vascular, inflammatory, degenerative or structural problems each unfold on a different timescale, so a
careful timeline does diagnostic work before any instrument is used.
[[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-causes|Overview of the Neurological Exam]]

**Each part of the exam samples one functional system.** Mental status, cranial nerves, motor function,
sensation, coordination and reflexes are tested separately because each corresponds to a distinct functional
division; a finding becomes localising only once tied to the domain that produced it.
[[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-domains|Overview of the Neurological Exam]]

**Anatomy explains why a pattern points to a place.** The spinal cord is organised into segments with
ascending and descending pathways in predictable positions, so the level and pattern of a deficit carry
information about where a lesion sits.
[[F59 Neuroscience Online spinal cord#^f59-tracts|Anatomy of the Spinal Cord]] Because many pathways cross
the midline, a deficit on one side of the body usually points to a problem on the opposite side, above the
crossing point. [[F52 OpenStax Central processing#^f52-pathway|Central Processing]]

**A result is a clue, not a conclusion.** Localisation is a probabilistic inference from a pattern, so a
finding is read against the hypothesis the history and exam already built, not taken as a stand-alone
verdict. [[F59 Neuroscience Online spinal cord#^f59-limit|Spinal Cord]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This case is invented to practise the reasoning. Two people are seen the same week: one has sudden weakness
of the right arm and face plus slurred speech, all within minutes; the other has gradually worsening
clumsiness of the left hand over eight months, with nothing else.

Localise first: the sudden case affects adjacent regions on one side, a pattern fitting a single area rather
than several nerves, and because the deficit is on the right, the responsible area is likely on the left,
above the crossing point. The gradual case, confined to one hand, points toward a slower process than an
event.

Add tempo: minutes signals an interrupted process; eight months is far too slow for that and suggests a
structural or degenerative process instead. Only after location and speed are set does it make sense to ask
which investigation would confirm or exclude the leading idea, because a test answers a question the history
and exam have already framed.

## Common confusions

- "The scan will show where the problem is, so history barely matters." History and examination generate the
  hypothesis a scan then tests; without them, an ambiguous scan is uninterpretable.
  [[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-purpose|Overview of the Neurological Exam]]
- "One deficit always means one exact spot." Fibres of passage, compensation and individual variation make
  localisation probabilistic rather than a precise address.
  [[F59 Neuroscience Online spinal cord#^f59-limit|Spinal Cord]]
- "A left-sided brain problem should cause left-sided symptoms." Many pathways cross, so a left-hemisphere
  lesion more often produces a right-sided deficit.
  [[F52 OpenStax Central processing#^f52-pathway|Central Processing]]
- "The textbook exam sequence is a fixed protocol." It is a teaching frame; reasoning adapts the sequence to
  the hypothesis being tested.

## What the sources do not establish

These sources describe localisation as a general method illustrated with textbook anatomy; they give no
sensitivity or specificity figures for any sign, and do not say how often atypical presentations occur. A
small lesion in a critical tract can produce effects out of proportion to its size, a known limit on pattern
alone. [[F59 Neuroscience Online spinal cord#^f59-limit|Spinal Cord]]

## Check yourself

1. Why does history usually come before the neurological exam, and the exam before imaging?
2. Why can a lesion on the left side of the brain produce a deficit on the right side of the body?
3. Besides a single finding, what two things does a localisation argument need?

## Answer notes

1. The history generates the hypotheses that the exam and later investigation go on to test, so imaging
   answers a question already framed. [[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-purpose|Overview of the Neurological Exam]]
2. Many pathways cross the midline, so damage above the crossing point on one side shows up as a deficit on
   the opposite side of the body. [[F52 OpenStax Central processing#^f52-pathway|Central Processing]]
3. The pattern and level of the deficit, read against the time course the history supplies, since anatomy
   alone cannot say when a process started. [[F59 Neuroscience Online spinal cord#^f59-tracts|Anatomy of the Spinal Cord]]
