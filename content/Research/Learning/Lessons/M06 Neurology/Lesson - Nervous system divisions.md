# Nervous system divisions

**Module.** M06 Neurology · **Topic.** [[Nervous system divisions]]

## Why this matters

Every later lesson in this module uses the map: central and peripheral, sensory and motor, autonomic and
somatic. The map is a teaching aid, and the most useful thing you can learn about it is where it stops being
useful.

## The core model

The nervous system is divided anatomically into central and peripheral parts and functionally into sensory
input, integration and motor output.
[[F01 OpenStax Nervous system structure and function#^f01-divisions|Basic Structure and Function of the Nervous System]]

The central division is the brain and spinal cord; the peripheral division carries signals to and from the rest
of the body.
[[F01 OpenStax Nervous system structure and function#^f01-divisions|Basic Structure and Function of the Nervous System]]

Autonomic function splits into sympathetic and parasympathetic branches that often oppose each other while
cooperating.
[[F01 OpenStax Nervous system structure and function#^f01-autonomic|Basic Structure and Function of the Nervous System]]

Where the map breaks down is anywhere a process crosses categories. A stress response starts with appraisal in
the central nervous system, recruits the sympathetic branch and the HPA axis, changes peripheral physiology and
feeds back into the brain. Pain involves sensory input, central modulation and autonomic responses. Movement
involves central plans, peripheral nerves, muscle and sensory feedback.

Two more terms complete the map. The somatic division carries voluntary and reflex signals to skeletal muscle,
while the autonomic division governs smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands; afferent describes traffic toward
the central nervous system and efferent describes traffic away from it. Clinical reasoning then asks which part
of this map is failing, which is why the examination lesson follows this one.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. Take a startle response to an unexpected loud noise: is it central or
peripheral?

Trace it. The sound is transduced in the periphery and carried to brainstem nuclei, which are central. Those
nuclei trigger motor output through peripheral nerves to muscles and autonomic output through sympathetic
pathways, and the response is modulated by central appraisal of the context. A single event has already used
every division.

The accurate answer: "the divisions describe components, not separate systems; the startle response is a circuit
that crosses all of them." That habit — asking which components participate — is what keeps anatomy useful.

## Common confusions

- "Central means voluntary and peripheral means automatic." The autonomic nervous system is peripheral and
  involuntary; the central nervous system also handles involuntary control.
- "Divisions describe separate machines." They describe anatomical and functional groupings inside one
  integrated system.
- "Sympathetic and parasympathetic are opposites." They often oppose each other on the same targets while
  cooperating in regulation.

## What the sources do not establish

The educational source is a general-audience overview and simplifies both anatomy and autonomic pharmacology.
It does not cover clinical examination technique or specific disorders, which the later lessons in this module
and the condition modules take up.

## Check yourself

1. Name the two anatomical divisions and the three functional flows.
2. Which branches make up the autonomic division?
3. Give an example of a process that crosses divisions and explain why.

## Answer notes

1. Central and peripheral; sensory input, integration and motor output.
2. Sympathetic and parasympathetic branches.
3. A stress response (or startle, pain or movement) because it uses central processing, peripheral nerves and
   autonomic output together.
