# Seizures and epilepsy

**Module.** M06 Neurology · **Topic.** [[Seizures and epilepsy]]

## Why this matters

"Seizure" and "epilepsy" get used interchangeably, but they answer different questions: one describes a
single event, the other describes an ongoing predisposition. Keeping that distinction straight is what
allows a reader to judge whether a claim about someone's risk, treatment or prognosis is actually supported.

## The core model

**An event, a predisposition and a syndrome are different levels.** A seizure is an episode of abnormal
excessive brain activity; epilepsy is a disease of the brain defined by an enduring predisposition to
generate seizures. The 2014 practical definition operationalises this through three routes: at least two
unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart, one unprovoked seizure with a recurrence risk over the next ten
years similar to that two-seizure risk, or a diagnosed epilepsy syndrome. A single seizure with low
recurrence risk is therefore not the same clinical problem as epilepsy.
[[F73 MedlinePlus Epilepsy#^f73-definition|Epilepsy]]
[[F130 Fisher 2014 ILAE epilepsy definition#^f130-criteria|Fisher 2014 ILAE]]

**Causes are heterogeneous, which is why assessment looks for one.** Head injury, stroke, infection, genetic
factors and metabolic disturbance can all cause seizures, and the same seizure type can arise from more than
one of them, which is precisely why evaluation searches for a cause rather than assuming one from the
seizure's appearance alone. [[F73 MedlinePlus Epilepsy#^f73-triggers|Epilepsy]]

**The mechanism is a shift in the excitation-inhibition balance.** Seizures reflect a disruption of the
balance between excitatory and inhibitory signalling in a brain region, which is why many antiseizure
medicines work by enhancing inhibition or reducing excitation. This is one of the clearest places where
circuit-level balance language earns its keep, rather than functioning as a vague metaphor.
[[F106 Neuroscience Online amino acid transmitters#^f106-inhibitory|Amino acid transmitters]]

**Diagnosis and treatment are staged by syndrome, not by counting events.** First-seizure assessment involves
specialist referral, history, examination, and investigations that include EEG and imaging where indicated,
and treatment guidance is organised by seizure type and syndrome rather than by a single response to every
event. [[F95 NICE Epilepsies NG217#^f95-diagnosis|Epilepsies NG217]]
[[F95 NICE Epilepsies NG217#^f95-treatment|Epilepsies NG217]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. Someone has one convulsive episode after a night with almost no
sleep and heavy alcohol use, with a normal examination afterward, and no other episodes. Apply the definition
rather than assuming a diagnosis. A single event is not automatically epilepsy: the practical definition
requires either a second unprovoked seizure, a single seizure with a specifically elevated recurrence risk,
or a syndrome diagnosis. Here, the seizure occurred with plausible provoking factors, sleep deprivation and
alcohol, so the reasoning has to ask whether this was a provoked, one-off event or the first sign of an
enduring predisposition, which is exactly the kind of question the staged diagnostic pathway, rather than the
event itself, is designed to answer.

## Common confusions

- "One seizure means epilepsy." The practical definition requires more than a single unprovoked event,
  unless recurrence risk is specifically elevated or a syndrome is identified.
  [[F130 Fisher 2014 ILAE epilepsy definition#^f130-criteria|ILAE practical definition]]
- "A normal EEG rules out epilepsy." Epileptiform activity is often absent between events, so diagnosis rests
  on the clinical picture plus targeted investigation. [[F95 NICE Epilepsies NG217#^f95-diagnosis|Epilepsies NG217]]
- "All seizures share one cause." Causes range from injury to infection to genetics to metabolic disturbance,
  and the same seizure type can come from different causes. [[F73 MedlinePlus Epilepsy#^f73-triggers|Epilepsy]]

## What the sources do not establish

These sources describe classification, mechanism and the shape of the diagnostic and treatment pathway; they
do not give individual recurrence-risk percentages for a specific presentation, and this note carries no
dosing or treatment-choice detail for any person.

## Answer notes

1. Two unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart, one unprovoked seizure with a sufficiently high
   ten-year recurrence risk, or a diagnosed epilepsy syndrome.
   [[F130 Fisher 2014 ILAE epilepsy definition#^f130-criteria|Fisher 2014 ILAE]]
2. Because seizures reflect a disruption of the balance between excitatory and inhibitory signalling, so
   restoring that balance is the shared mechanism behind several drug classes.
   [[F106 Neuroscience Online amino acid transmitters#^f106-inhibitory|Amino acid transmitters]]
3. Because the same event pattern can arise from different syndromes that carry different treatment
   implications, so the syndrome, not just the event, drives the plan.
   [[F95 NICE Epilepsies NG217#^f95-treatment|Epilepsies NG217]]
