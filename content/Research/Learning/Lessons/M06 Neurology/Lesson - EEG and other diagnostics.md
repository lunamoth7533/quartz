# EEG and other diagnostics

**Module.** M06 Neurology · **Topic.** [[EEG and other diagnostics]]

## Why this matters

Ordering a test without a question in mind produces findings that are hard to interpret and easy to
over-read. Knowing what each neurological diagnostic actually measures, and what it cannot measure, is what
lets a reader tell a targeted, informative test apart from one collected for completeness.

## The core model

**Every diagnostic answers a named question.** History and examination, imaging, electroencephalography
(EEG) and laboratory tests each measure something specific: EEG asks what the brain's summed electrical
rhythm is doing and when; imaging asks about structure; laboratory studies ask about inflammation or
infection. The reasoning is question-first, choosing the test whose answer can change the working
differential rather than collecting tests and reasoning backwards from whatever turns up.
[[F15 NINDS Neurological diagnostic tests and procedures#^f15-modalities|Neurological Diagnostic Tests and Procedures]]

**EEG trades spatial precision for speed.** Scalp electrodes record summed electrical activity with high
temporal resolution, accurate to within milliseconds, but limited spatial precision, which is why EEG is the
tool of choice for catching epileptiform activity and for staging sleep rather than for locating a lesion
precisely. [[F28 OpenStax Brain imaging Psychology 2e#^f28-eeg|Brain imaging]]

**"MRI" is a family, not one measurement.** Conventional structural MRI images anatomy, while functional MRI
follows a slower, blood-flow-related signal with more spatial detail than EEG but on a much slower timescale.
Because these methods measure different quantities, disagreement between them is not automatically an error,
and agreement does not turn any one of them into a diagnostic classifier by itself.
[[F28 OpenStax Brain imaging Psychology 2e#^f28-fmri|Brain Imaging]]

**Peripheral testing separates nerve from muscle.** Peripheral nerve disorders are a large group, with over a
hundred recognised types and causes spanning diabetes, infection, nutritional deficiency, toxins, trauma and
inherited conditions, which is why nerve conduction and related studies are used to characterise a pattern
rather than to name a disease outright.
[[F78 MedlinePlus Peripheral nerve disorders#^f78-list|Peripheral Nerve Disorders]]
[[F78 MedlinePlus Peripheral nerve disorders#^f78-causes|Peripheral Nerve Disorders]]

**A normal result is still a probability update.** A normal EEG between events does not exclude epilepsy,
which is why national guidance structures epilepsy diagnosis around history plus targeted investigation
rather than a test-first pathway; interpreting a negative test needs the same pre-test-probability reasoning
as a positive one. [[F95 NICE Epilepsies NG217#^f95-diagnosis|Epilepsies NG217]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. Someone had brief episodes of jerking that stopped a year ago, and a
clinician orders an EEG today, which comes back normal. Read the result correctly: a single normal EEG
recorded between events does not confirm that the earlier episodes were not seizures, because the test
measures ongoing electrical activity at the moment of recording, and epileptiform activity between events is
often absent even in confirmed epilepsy. The right next question is not "does the normal result rule this
out?" but "what pre-test probability did the history already establish, and does this result move it much
either way?" If the history strongly suggested seizures, a normal EEG lowers but does not eliminate that
probability, and a longer recording or a different test aimed at the same question may be needed, rather than
treating one normal scan as the final answer.

## Common confusions

- "A normal test means nothing is wrong." A normal EEG between episodes does not exclude epilepsy, and a
  normal scan does not exclude a seizure tendency. [[F95 NICE Epilepsies NG217#^f95-diagnosis|Epilepsies NG217]]
- "MRI is one test." Structural and functional MRI measure different things on different timescales, under
  the same acronym. [[F28 OpenStax Brain imaging Psychology 2e#^f28-fmri|Brain Imaging]]
- "More detailed imaging always beats EEG." For a timing question, EEG's millisecond resolution is what is
  needed, not anatomical detail. [[F28 OpenStax Brain imaging Psychology 2e#^f28-eeg|Brain imaging]]

## What the sources do not establish

These sources describe what each modality measures in general terms; they do not give sensitivity or
specificity figures for particular presentations, and the peripheral nerve overview does not rank causes by
frequency, so it supports pattern description rather than a specific workup order.
[[F78 MedlinePlus Peripheral nerve disorders#^f78-list|Peripheral Nerve Disorders]]
