# Neurological examination

**Module.** M06 Neurology · **Topic.** [[Neurological examination]]

## Why this matters

Imaging is dramatic and examination is quiet, which inverts their actual roles. The examination generates the
question that technology answers, and it is where the distinction between a normal finding and a meaningful one
gets made. This lesson is the clinical-reasoning centre of the module.

## The core model

The NINDS overview places history and examination alongside imaging, EEG and laboratory testing in the
diagnostic process.
[[F15 NINDS Neurological diagnostic tests and procedures#^f15-modalities|Neurological Diagnostic Tests and Procedures]]

Read that ordering as a workflow rather than a menu. History and examination establish where the problem is,
which systems are involved, and how quickly it is evolving. That shapes which test is chosen, how its result is
interpreted, and what would change the plan.

Findings are interpreted against norms and context; a normal examination does not exclude every neurological or
psychiatric condition.
[[F15 NINDS Neurological diagnostic tests and procedures#^f15-caution-modality|AI synthesis: Neurological Diagnostic Tests and Procedures]]

Two sources of variability sit in the examination itself: the examiner's technique and the person's
cooperation, attention and state. That does not make the examination unreliable; it makes it evidence of a
specific kind, and it is why repeat examinations and corroborating history matter.

One reading rule follows: a normal examination is a negative finding under specific conditions, not a
certificate. Its value depends on technique, cooperation and the timing of the problem, and it is interpreted
against the history that prompted it — which is why the overview treats history and examination together rather
than as alternatives.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A person reports intermittent weakness in one hand over three weeks. The
examination is normal today. Should imaging be ordered immediately?

Reason from the examination's role. A normal examination on a day without symptoms is itself a datum: it tells
you that current function is intact. What it does not do is exclude intermittent pathology. The next step
depends on what the history suggests — the distribution, timing, triggers, associated symptoms — and on the
exam findings that can be reproduced or provoked. That logic determines whether imaging, nerve conduction
studies or observation with follow-up is the right test.

The teaching point: the test follows the question. "Order an MRI just in case" is not a question, and the
result it produces will be as uninterpretable as the referral.

## Common confusions

- "A normal examination rules everything out." It rules out what it can detect on that day.
- "Imaging is more objective, so it replaces examination." Imaging answers a narrower question than the
  examination and often needs the examination to be interpretable.
- "Neurological examination is only for neurological disease." It is a structured way to localise function
  across the nervous system.

## What the sources do not establish

The NINDS overview is a general-audience description of diagnostic categories and does not teach examination
technique, sensitivity, specificity or clinical decision rules. This vault holds no source that supports using
examination findings to diagnose any condition.
