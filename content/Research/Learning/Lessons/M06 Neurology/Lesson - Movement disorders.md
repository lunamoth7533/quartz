# Movement disorders

**Module.** M06 Neurology · **Topic.** [[Movement disorders]]

## Why this matters

Tremor, rigidity, chorea and dystonia can look similar to an untrained eye, but they point to different
circuits and often to different mechanisms. Learning the hypokinetic-versus-hyperkinetic split gives a
reader a fast, well-grounded way to sort a movement description before reaching for a diagnosis.

## The core model

**Basal ganglia modulate movement rather than generating it.** Symptoms arising from basal ganglia
dysfunction are called extrapyramidal, to distinguish them from the weakness produced by damage to the
pyramidal (corticospinal) tract, because the basal ganglia shape cortical output through direct and indirect
pathways rather than producing movement on their own.
[[F112 Neuroscience Online motor disorders#^f112-extrapyramidal|Motor Disorders]]
[[F56 Neuroscience Online basal ganglia#^f56-pathways|Basal ganglia]]

**Hypokinetic and hyperkinetic are the two poles of the family.** Parkinsonism, bradykinesia with rigidity
and resting tremor, is the hypokinetic pole, where movement is reduced; chorea, dystonia and tics are
hyperkinetic phenomena, where movement is excessive or unwanted. The two poles map onto different disruptions
of the basal ganglia's direct and indirect pathways, and they predict pharmacology in opposite directions:
reduced dopaminergic signalling produces one pole, and dopaminergic overstimulation produces the other.
[[F112 Neuroscience Online motor disorders#^f112-dyskinesias|Disorders of the Motor System]]
[[F56 Neuroscience Online basal ganglia#^f56-pathways|Basal ganglia]]

**The phenomenology comes first, before any test.** Distinguishing a tremor by its frequency and by when it
occurs, at rest or during movement, is itself a localising clue, and resting tremor at about four to seven
hertz is most often associated with Parkinson's disease.
[[F112 Neuroscience Online motor disorders#^f112-dyskinesias|Disorders of the Motor System]]

**Parkinson's disease shows that a clear mechanism does not always mean a direct test.** Parkinson's disease
is a progressive disorder in which dopamine-producing neurons degenerate, but there is no single definitive
test in ordinary clinical use: diagnosis rests on history and examination, because the specific cell loss is
not directly observable in a living person and available imaging measures structure or metabolism rather
than that loss itself. [[F76 MedlinePlus Parkinson's disease#^f76-mechanism|Parkinson's Disease]]
[[F76 MedlinePlus Parkinson's disease#^f76-diagnosis|Parkinson's Disease]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. Two movement descriptions arrive without a diagnosis attached. The
first is a steady, four-to-six-hertz tremor of one hand that is most obvious when the hand rests in the lap
and reduces during a purposeful reach. The second is an irregular, flowing, unpredictable movement of the
limbs that appears during activity and cannot be voluntarily suppressed for long. Sort by phenomenology
before naming anything. The first description, a resting tremor at a typical frequency that lessens with
voluntary movement, sits on the hypokinetic side of the family and fits the pattern usually associated with
reduced dopaminergic signalling. The second, an irregular, flowing movement, is a hyperkinetic phenomenon
consistent with chorea rather than tremor. Neither description alone names a disease; each narrows the
circuit-level hypothesis that the rest of the assessment, history, examination and sometimes pharmacological
response, will go on to test.

## Common confusions

- "A tremor is just a tremor." Frequency, whether it occurs at rest or with action, and suppressibility all
  carry different localising information.
  [[F112 Neuroscience Online motor disorders#^f112-dyskinesias|Disorders of the Motor System]]
- "Parkinson's disease is confirmed by a scan." Diagnosis rests on history and examination; imaging is not a
  direct test for the specific cell loss involved. [[F76 MedlinePlus Parkinson's disease#^f76-diagnosis|Parkinson's Disease]]
- "The basal ganglia generate movement." They modulate cortical output through direct and indirect pathways
  rather than initiating movement themselves. [[F56 Neuroscience Online basal ganglia#^f56-pathways|Basal ganglia]]

## What the sources do not establish

These sources describe phenomenology and circuit associations at a general level; they do not give
diagnostic accuracy figures for distinguishing movement disorders from one another by examination alone, and
treatment effects vary by condition and stage in ways not detailed here.

## Answer notes

1. Extrapyramidal signs come from basal ganglia dysfunction affecting the modulation of movement, while
   pyramidal-tract signs come from damage to the corticospinal pathway itself and typically involve weakness.
   [[F112 Neuroscience Online motor disorders#^f112-extrapyramidal|Motor Disorders]]
2. The hypokinetic pole, parkinsonism, involves bradykinesia, rigidity and resting tremor; the hyperkinetic
   pole includes chorea, dystonia and tics.
   [[F112 Neuroscience Online motor disorders#^f112-dyskinesias|Disorders of the Motor System]]
3. Because the specific loss of dopamine-producing neurons is not directly observable in a living person, and
   available imaging measures structure or metabolism rather than that loss.
   [[F76 MedlinePlus Parkinson's disease#^f76-diagnosis|Parkinson's Disease]]
