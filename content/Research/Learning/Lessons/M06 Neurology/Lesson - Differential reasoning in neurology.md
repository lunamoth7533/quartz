# Differential reasoning in neurology

**Module.** M06 Neurology · **Topic.** [[Differential reasoning in neurology]]

## Why this matters

Fatigue, poor concentration and sensory complaints show up in neurology clinics and in psychiatric ones.
Differential reasoning is the disciplined habit of holding several explanations open at once and narrowing
them with location, timing and pattern, rather than reaching for the first familiar label a shared symptom
brings to mind.

## The core model

**Localise first, then name.** The exam identifies where function is lost, and causes are grouped by
mechanism — vascular, infectious, inflammatory, genetic, degenerative, neoplastic, toxic, traumatic and
functional — each generating different expectations about how quickly a problem should appear and unfold.
Location plus mechanism together generate a differential rather than a single guess.
[[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-purpose|Overview of the Neurological Exam]]
[[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-causes|Overview of the Neurological Exam]]

**Signs are read through circuits, not treated as isolated complaints.** The motor-disorders literature maps
signs onto circuits, distinguishing extrapyramidal disorders that arise from basal ganglia dysfunction from
pyramidal-tract weakness, so pattern recognition in neurology is really circuit reasoning wearing a clinical
name. [[F112 Neuroscience Online motor disorders#^f112-mapping|Motor Disorders]]
[[F112 Neuroscience Online motor disorders#^f112-extrapyramidal|Motor Disorders]]

**Tempo does more work than most single tests.** Vascular disease acts over minutes, inflammatory disease
over days to weeks, neoplastic disease over weeks to months and degenerative disease over years, so a
careful timeline narrows the list before any investigation is chosen. That is why the history remains the
core skill even where imaging is freely available.
[[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-causes|Overview of the Neurological Exam]]

**A test result is an input, not a verdict.** Diagnosis is assembled by combining history, examination,
imaging and laboratory findings, and a finding that looks diagnostic in isolation, such as an imaging change
resembling multiple sclerosis, can appear without the disease. A test changes the probability of each
candidate explanation; it does not replace the reasoning that produced the list.
[[F75 MedlinePlus Multiple sclerosis#^f75-diagnosis|Multiple Sclerosis]]
[[F15 NINDS Neurological diagnostic tests and procedures#^f15-caution-modality|Appraisal: Neurological diagnostic tests]]

**Reading rule.** State the location, the time course, the pattern that fits, and what finding would
disconfirm the leading idea. [[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-domains|Overview of the Neurological Exam]]
[[F33 OpenStax Overview of the neurological exam#^f33-limit|Overview of the Neurological Exam]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A person reports six weeks of gradually worsening leg weakness plus
occasional double vision that comes and goes. Apply the two axes. Location: leg weakness plus intermittent
vision change suggests more than one site is involved, so the picture is not a single, focal lesion. Tempo:
six weeks with fluctuation does not fit a vascular event, which would be too fast to unfold this slowly, or a
fixed structural lesion, since fluctuation is unusual for that, and it is also too fast and too variable for
a purely degenerative course. A multifocal, sub-acute, partly fluctuating picture narrows the list toward an
inflammatory process affecting more than one location, which is the kind of pattern that would justify
imaging and laboratory tests chosen to look for that specific candidate, rather than a general work-up
ordered before the pattern was described.

## Common confusions

- "A named test result settles the diagnosis." A single finding is interpreted against the pattern that
  generated the hypothesis, not read on its own. [[F75 MedlinePlus Multiple sclerosis#^f75-diagnosis|Multiple Sclerosis]]
- "More tests are always safer." Tests change probabilities; ordered without a question in mind, they mainly
  add incidental findings to explain.
- "Shared symptoms mean shared cause." Fatigue, concentration problems and sensory change cross neurological
  and psychiatric categories, so overlap in symptoms is not evidence of a single underlying process.

## What the sources do not establish

These sources describe differential reasoning as a general framework; they do not quantify diagnostic error
rates for specific presentations, and they do not say how often a pattern that looks multifocal turns out to
have a single cause. Errors in both directions, missed disease and over-investigation, are acknowledged
without being sized.
