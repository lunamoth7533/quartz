# Headache and pain

**Module.** M06 Neurology · **Topic.** [[Headache and pain]]

## Why this matters

Pain is often treated as a simple signal of damage, but headache disorders show why that shortcut fails: a
disorder can be primary, meaning the head pain is the condition itself, with no tissue damage to find, while
a secondary headache signals something else entirely. Separating the two is the first and most consequential
fork in assessment.

## The core model

**Nociception is the starting signal, not the whole story.** Nociceptors are free nerve endings that respond
to damaging or potentially damaging stimuli and to chemicals released by injured tissue, the first stage of a
pathway that ends in a subjective experience rather than a direct read-out of tissue state.
[[F111 Neuroscience Online pain principles#^f111-nociceptors|Pain Principles]]

**Sensitisation explains why old injuries can stay tender.** Tissue damage releases substances that lower
the threshold of nearby nociceptors, so the same stimulus becomes more painful after injury, and processing
further up the pathway can amplify this further, letting pain persist after healing and spread beyond the
original site. This is the mechanism-level reason chronic pain is treated as a disorder of the nervous system
rather than a direct measure of ongoing damage.
[[F111 Neuroscience Online pain principles#^f111-inflammation|Pain Principles]]
[[F111 Neuroscience Online pain principles#^f111-transmission|Pain Principles]]

**Primary versus secondary headache is the first fork.** Migraine, tension-type headache and cluster
headache are primary, meaning the headache is the disorder; a secondary headache signals another cause, and
red-flag patterns, such as sudden onset, a progressive course, systemic signs or new neurological deficits,
are what change the workup rather than the presumed diagnosis.
[[F77 MedlinePlus Migraine#^f77-patterns|Migraine]]

**Migraine is a recurring disorder with a characteristic attack pattern.** Migraine attacks can include
nausea, sensitivity to light and sound, and visual or sensory changes, with attacks occurring with and
without an aura, and both frequency and triggers varying between people; imaging is reserved for
presentations that suggest another cause rather than used to confirm a typical case.
[[F77 MedlinePlus Migraine#^f77-attack|Migraine]] [[F77 MedlinePlus Migraine#^f77-care|Migraine]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. One person has had the same one-sided, throbbing headache pattern
with light sensitivity for years, always resolving within a day. Another develops the worst headache of
their life over a few seconds, with no history of anything similar. Apply the primary-versus-secondary fork.
The first pattern, recurring, stereotyped, self-limited, fits the profile of a primary headache disorder,
where imaging would typically be reserved rather than routine. The second pattern, sudden and unprecedented,
is exactly the kind of red flag that pushes the assessment toward a secondary cause and changes the workup
immediately, regardless of how severe the pain feels compared with the first. The lesson is that severity is
not what does the sorting; pattern, onset and associated features are.

## Common confusions

- "Worse pain means a more dangerous cause." Sudden onset and an unprecedented pattern are stronger red
  flags than intensity alone. [[F77 MedlinePlus Migraine#^f77-patterns|Migraine]]
- "Pain intensity is a direct readout of tissue damage." Modulation happens at several levels of the
  pathway, so reported pain can rise or fall without a matching change in tissue state.
  [[F111 Neuroscience Online pain principles#^f111-transmission|Pain Principles]]
- "Migraine is diagnosed by imaging." Imaging is used to exclude other causes in specific presentations, not
  to confirm a typical primary headache. [[F77 MedlinePlus Migraine#^f77-care|Migraine]]
