# Antipsychotic mechanisms

**Module.** M05 Pharmacology · **Topic.** [[Antipsychotic mechanisms]]

## Why this matters

For decades, "antipsychotic" meant one thing pharmacologically: a drug that blocks the dopamine D2 receptor.
That shorthand explained both why these drugs work and why they cause particular side effects. Since 2024 it is
no longer true of the whole class, which makes this a good place to practice a specific reading skill: knowing
when a settled mechanism claim needs an added date.

## The core model

Across the dopamine-blocking drugs that made up the class for decades, laboratory, imaging and clinical evidence
converge on one shared target: blockade of the dopamine D2 receptor is the mechanism behind both clinical
response and several characteristic side effects.
[[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-d2|Antipsychotics mechanisms]] Because response and
harm share a target, these drugs are described as working within a therapeutic window of D2 occupancy rather
than a dose that can simply be raised for more benefit.

That one receptor does different things depending on where it sits in the brain, so a mechanism claim only
becomes precise once a specific pathway is named: the review locates clinical response and movement side
effects in different circuits, and endocrine effects in a separate pathway again, which is why blocking one
receptor can produce several distinguishable clinical effects that do not all have to appear together in the
same person.
[[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-d2|Antipsychotics mechanisms]] Binding at other
receptor types — serotonin, histamine, acetylcholine and adrenaline receptors — adds further effects such as
weight gain, sedation or dysphoria that differ from drug to drug.
[[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-offtarget|Antipsychotics mechanisms]]

In September 2024, that one-mechanism story stopped covering the whole class. The US Food and Drug
Administration approved a combination of xanomeline, which stimulates M1 and M4 acetylcholine receptors in the
brain, and trospium, which blocks acetylcholine receptors mainly outside the brain, for schizophrenia in adults.
[[F127 FDA prescribing information COBENFY#^f127-mechanism|COBENFY prescribing information]]
[[F126 FDA Drug Trials Snapshot COBENFY#^f126-approval|Drug Trials Snapshot: COBENFY]] A separate drug in the
class, pimavanserin, works differently again: it is approved for Parkinson's disease psychosis and acts on
serotonin 5-HT2A receptors with no meaningful action at dopamine or acetylcholine receptors.
[[F128 DailyMed label NUPLAZID pimavanserin#^f128-mechanism|NUPLAZID approved label]]

**Reading rule.** Keep four separate claims apart: which subclass of drugs a mechanism statement covers, which
pathway produces which clinical effect, what else a particular drug binds, and how an individual will actually
respond. A textbook line that "antipsychotics work by blocking dopamine" is now a claim about a subclass with a
date attached, not a definition of the whole drug class.
[[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-limits|Antipsychotics mechanisms]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. Suppose an older textbook states flatly: "Antipsychotics work by
blocking dopamine receptors." Treat that as a claim to date and scope rather than accept or reject outright.
First ask which drugs it was written to cover — if it predates 2024, it describes the dopamine-blocking drugs
that then made up the entire class. Then check whether a newer drug breaks the pattern: xanomeline-trospium has
no direct D2-blocking action, and its own label calls its mechanism in schizophrenia unclear even while
attributing efficacy to muscarinic receptors.

The corrected statement becomes: "the dopamine-blocking antipsychotics work by blocking D2 receptors; the class
as a whole, since 2024, does not."

## Common confusions

- "All antipsychotics block dopamine." True of most, no longer true of the whole class.
- "One receptor means one effect." The same D2 receptor produces different effects in different pathways.
- "A drug's mechanism explains why an individual responds or does not." Occupancy accounts explain the drug's
  action on a target, not one person's outcome.

## What the sources do not establish

The review's evidence is strongest for the D2 mechanism in the dopamine-blocking drugs it surveyed; it does not
explain why response varies so much from person to person, and negative and cognitive symptoms are explicitly
areas where this account has the least reach.
[[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-limits|Antipsychotics mechanisms]] The
xanomeline-trospium trial evidence cited here is a single placebo-controlled trial and does not compare the drug
against dopamine-blocking antipsychotics.
[[P38691387 Kaul 2024 Xanomeline-trospium EMERGENT-3 trial#^p38691387-caution-comparison|Appraisal: Kaul 2024]]

2. Why can one receptor produce several different clinical effects?
3. Name one antipsychotic drug that does not fit the D2-blockade account, and what it does instead.

## Answer notes

1. Blockade of the dopamine D2 receptor.
2. Because the same receptor sits in different pathways, and blockade in each pathway produces a different
   downstream effect.
3. Xanomeline-trospium, which stimulates M1 and M4 acetylcholine receptors rather than blocking dopamine; or
   pimavanserin, which acts on serotonin 5-HT2A receptors.
