# Drug classes and mechanisms overview

**Module.** M05 Pharmacology · **Topic.** [[Drug classes and mechanisms overview]]

## Why this matters

Every later lesson in this module uses a class name - antidepressant, antipsychotic, mood stabiliser, stimulant -
as shorthand. Shorthand is useful only if you know what it stands for. Class names sort drugs by target, by
approved use or by effect, not by one shared mechanism, so learning to read past the label is the skill that
keeps every other pharmacology lesson honest.

## The core model

Two axes describe what happens whenever a drug is taken. Pharmacodynamics is what the drug does to the body -
usually by acting on receptors, enzymes, transporters or ion channels. Pharmacokinetics is what the body does to
the drug - absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion.
[[F13 NIGMS How do medicines work#^f13-targets|How do medicines work]];
[[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What happens to medicine in your body]]

Class names are not built from either axis alone. A class name can describe a target (reuptake inhibitor), an
approved or historical use (antidepressant, mood stabiliser) or an effect (stimulant), and the same label can
cover drugs with different molecular targets. "Antidepressant" contains reuptake inhibitors, enzyme inhibitors
and receptor modulators. "Mood stabiliser" is a use label whose members share no single mechanism. The label is
a navigation aid for clinicians and regulators, not a mechanism claim, and any mechanism statement built from a
class name inherits every exception inside that class.
[[F13 NIGMS How do medicines work#^f13-targets|How Do Medicines Work?]]

Antipsychotics show what a class mechanism claim looks like when it is well supported and still bounded. For the
dopamine-blocking majority of the class, the same D2 receptor blockade that produces clinical response also
produces the motor and hormonal side effects that limit the usable dose range.
[[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-d2|Antipsychotics mechanisms]] Binding at other
receptor types, off-target from the main dopamine story, adds effects such as sedation and weight gain and
accounts for much of the difference between individual drugs.
[[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-offtarget|Antipsychotics mechanisms]] But the class
now also includes a drug that works through a muscarinic-receptor mechanism rather than blocking dopamine at
all, so "antipsychotics block dopamine" is a statement about most of the class at a given date, not a permanent
description of every member.
[[F128 DailyMed label NUPLAZID pimavanserin#^f128-mechanism|NUPLAZID approved label]]

The pattern repeats elsewhere. Antidepressants raise monoamine levels within hours, yet the mood change that
gives the class its name usually takes weeks to appear - a mismatch that has pushed the field toward accounts
involving emotional processing and slower plasticity change rather than the acute chemistry alone.
[[P28153641 Harmer 2017 How do antidepressants work#^p28153641-monoamine|How do antidepressants work]] Lithium
is the clearest case of a class label outrunning mechanistic understanding: its pharmacokinetics are well
characterised, but how it prevents mood episodes over the long run remains only partly understood.
[[P25687772 Alda 2015 Lithium pharmacology and pharmacogenetics#^p25687772-unknown|Lithium pharmacology and pharmacogenetics]]
Stimulants and non-stimulants, by contrast, are named for a shared effect - increased catecholamine signalling -
reached by different routes.
[[P30097390 Cortese 2018 ADHD medication efficacy and tolerability#^p30097390-longterm|ADHD medication efficacy and tolerability]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. Suppose a science article reports: "Compound Z is classified as an
antidepressant, so it works by raising serotonin." Read that sentence the way this lesson teaches.

"Antidepressant" is a use label, not a target. It could describe a serotonin reuptake inhibitor, but it could
equally describe an enzyme inhibitor, a receptor modulator, or - among newer entries - a drug acting on a
completely different signalling system with no direct serotonin action at all. The class name tells you the
drug is used for depression; it does not tell you which receptor, enzyme or transporter it acts on. To find
that out, you would need the mechanism literature for that specific drug, not the class name on its own. The
correct next step is to ask "which target, in which study?" rather than to assume the class's most familiar
member is representative of all of it.

## Common confusions

- "Same class name means same mechanism." Class membership is organised by target, use or effect
  inconsistently, so two members can work through unrelated biology.
- "This drug treats depression, so it must raise serotonin." Antidepressant is a use label; several
  mechanistically distinct routes reach that use.
- "A drug's mechanism is settled because it is approved and prescribed." Lithium is approved, monitored and
  widely used while its mood-stabilising mechanism is still only partly understood.

## What the sources do not establish

These sources describe target categories and give worked examples from two classes; they do not supply a
complete mapping from every class name to every member's mechanism, and they say nothing about why any one
person responds to one drug and not another within a class.

## Answer notes

1. Pharmacodynamics (what the drug does to the body, usually via receptors, enzymes, transporters or ion
   channels) and pharmacokinetics (what the body does to the drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism,
   excretion).
2. Because the class's dopamine-blocking account, though well supported for most members, no longer covers a
   newer muscarinic-receptor drug in the same class.
3. "Antidepressant" and "mood stabiliser" are both use labels; "stimulant" names a shared effect reached by
   different routes.
