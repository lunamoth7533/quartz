# Antidepressant mechanisms

**Module.** M05 Pharmacology · **Topic.** [[Antidepressant mechanisms]]

## Why this matters

Every antidepressant class covered in the rest of this module - SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclics, MAOIs, the atypical
group and the rapid-acting drugs - shares one puzzle: the pharmacology happens in hours, and the clinical change
happens in weeks. This lesson lays out the accounts proposed to explain that gap, so the class-specific lessons
can build on it instead of re-explaining it each time.

## The core model

Transporter or enzyme blockade raises synaptic monoamine availability within hours of the first dose, while mood
change typically appears over weeks. Monoamine-based accounts explain that acute pharmacology poorly relative to
the clinical time course, and the review that sets out this mismatch treats it as the central theoretical
problem in the field - monoamine elevation alone is not treated as an adequate mechanistic account of why mood
improves.
[[F12 NIGMS What happens to medicine in your body#^f12-adme|What happens to medicine in your body]];
[[P28153641 Harmer 2017 How do antidepressants work#^p28153641-monoamine|How do antidepressants work?]]

Two proposals try to bridge that gap. The processing account holds that antidepressants shift emotional and
cognitive processing early in treatment - attention to and interpretation of emotional material change before
mood does - and those early biases compound into clinical change over the following weeks through ordinary
learning and environmental feedback. On this account the drug changes the system that generates mood rather than
adding mood directly, which makes "delay" a compounding process instead of a mystery. It remains a proposal with
partial evidence rather than an established mechanism.
[[P28153641 Harmer 2017 How do antidepressants work#^p28153641-processing|How do antidepressants work?]]

The plasticity account treats cellular plasticity and stress-related signalling as a complementary mechanism: the
treated system has to regain adaptive capacity, not merely receive more transmitter exposure, which links the
pharmacology to the body's stress-response circuitry. Neither account is established as the explanation, and the
two are not mutually exclusive.
[[P28153641 Harmer 2017 How do antidepressants work#^p28153641-plasticity|How do antidepressants work?]];
[[F08 OpenStax What is stress#^f08-axis|What is stress?]]

Receptor and transporter adaptation is part of the same story: these changes develop over days to weeks of
continued exposure, which is also part of why stopping a drug abruptly can produce discontinuation effects.
[[P25566076 Allouche 2014 Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance#^p25566076-tolerance|Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance]]
None of this mechanism plausibility tells a reader who will respond, how quickly, or at what cost - those are
empirical questions answered by trials with named outcomes and raters, and tolerability differs between drugs
and age groups even where the underlying mechanism story is similar.
[[P30097390 Cortese 2018 ADHD medication efficacy and tolerability#^p30097390-tolerability|ADHD medication efficacy and tolerability]]

The delay problem is sharpened, not resolved, by drugs at the fast end of the spectrum: ketamine and esketamine
can act rapidly in treatment-resistant depression with effects that outlast the drug's presence in the body, and
one synthesis argues that pharmacologically different rapid-acting compounds converge on stronger excitatory
synapses and an altered capacity for later plasticity - a version of the plasticity account above. That
synthesis is largely preclinical and concerns the rapid-acting compounds specifically; it does not show that
conventional monoamine drugs work the same way.
[[P38177353 Brown 2024 Metaplasticity and sustained antidepressant action#^p38177353-convergence|Brown 2024]];
[[P38177353 Brown 2024 Metaplasticity and sustained antidepressant action#^p38177353-caution-scope|Appraisal: Brown 2024]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A news article states: "Scientists prove depression is a serotonin
deficiency, because antidepressants raise serotonin and people's mood improves." Test that reasoning against the
timeline in this lesson. Serotonin transporter blockade happens within hours; if low serotonin alone caused the
depressed mood, relief should track the transporter blockade just as quickly. It does not - mood change usually
takes weeks, which is exactly the mismatch this lesson opens with. That gap is why the field looks past simple
monoamine elevation to processing changes and slower plasticity change. The article's causal chain skips the
several weeks of unexplained delay in the middle, which is the part that a mechanism claim actually has to
account for. A drug's action on a transmitter system is not the same claim as a settled account of what causes
the condition it treats.

## Common confusions

- "Antidepressants work quickly because they raise serotonin fast." Transporter blockade is fast; the clinical
  effect is not, and that mismatch is the field's central open question.
- "The delay is fully explained now." Two accounts - processing and plasticity - are proposed and partly
  supported, not established as the mechanism.
- "A drug's known target proves what causes the condition." A mechanism of action is a claim about the drug, not
  a settled claim about the biological cause of the condition it treats.

## What the sources do not establish

These sources describe proposed mechanisms and the evidence-quality context for the trials, not a resolved
account of why mood changes over weeks, and they do not predict which specific person will respond to which
drug or how quickly.
