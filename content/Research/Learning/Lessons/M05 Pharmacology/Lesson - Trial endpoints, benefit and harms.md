# Trial endpoints, benefit and harms

**Module.** M05 Pharmacology · **Topic.** [[Trial endpoints, benefit and harms]]

## Why this matters

Every drug-class lesson ahead quotes a benefit figure from a trial. Reading those figures well means knowing
what was actually measured, who rated it, whether the harms were reported alongside the benefit, and how
certain the evidence is - four questions that turn a headline number into something you can actually evaluate.

## The core model

An endpoint is the outcome a trial is designed to measure, and a trial's primary endpoint decides what counts as
success. Different choices answer different questions: a symptom scale measures symptoms, a functioning
instrument measures activity and participation, and a quality-of-life measure measures something else again, so
improvement on one does not imply improvement on another. WHODAS 2.0, for example, covers cognition, mobility,
self-care, getting along with others, life activities and participation - a functioning score summarises
reported activity rather than symptom severity.
[[F23 WHO International Classification of Functioning#^f23-domains|International Classification of Functioning]]

Duration is a second choice built into every trial. Short-term symptom trials dominate the evidence base for
most drug classes, while long-term questions about function and safety usually rest on observational data
carrying different limitations of their own.
[[P30097390 Cortese 2018 ADHD medication efficacy and tolerability#^p30097390-longterm|ADHD medication efficacy and tolerability]]

Rater and setting define the measurement as much as the instrument does. Self-report, clinician rating and
informant rating produce systematically different estimates, which is why trials report them separately rather
than pooling them. In the ADHD medication literature, every drug beat placebo on clinicians' ratings in
children, while only two did on teachers' ratings - a change in result produced by a change in who was rating,
not by a change in the treatment. Any benefit claim should name who rated it before it is treated as settled.
[[P30097390 Cortese 2018 ADHD medication efficacy and tolerability#^p30097390-efficacy|ADHD medication efficacy and tolerability]];
[[P29566425 Lopez 2018 Cognitive behavioural interventions for ADHD#^p29566425-caution-rater|Appraisal: Cognitive behavioural interventions for ADHD]]

Harms are part of the effect, not a footnote to it. In the same ADHD literature, amphetamines were less well
tolerated than placebo in both children and adults, and dose-related discontinuation rose above study-specific
ranges for amphetamines and methylphenidate.
[[P30097390 Cortese 2018 ADHD medication efficacy and tolerability#^p30097390-tolerability|ADHD medication efficacy and tolerability]];
[[P42134365 Nourredine 2026 ADHD medication dose-effect synthesis#^p42134365-tolerability|ADHD medication dose-effect synthesis]]
The antipsychotic literature makes the same point even more sharply: benefit and several side effects can share
one receptor mechanism, so the two are not separable questions about a single drug.
[[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-d2|Antipsychotics mechanisms]]

Certainty and recommendation are separate outputs, and conflating them is a common misreading. The GRADE
framework rates the certainty of evidence per outcome - downgraded for risk of bias, inconsistency,
indirectness, imprecision or publication bias - separately from the strength of any recommendation built on it.
A strong recommendation resting on low-certainty evidence can be entirely legitimate where the values and
context justify it; that is exactly why a reader needs both numbers, not just one.
[[P18436948 Guyatt 2008 GRADE#^p18436948-certainty|GRADE]]; [[P18436948 Guyatt 2008 GRADE#^p18436948-domains|GRADE]]

Finally, a trial that fails to detect a difference has not shown that the treatments are equivalent - absence of
evidence is not evidence of absence, and a non-significant result deserves the same scrutiny of sample size and
design as a significant one.
[[P39701638 Ostinelli 2025 Comparative interventions for ADHD#^p39701638-caution-absence|Appraisal: Comparative interventions for ADHD]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A press summary reads: "New trial: Drug Z significantly reduces
symptoms of Condition Y." Before treating that as a complete picture, ask the four questions this lesson
supplies. What endpoint was measured - a symptom scale, a functioning measure, or something else - and was it
named as primary before the trial started? Who rated it: the participants themselves, a clinician, or an
outside informant, and would the answer differ across raters? Where is the matched harm figure - did more
people on Drug Z stop the trial, and for what reason? And what certainty grade accompanies the benefit claim,
separately from how strongly anyone recommends the drug? A headline that answers none of these four questions is
not yet a claim you can evaluate; the underlying trial report usually can answer all four, even when the summary
does not.

## Common confusions

- "The drug worked, so it must be safe." Benefit and harm are measured separately and can share the same
  underlying mechanism.
- "A non-significant trial proves the treatments are equally good." It shows the trial did not detect a
  difference, which is a different claim from equivalence.
- "Low-certainty evidence means the recommendation must be weak." Certainty and recommendation strength are
  scored separately, and a strong recommendation on low-certainty evidence can be justified by context.

## What the sources do not establish

These sources describe how endpoints, raters and certainty grading work and give worked examples from ADHD and
antipsychotic trials; they do not supply a universal checklist that applies identically to every drug class, and
naming the endpoint correctly does not by itself tell you whether a specific person will benefit.

## Check yourself

1. Why can a functioning score and a symptom scale move in different directions in the same trial?
2. What did the difference between clinician ratings and teacher ratings show in the ADHD medication example?
3. What is the difference between certainty of evidence and strength of recommendation?

## Answer notes

1. Because they measure different things - a symptom scale measures symptom severity, while a functioning
   measure summarises reported activity and participation - so improvement on one does not guarantee movement on
   the other.
2. That every drug beat placebo on clinician ratings while only two did on teacher ratings, showing that the
   rater, not only the treatment, shaped the result.
3. Certainty rates how confident the evidence itself is, downgraded for factors like risk of bias and
   imprecision; recommendation strength is a separate judgement about how strongly to act on that evidence, and
   the two can point in different directions.
