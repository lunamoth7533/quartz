# First-generation antipsychotics

**Module.** M05 Pharmacology · **Topic.** [[First-generation antipsychotics]]

## Why this matters

First-generation antipsychotics are often summarised in one sentence: older, effective, but heavy on movement
side effects. Testing that summary against the actual comparisons behind it is a good exercise in reading a
class label critically, because much of what looks like a "generation" effect turns out to depend on which
specific drug was used as the comparator.

## The core model

First-generation, or "typical", antipsychotics are the earlier drugs in the class, beginning with chlorpromazine,
and they act mainly by blocking dopamine D2 receptors.
[[P39350614 Leucht 2024 Antipsychotic drugs concise review#^p39350614-limit|Leucht 2024]] Haloperidol, a
high-potency member, became the standard comparator against which most newer drugs were later tested.
[[P19058842 Leucht 2009 Second-generation versus first-generation antipsychotics#^p19058842-eps|Leucht 2009]]

That choice of comparator matters more than it first appears. Against haloperidol, newer drugs produced fewer
movement side effects even at low doses; against low-potency first-generation drugs, only a handful of newer
drugs still showed that advantage, and the low-potency older drugs caused about as much weight gain as most
newer ones.
[[P19058842 Leucht 2009 Second-generation versus first-generation antipsychotics#^p19058842-eps|Leucht 2009]]
[[P19058842 Leucht 2009 Second-generation versus first-generation antipsychotics#^p19058842-weight|Leucht 2009]]
In other words, the older drugs' reputation for causing movement problems is partly the reputation of one
particularly strong comparator drug.
[[P19058842 Leucht 2009 Second-generation versus first-generation antipsychotics#^p19058842-caution-haloperidol|Appraisal: Leucht 2009]]

Tardive dyskinesia — a movement disorder that can persist after a drug is stopped — followed a similar pattern.
Across a meta-analysis of 41 studies, it was identified by rating scale more often during first-generation
(30.0%) than second-generation treatment (20.7%).
[[P28146614 Carbon 2017 Tardive dyskinesia prevalence meta-analysis#^p28146614-class|Carbon 2017]] But people who
had never been exposed to first-generation drugs had the lowest rates of all, which means part of that contrast
reflects how long someone has been treated and in which era, not only which drug they are on now.
[[P28146614 Carbon 2017 Tardive dyskinesia prevalence meta-analysis#^p28146614-naive|Carbon 2017]]

None of this erases genuine efficacy. In a large network comparing 32 antipsychotic drugs, nearly all of them,
first- and second-generation alike, beat placebo in acute schizophrenia by their point estimates.
[[P31303314 Huhn 2019 Comparative efficacy of 32 antipsychotics#^p31303314-efficacy|Huhn 2019]] A 2024 review
goes further and argues that splitting the class into "first" and "second" generation is neither a valid nor a
useful distinction, and proposes naming each drug by its own pharmacology instead.
[[P39350614 Leucht 2024 Antipsychotic drugs concise review#^p39350614-classification|Leucht 2024]]

**Reading rule.** Before treating any first- versus second-generation comparison as settled, name the specific
drug, its potency, the specific comparator drug, and how long the compared groups had been treated. Movement-
effect contrasts shrink against low-potency comparators, and tardive dyskinesia contrasts mix different
treatment eras.
[[P19058842 Leucht 2009 Second-generation versus first-generation antipsychotics#^p19058842-caution-haloperidol|Appraisal: Leucht 2009]]
[[P28146614 Carbon 2017 Tardive dyskinesia prevalence meta-analysis#^p28146614-caution-crosssectional|Appraisal: Carbon 2017]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. Imagine a chart in a review article shows "first-generation" drugs with
double the movement-side-effect rate of "second-generation" drugs, with no drug names attached. Before accepting
the two-bar summary, ask what the first-generation bar is averaging over — if it is dominated by a high-potency
drug like haloperidol, the gap will look larger than it would against a low-potency older drug. Ask the same
question of the tardive dyskinesia figures: were the compared groups matched for how long they had been treated
and in which decade?

Once those questions are answered, the accurate version of the claim becomes something closer to: "on average
across many studies and drugs, movement side effects were more common with first-generation than
second-generation antipsychotics, but the size of that gap depends heavily on which specific drugs and
treatment histories are being compared."

## Common confusions

- "First-generation always means worse tolerated." Low-potency older drugs share many harms with newer drugs.
- "The generations are a mechanism distinction." Both act mainly through D2 blockade; the split is historical.
- "Second-generation drugs cause less tardive dyskinesia because of what they are." Part of the gap reflects
  less cumulative exposure to older drugs, not only the newer drugs' pharmacology.

## What the sources do not establish

The comparative base is short-term — mostly short double-blind trials — and confidence in many of the efficacy
comparisons is low or very low.
[[P19058842 Leucht 2009 Second-generation versus first-generation antipsychotics#^p19058842-limit|Leucht 2009]]
The tardive dyskinesia evidence rests on cross-sectional rating-scale data that could not be pooled for
severity, so how much this movement disorder burdens the people it affects is not established here.
[[P28146614 Carbon 2017 Tardive dyskinesia prevalence meta-analysis#^p28146614-limit|Carbon 2017]]
