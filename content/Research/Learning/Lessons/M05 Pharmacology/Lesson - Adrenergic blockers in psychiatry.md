# Adrenergic blockers in psychiatry

**Module.** M05 Pharmacology · **Topic.** [[Adrenergic blockers in psychiatry]]

## Why this matters

Prazosin, propranolol, clonidine and guanfacine are all described loosely as "adrenergic" drugs, but two of them
block a receptor and two of them stimulate a different one, and that distinction changes what each drug is
plausibly good for. This lesson uses trauma-related nightmares, anxiety and ADHD to show why the receptor, not
the word "adrenergic," is what decides the claim.

## The core model

Noradrenaline's effect depends entirely on which receptor it meets, and these four drugs act on different
members of that receptor family. Prazosin blocks the alpha-1 receptor; propranolol blocks beta receptors;
clonidine and guanfacine, by contrast, stimulate alpha-2 receptors rather than blocking anything.
[[P29414272 Raskind 2018 Prazosin trial in veterans with PTSD#^p29414272-rationale|Raskind 2018]]
[[P39271062 Archer 2025 Beta-blockers for anxiety disorders#^p39271062-prescribing|Archer 2025]]
[[P35697393 Newcorn 2022 Nonstimulant treatments for ADHD#^p35697393-approved|Newcorn 2022]]

In trauma, the rationale for prazosin rests on the idea that hyperarousal and disturbed sleep in PTSD reflect
overactive adrenergic signalling, making an alpha-1 blocker a reasonable target. Early trials in veterans
supported that idea, but a later, larger trial of 304 veterans found prazosin did not beat placebo on distressing
dreams, sleep quality or overall change.
[[P29414272 Raskind 2018 Prazosin trial in veterans with PTSD#^p29414272-rationale|Raskind 2018]]
[[P29414272 Raskind 2018 Prazosin trial in veterans with PTSD#^p29414272-null|Raskind 2018]] Pooling across ten
trials, however, prazosin did improve nightmares and insomnia, though not overall PTSD symptoms — leaving a
genuinely mixed picture where the pooled result and one large trial point in different directions.
[[P39828080 Mendes 2025 Prazosin for PTSD sleep meta-regression#^p39828080-sleep|Mendes 2025]] Trials with more
co-prescribed benzodiazepines showed larger prazosin effects,
[[P39828080 Mendes 2025 Prazosin for PTSD sleep meta-regression#^p39828080-moderator|Mendes 2025]] but that is a
pattern across trials, not evidence about combining the two drugs for any one person.
[[P39828080 Mendes 2025 Prazosin for PTSD sleep meta-regression#^p39828080-caution-ecological|Appraisal: Mendes 2025]]

Beta-blockers for anxiety show a different mismatch: between 2003 and 2018, prescribing of beta-blockers for
anxiety rose substantially, without matching clinical guidance, yet pooled trials found no benefit over placebo
or benzodiazepines in social phobia or panic disorder.
[[P39271062 Archer 2025 Beta-blockers for anxiety disorders#^p39271062-prescribing|Archer 2025]]
[[P39271062 Archer 2025 Beta-blockers for anxiety disorders#^p39271062-null|Archer 2025]] Given within three
months of a traumatic event, propranolol did not prevent PTSD in pooled randomised trials, where only
hydrocortisone outperformed placebo among the drugs tested.
[[P31819037 Astill Wright 2019 Early pharmacological prevention of PTSD#^p31819037-null|Astill Wright 2019]]
[[P31819037 Astill Wright 2019 Early pharmacological prevention of PTSD#^p31819037-hydrocortisone|Astill Wright 2019]]

ADHD tells a more encouraging story for the alpha-2 agonists. In children and adolescents, alpha-2 agonists such
as clonidine and guanfacine reduced ADHD symptoms in the short term with medium-to-large effects and
moderate-to-high certainty.
[[P41297970 Gosling 2025 Umbrella review of ADHD interventions#^p41297970-children|Gosling 2025]] A clinical
review places these non-stimulants as options where stimulant response or tolerability is poor, where other
conditions co-occur, or where a person prefers them,
[[P35697393 Newcorn 2022 Nonstimulant treatments for ADHD#^p35697393-role|Newcorn 2022]] while noting that
response varies both within and between the drug groups.
[[P35697393 Newcorn 2022 Nonstimulant treatments for ADHD#^p35697393-variability|Newcorn 2022]]

**Reading rule.** Always name the specific receptor and the specific action: "adrenergic" spans blockade at
alpha-1 and beta receptors and stimulation at alpha-2 receptors, and a result for nightmares, anxiety or ADHD
symptoms does not transfer to the other two targets, or from a veteran sample to civilian or complex trauma.
[[P29414272 Raskind 2018 Prazosin trial in veterans with PTSD#^p29414272-caution-population|Appraisal: Raskind 2018]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. Imagine a claim circulates: "Adrenergic drugs calm the nervous system,
so any of them should help with trauma symptoms." Test it receptor by receptor. Prazosin, an alpha-1 blocker,
has a genuinely mixed evidence base for trauma-related nightmares — a pooled benefit alongside one large null
trial. Propranolol, a beta-blocker, showed no benefit over placebo when given after trauma to try to prevent
PTSD in pooled trials.

The claim collapses once the receptors are separated: "adrenergic" is not one effect, and evidence for one
target in one condition says little about another target or another condition.

## Common confusions

- "Adrenergic blocker and adrenergic agonist do the same kind of thing." Clonidine and guanfacine stimulate a
  receptor; prazosin and propranolol block one.
- "Rising prescriptions mean rising evidence of benefit." Beta-blocker prescribing for anxiety rose without
  matching trial support.
- "A prazosin trial result in veterans applies to any trauma population." The trials were conducted in veterans,
  and transfer to civilian or complex trauma is untested.

## What the sources do not establish

Which trauma populations, if any, reliably benefit from prazosin is unresolved given the discordant trials, and
whether propranolol has a smaller preventive effect that underpowered trials simply could not detect remains an
open question.
[[P29414272 Raskind 2018 Prazosin trial in veterans with PTSD#^p29414272-caution-population|Appraisal: Raskind 2018]]
[[P31819037 Astill Wright 2019 Early pharmacological prevention of PTSD#^p31819037-caution-power|Appraisal: Astill Wright 2019]]

## Answer notes

1. Prazosin (alpha-1) and propranolol (beta) block a receptor; clonidine and guanfacine stimulate alpha-2
   receptors.
2. The largest trial found no benefit over placebo on distressing dreams, sleep quality or overall change, while
   the pooled ten-trial result showed benefit on nightmares and insomnia but not overall PTSD symptoms — a
   genuinely mixed picture.
3. Prescribing rates and trial evidence are separate facts; here, prescribing rose while pooled trials found no
   benefit over placebo or benzodiazepines.
