# Receptor adaptation tolerance and dependence

**Module.** M05 Pharmacology · **Topic.** [[Receptor adaptation tolerance and dependence]]

## Why this matters

Tolerance, dependence and withdrawal get used as near-synonyms in everyday speech, but they answer three
different questions, and mixing them up leads to real mistakes - treating withdrawal as a return of the
original problem, or assuming that dependence on its own means addiction. Separating the three words is what
lets the antidepressant-class lessons ahead describe discontinuation accurately.

## The core model

The three terms answer different questions. Tolerance answers "does the same exposure still produce the same
effect." Dependence answers "does the system now require the drug to function as it has adapted to." Withdrawal
answers "what happens when exposure stops." They travel together often enough to be confused, but they come
apart in practice: tolerance to one effect of a drug can develop while another effect stays the same or even
intensifies, dependence can develop without any pattern of misuse, and withdrawal syndromes differ in duration
and severity across drug classes.
[[P25566076 Allouche 2014 Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance#^p25566076-tolerance|Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance]]

The receptor-level mechanisms behind tolerance are a menu, not one switch: desensitisation, uncoupling from
second messengers, internalisation and altered trafficking are all candidate processes, each on a different
timescale and with different reversibility, which is one reason tolerance develops for some effects of a drug
and not others.
[[P25566076 Allouche 2014 Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance#^p25566076-desensitization|Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance]];
[[P25566076 Allouche 2014 Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance#^p25566076-mechanism|Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance]]
Receptor number and composition can change within minutes to hours, while gene expression and synaptic structure
change over days or longer, so a tolerance claim that does not name which timescale it means is not really
testable.
[[F100 Neuroscience Online synaptic plasticity#^f100-lasting|Synaptic Plasticity]];
[[P21779718 Bliss 2011 Long-term potentiation and depression#^p21779718-mechanism|Long-term potentiation and depression]]

Benzodiazepines show the three phenomena together in one class: tolerance, dependence and withdrawal appear
alongside adverse effects such as sedation and falls in older adults, a cluster shaped both by receptor
adaptation and by the clinical circumstances of long-term use. Dependence here is a physiological state and
withdrawal is the syndrome that follows a reduction in the drug; neither word is a synonym for addiction.
[[P23789008 Griffin 2013 Benzodiazepine pharmacology#^p23789008-risks|Benzodiazepine pharmacology]] Both the
opioid and benzodiazepine literatures treat discontinuation as an effect in its own right, not a failure of
willpower.
[[P25566076 Allouche 2014 Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance#^p25566076-tolerance|Opioid receptor desensitization and tolerance]]

Adaptation is not always harmful. The same word covers the receptor and transporter changes that unfold over
weeks of antidepressant use - changes that are the leading candidate explanation for the therapeutic effect
itself, not an adverse outcome. Whether an adaptation helps or costs depends on which system adapts, to what,
and against what baseline, so "adaptation" and "tolerance" should not be treated as equivalent.
[[P28153641 Harmer 2017 How do antidepressants work#^p28153641-plasticity|How do antidepressants work?]]

A recent synthesis puts a number on one discontinuation picture: across 79 studies, roughly 31% of people had at
least one discontinuation symptom after stopping an antidepressant, against about 17% after stopping placebo -
an estimated specific incidence near one in six to seven, with severe symptoms in about 3%.
[[P38851198 Henssler 2024 Antidepressant discontinuation symptom incidence#^p38851198-incidence|Henssler 2024]];
[[P38851198 Henssler 2024 Antidepressant discontinuation symptom incidence#^p38851198-severe|Henssler 2024]] The
placebo-group rate is not a footnote - it shows how much of what follows stopping any treatment is non-specific,
which matters when withdrawal has to be told apart from a return of the original problem.
[[P38851198 Henssler 2024 Antidepressant discontinuation symptom incidence#^p38851198-caution-incidence|Appraisal: Henssler 2024]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A headline reports: "New trial finds 40% of long-term users of Drug X
show dependence." Before accepting that as alarming, ask what the trial actually measured. Did it measure
tolerance (reduced effect at the same exposure), dependence (a state revealed by what happens on cessation), or
withdrawal (the syndrome itself), and for which of the drug's several effects? A dependence figure without a
matched withdrawal-severity figure and a placebo or no-treatment comparison tells you a state exists, not how
disruptive stopping would be or how much of any later symptom is specific to the drug rather than to stopping
anything routine. The claim needs the effect named, the timescale named and a comparison group before it is
readable.

## Common confusions

- "Tolerance, dependence and addiction are the same thing." They are distinct: dependence and withdrawal can
  occur without any pattern of misuse.
- "Withdrawal proves you were addicted." Withdrawal is a physiological response to reduced exposure, not
  evidence of a behavioural use disorder.
- "If the system adapted, that is a bad sign." Some of the most important adaptations, such as the weeks-long
  receptor and transporter changes behind antidepressant benefit, are the mechanism of the therapeutic effect.

## What the sources do not establish

Receptor-level adaptation is well characterised in cell and animal systems, but predicting one individual's
tolerance or withdrawal from mechanism alone is not reliable, and this library contains no tapering or
substitution guidance. The 31%/17% figures describe antidepressants as studied in the cited synthesis; they are
not a general withdrawal rate for every drug class.

## Answer notes

1. Tolerance: does the same exposure still produce the same effect. Dependence: does the system now require the
   drug to function as adapted. Withdrawal: what happens when exposure stops.
2. Because adaptation happens on different timescales - minutes to hours for receptor number and composition,
   days or longer for gene expression and structural change - and a claim that does not specify which one is
   being measured cannot be checked against evidence.
3. That a substantial share, about 17%, of symptoms after stopping treatment occurred with placebo too, showing
   that some of what follows discontinuation is non-specific rather than caused by the active drug.
