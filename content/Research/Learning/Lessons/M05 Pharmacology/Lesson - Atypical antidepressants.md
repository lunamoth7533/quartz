# Atypical antidepressants

**Module.** M05 Pharmacology · **Topic.** [[Atypical antidepressants]]

## Why this matters

Every class so far in this module is named for what it does: block one transporter, block two, inhibit an
enzyme. This one is named for what it is not. That makes it the clearest possible demonstration of this
module's opening lesson - that a class name is a navigation aid, not a mechanism claim - and the reading habit it
teaches applies to every drug you meet outside a tidy class.

## The core model

"Atypical antidepressants" is a residual grouping for drugs whose main actions fall outside the SSRI, SNRI,
tricyclic and MAOI classes; one review argues that agomelatine, a member of this group, fits none of the
existing classes at all.
[[P31211282 Fasipe 2019 Novel antidepressants and agomelatine#^p31211282-agomelatine|Fasipe 2019]] What the
members share is not a mechanism but a use: in depression they show efficacy against placebo similar to the
older classes, with differing acceptability between them.
[[P40282849 Gao 2025 Pharmacological monotherapy for depressive disorders#^p40282849-classes|Gao 2025]]

Read drug by drug and the differences are large. Bupropion is grouped as a dopamine reuptake inhibitor, placing
it with the catecholamine-acting drugs rather than the serotonin-acting ones.
[[P40282849 Gao 2025 Pharmacological monotherapy for depressive disorders#^p40282849-classes|Gao 2025]]
Mirtazapine, an atypical tetracyclic, blocks presynaptic alpha-2 adrenergic receptors and so increases both
serotonin and noradrenaline release - the same receptor profile that is linked to its sedative, antiemetic,
anxiolytic and appetite-stimulating effects.
[[P30085601 Jilani 2024 Mirtazapine pharmacology and uses#^p30085601-mechanism|Jilani 2024]] Trazodone inhibits
the serotonin transporter while also blocking serotonin type 2, histamine and alpha-1 adrenergic receptors.
[[P29262060 Shin 2024 Trazodone pharmacology and uses#^p29262060-mechanism|Shin 2024]] Agomelatine, by contrast,
is an agonist at melatonergic receptors and an antagonist at two serotonin receptor subtypes - a profile with
nothing in common with bupropion's or mirtazapine's.
[[P31211282 Fasipe 2019 Novel antidepressants and agomelatine#^p31211282-agomelatine|Fasipe 2019]] These receptor
lists rarely report relative strengths, so they describe what a drug can do, not which action dominates at a
given exposure.
[[P29262060 Shin 2024 Trazodone pharmacology and uses#^p29262060-caution-receptors|Appraisal: Shin 2024]]

On efficacy, every drug in the largest depression network beat placebo, and agomelatine was one of only two of
the 21 drugs compared with fewer dropouts than placebo.
[[P29477251 Cipriani 2018 Comparative efficacy of 21 antidepressants#^p29477251-placebo|Cipriani 2018]];
[[P29477251 Cipriani 2018 Comparative efficacy of 21 antidepressants#^p29477251-acceptability|Cipriani 2018]]
Mirtazapine is used off-label for insomnia, several anxiety disorders and migraine, and is often considered when
depression comes with insomnia or low body weight - the same sedating, appetite-stimulating profile that is a
wanted effect for some people and an unwanted one for others.
[[P30085601 Jilani 2024 Mirtazapine pharmacology and uses#^p30085601-uses|Jilani 2024]] Bupropion is also one
half of a combination with dextromethorphan that acted faster than monoamine antidepressants, though with a
similar effect size rather than a larger one.
[[P40282849 Gao 2025 Pharmacological monotherapy for depressive disorders#^p40282849-rapid|Gao 2025]]

This group also supplies the group's one sourced ADHD result: bupropion beat placebo on clinician ratings in
adults with ADHD in the largest network meta-analysis of ADHD medications, the only drug among this varied group
with ADHD trial evidence checked here - and, as with any trial ranking, that is a group-level result rather than
prescribing guidance for an individual.
[[P30097390 Cortese 2018 ADHD medication efficacy and tolerability#^p30097390-efficacy|Cortese 2018]];
[[P30097390 Cortese 2018 ADHD medication efficacy and tolerability#^p30097390-caution-choice|Appraisal: Cortese 2018]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A patient guide states: "Atypical antidepressants work by a unique
mechanism different from typical antidepressants." Test that sentence against what this lesson has shown.
"Atypical" tells you a drug's main action falls outside four other classes; it does not tell you what that
action is, and the members do not share one mechanism with each other either. Bupropion acts mainly on dopamine
reuptake. Mirtazapine blocks a presynaptic adrenergic receptor to boost release of two transmitters at once.
Agomelatine acts on melatonin receptors and has no meaningful reuptake action at all. There is no single
"atypical mechanism" to describe - the guide's sentence should be read as "these drugs are grouped by exclusion,
not by a shared mechanism," and any specific mechanism claim needs the specific drug named.

## Common confusions

- "Atypical antidepressants share a mechanism that makes them different." They share only what they are not;
  their actual mechanisms differ from each other as much as from the four named classes.
- "Mirtazapine's sedation is always a side effect." The same receptor action is a wanted effect when depression
  comes with insomnia, and an unwanted one otherwise.
- "A drug's off-label use is proof it works for that purpose." Off-label lists describe prescribing patterns,
  not trial-tested efficacy for every listed use.

## What the sources do not establish

Drug-specific serious risks described in product labels are not covered by a source checked in this library, no
evidence on this group specific to autistic people is sourced here, and the receptor-binding lists given for
each drug do not report which action dominates at ordinary exposures.

## Check yourself

1. What does "atypical" actually describe about this group of drugs?
2. Name two members of this group and one way their mechanisms differ from each other.
3. Why is mirtazapine's sedation described as both a wanted and an unwanted effect?

## Answer notes

1. That a drug's main action falls outside the SSRI, SNRI, tricyclic and MAOI classes - an exclusion, not a
   shared mechanism.
2. Bupropion acts mainly as a dopamine reuptake inhibitor; agomelatine acts on melatonin receptors and blocks two
   serotonin receptor subtypes, with no meaningful reuptake-inhibiting action.
3. Because the same alpha-2-blockade-driven increase in transmitter release that produces sedation and appetite
   gain is useful for someone with depression, insomnia and low appetite, and unwanted for someone without those
   features.
