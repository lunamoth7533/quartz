# Therapeutic index, monitoring and interactions

**Module.** M05 Pharmacology · **Topic.** [[Therapeutic index, monitoring and interactions]]

## Why this matters

Some medicines sit in a narrow window between too little and too much, and that property shapes how they are
used: monitoring, interaction checks and attention to absorption. This lesson explains the logic without
reproducing any clinical instructions.

## The core model

Every medicine sits between a concentration that does too little and one that produces toxicity; that range is
the therapeutic window.
[[F18 FDA Narrow therapeutic index drugs#^f18-window|Setting and Implementing Standards for Narrow Therapeutic Index Drugs]]

Drugs with a narrow therapeutic index need tighter control of exposure and often biological monitoring, because
small changes in absorption, metabolism or interactions can move concentration out of the window.
[[F18 FDA Narrow therapeutic index drugs#^f18-nti|Setting and Implementing Standards for Narrow Therapeutic Index Drugs]]

Interactions make the variability worse. They group as drug-drug, drug-food and drug-condition interactions,
and they can change how much drug reaches its target.
[[F19 FDA Drug interactions what you should know#^f19-types|Drug Interactions: What You Should Know]]

Where a drug acts at multiple signalling nodes, as lithium does, the mechanism story does not change the
monitoring logic: a narrow window is a property of exposure and safety, not of the receptor story.
[[P25687772 Alda 2015 Lithium pharmacology and pharmacogenetics#^p25687772-signalling|Lithium pharmacology review]]

The boundary of this lesson is explicit. The FDA directs individual decisions to product labelling and
qualified clinicians or pharmacists rather than a general guide.
[[F19 FDA Drug interactions what you should know#^f19-advice|Drug Interactions: What You Should Know]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A medicine is described in an educational article as acting on several
signalling pathways with a mechanism that is only partly understood, and its product information recommends
periodic concentration measurement.

Connect the two facts correctly. The uncertain mechanism is a scientific question about how the drug works; the
monitoring recommendation follows from the drug's narrow window and its variable exposure, which are properties
that can be assessed without resolving the mechanism. A reader who conflates them might think monitoring exists
because the mechanism is unknown — or that understanding the mechanism would remove the need for monitoring.

The accurate statement: "mechanism and monitoring answer different questions; monitoring exists because
exposure varies and the margin between benefit and harm is small."

## Common confusions

- "Monitoring measures how well the drug is working." It measures exposure, which is one input into a clinical
  assessment.
- "Interactions are rare and mostly drug-drug." Food and condition interactions are part of the same list.
- "A narrow window means a dangerous drug." It means the exposure range is narrow, which changes management, not
  necessarily the risk-benefit balance.

## What the sources do not establish

The FDA pages are general-audience guidance and do not provide thresholds, schedules or management algorithms;
this vault includes none of those by design. Nothing here supports managing any medicine without clinical
supervision.

## Answer notes

1. The concentration range between insufficient and excessive effect; a narrow window makes small exposure
   changes meaningful and usually brings monitoring.
2. Drug-drug, drug-food and drug-condition.
3. Mechanism concerns how the effect arises; monitoring concerns keeping exposure in a range, which depends on
   variability and safety margins.
