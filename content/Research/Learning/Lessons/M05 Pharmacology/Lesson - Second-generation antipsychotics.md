# Second-generation antipsychotics

**Module.** M05 Pharmacology · **Topic.** [[Second-generation antipsychotics]]

## Why this matters

"Second-generation" and "atypical" sound like they describe one kind of drug. They describe a list instead —
most members block dopamine D2 receptors like the older drugs, while a smaller group of partial agonists act at
the same receptor in a fundamentally different way. Learning to separate those two mechanisms, and to read
phase-specific uses in bipolar disorder, is the point of this lesson.

## The core model

Most second-generation antipsychotics still block D2 receptors, the same target as the first-generation drugs.
[[P39350614 Leucht 2024 Antipsychotic drugs concise review#^p39350614-mechanism|Leucht 2024]] A smaller group —
aripiprazole, brexpiprazole and cariprazine — are partial agonists at D2 and D3 receptors instead: they occupy
the same receptor but trigger a weaker, submaximal signal rather than blocking it outright.
[[P32246399 Keks 2020 Tolerability of dopamine partial agonists#^p32246399-pharmacology|Keks 2020]] Even within
that smaller group the drugs are not identical — brexpiprazole has weaker intrinsic activity at D2, while
cariprazine has the strongest D3 affinity and the longest half-life of the three.
[[P32246399 Keks 2020 Tolerability of dopamine partial agonists#^p32246399-pharmacology|Keks 2020]]

This mechanism split shows up clearly in side effects. Across an 18-drug network measuring weight, lipids and
glucose, olanzapine and clozapine had the least favourable metabolic profiles and the partial agonists among the
more benign ones.
[[P31860457 Pillinger 2020 Metabolic effects of 18 antipsychotics#^p31860457-ranking|Pillinger 2020]] But the
partial agonists are not free of harm; they carry their own signature side effect, akathisia — a feeling of
inner restlessness — which is usually mild to moderate and appears early.
[[P32246399 Keks 2020 Tolerability of dopamine partial agonists#^p32246399-akathisia|Keks 2020]]

Use is also phase-specific rather than uniform. A living umbrella review lists several second-generation drugs
as effective for adult mania, a partly different set for bipolar depression, and a smaller set again for
long-term maintenance.
[[P42580772 De Prisco 2026 Umbrella review of bipolar treatments#^p42580772-mania|De Prisco 2026]]
[[P42580772 De Prisco 2026 Umbrella review of bipolar treatments#^p42580772-depression|De Prisco 2026]]
[[P42580772 De Prisco 2026 Umbrella review of bipolar treatments#^p42580772-maintenance|De Prisco 2026]] A claim
like "this drug works in bipolar disorder" is therefore incomplete until it names which phase — mania,
depression or maintenance — the evidence actually covers.

**Reading rule.** Treat "atypical" or "second-generation" as a list, not a mechanism. For any member, ask
whether it blocks D2 or partially activates it, which harm profile it carries — metabolic, movement, prolactin,
cardiac or akathisia — and which bipolar phase or population any efficacy claim was measured in.
[[P19058842 Leucht 2009 Second-generation versus first-generation antipsychotics#^p19058842-heterogeneity|Leucht 2009]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. Suppose a summary states: "Second-generation antipsychotics are
metabolically safer than first-generation ones." Rather than accepting or rejecting that as one fact, break it
apart drug by drug. The metabolic ranking data actually show a spread within the second-generation group itself
— olanzapine and clozapine sit at one end, aripiprazole and several partial agonists at the other.

So the accurate version is not "second-generation is safer" but "metabolic risk varies enormously within the
second-generation group, and some members carry more metabolic risk than typical older comparators."

## Common confusions

- "Second-generation means partial agonist." Only three named members are partial agonists; most still block
  D2.
- "Fewer movement effects means fewer side effects overall." Metabolic and akathisia risks can be larger than
  movement risk for some members.
- "A drug listed for bipolar disorder helps every phase." Mania, depression and maintenance evidence are graded
  separately.

## What the sources do not establish

Head-to-head trials directly comparing the partial agonists against each other are still needed, and their
long-term diabetes and tardive dyskinesia risks are not yet known.
[[P32246399 Keks 2020 Tolerability of dopamine partial agonists#^p32246399-limit|Keks 2020]] The bipolar phase
lists rest on meta-analyses graded mostly moderate or low in certainty, and a review's living lists carry a
version date rather than a permanent ranking.
[[P42580772 De Prisco 2026 Umbrella review of bipolar treatments#^p42580772-limit|De Prisco 2026]]
[[P42580772 De Prisco 2026 Umbrella review of bipolar treatments#^p42580772-caution-lists|Appraisal: De Prisco 2026]]

## Check yourself

2. Name one harm that is more associated with the partial agonists than with D2 blockers.
3. Why is "this drug treats bipolar disorder" an incomplete claim on its own?

## Answer notes

1. A blocker prevents the receptor from signalling; a partial agonist still occupies the receptor but produces a
   weaker, submaximal signal rather than blocking it entirely.
2. Akathisia, a feeling of inner restlessness.
3. Because bipolar evidence is phase-specific — a drug can be well supported for mania, depression or
   maintenance without being supported for all three.
