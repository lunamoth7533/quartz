# Borderline personality disorder

**Module.** M15 Clinical Psychiatry and Psychopathology · **Topic.** [[Borderline personality disorder]]

## Why this matters

Borderline personality disorder sits at a crossroads: it shares features with bipolar II disorder, complex PTSD
and autism in women closely enough that telling them apart is one of the harder differential problems in this
library, and it has the best class-level treatment evidence of any personality construct here. Reading it well
means keeping remission of symptoms and recovery of functioning as two different questions.

## The core model

The construct describes a long-standing pattern of instability in mood, self-image and relationships, with
marked sensitivity to rejection and abandonment, affecting roughly 0.7% to 2.7% of adults and including
impulsivity, intense anger, emptiness, self-harm and severe dissociation.
[[P36853245 Leichsenring 2023 Borderline personality disorder review#^p36853245-features|Leichsenring 2023]]
Co-occurrence is close to the norm rather than the exception: most people with the diagnosis also have a mood
(83%), anxiety (85%) or substance use disorder (78%).
[[P36853245 Leichsenring 2023 Borderline personality disorder review#^p36853245-comorbidity|Leichsenring 2023]]
With overlap that common, some features credited to borderline personality disorder may partly belong to the
conditions travelling alongside it.
[[P36853245 Leichsenring 2023 Borderline personality disorder review#^p36853245-caution-overlap|Appraisal: Leichsenring 2023]]

The course separates two things that are easy to blur. In a ten-year study of 175 treatment-seeking adults, 85%
remitted below the diagnostic threshold, but social functioning stayed severely impaired and improved only
modestly.
[[P21464343 Gunderson 2011 Ten-year course of borderline personality disorder#^p21464343-remission|Gunderson 2011]]
[[P21464343 Gunderson 2011 Ten-year course of borderline personality disorder#^p21464343-function|Gunderson 2011]]
Remission there means falling below a diagnostic threshold, so a remission rate alone overstates how well people
are actually doing day to day.
[[P21464343 Gunderson 2011 Ten-year course of borderline personality disorder#^p21464343-caution-remission|Appraisal: Gunderson 2011]]

Treatment evidence at class level is the strongest part of this construct. Structured psychotherapies lowered
symptom severity compared with usual care, the one effect that passed a Cochrane review's threshold for clinical
relevance; dialectical behaviour therapy, about a third of the trials, also reduced self-harm and improved
functioning, each on a handful of small, low-quality trials.
[[P32368793 Storebo 2020 Psychological therapies for borderline personality disorder#^p32368793-tau|Storebø 2020]]
[[P32368793 Storebo 2020 Psychological therapies for borderline personality disorder#^p32368793-dbt|Storebø 2020]]
Most of that evidence is low certainty, which limits how far it should be generalised beyond the samples
studied.
[[P32368793 Storebo 2020 Psychological therapies for borderline personality disorder#^p32368793-caution-certainty|Appraisal: Storebø 2020]]

The bipolar II differential is instructive because it shows what does and does not discriminate well. Biological
comparisons - genetics, epigenetics, daily rhythms, imaging - show suggestive differences not yet good enough to
guide a diagnosis.
[[P31749106 Bayes 2019 Bipolar II versus borderline personality disorder#^p31749106-biology|Bayes 2019]] Family
history, developmental antecedents, illness course and how mood states are experienced discriminate better than
impulsivity, neuropsychological profile or treatment response, which discriminate poorly.
[[P31749106 Bayes 2019 Bipolar II versus borderline personality disorder#^p31749106-discriminators|Bayes 2019]]
[[P31749106 Bayes 2019 Bipolar II versus borderline personality disorder#^p31749106-weak|Bayes 2019]] These are
patterns seen across studies, not a validated rule for any one presentation, and the two conditions can co-occur.
[[P31749106 Bayes 2019 Bipolar II versus borderline personality disorder#^p31749106-caution-rules|Appraisal: Bayes 2019]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This is an invented reasoning exercise about a differential, not a diagnosis of any real person.

Imagine a fictional case summary: a person reports mood swings and unstable relationships, and a reader has to
weigh bipolar II against borderline personality disorder. Rather than reaching for impulsivity - one of the
weaker discriminators - work through the stronger ones: what the family history shows; when the pattern started
relative to any mood episodes; whether mood looks like discrete episodes or continuous reactivity to
relationship events; and how treatment history reads so far. None of these settles the question alone, and a
reasoned answer says which discriminators point which way, without forcing a single label onto ambiguous
information.

## Common confusions

- "85% remission means most people recover." Remission means falling below a symptom threshold; functioning
  improved far less in the same cohort.
- "Impulsivity tells the two conditions apart." It is named as one of the weaker discriminators between bipolar
  II and borderline personality disorder.
- "Co-occurring conditions are separate from the borderline picture." Mood, anxiety and substance use disorders
  accompany the diagnosis in the large majority of cases.

## What the sources do not establish

The bipolar II and complex PTSD differentials rest on narrative and systematic reviews without pooled accuracy
figures, so no source here gives a tested decision rule for an individual presentation. The strongest treatment
evidence covers symptom severity; self-harm, suicide-related outcomes and functioning rest on low-quality
evidence, and how far results from borderline samples transfer to bipolar, autistic or complex-trauma
populations is unresolved.

## Answer notes

1. Remission means falling below the diagnostic threshold; functioning, especially social functioning, improved
   far less over the same ten years.
2. Weak: impulsivity, neuropsychological profile, sex distribution, comorbidity pattern, treatment response.
   Stronger: family history, developmental antecedents, illness course, experience of mood states, personality
   style and relationships.
3. Symptom severity.
