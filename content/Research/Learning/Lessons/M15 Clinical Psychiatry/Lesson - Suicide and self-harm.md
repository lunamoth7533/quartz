# Suicide and self-harm

**Module.** M15 Clinical Psychiatry and Psychopathology · **Topic.** [[Suicide and self-harm]]

> [!warning] If you are at risk now
> This lesson is educational and cannot help in a crisis. If you are thinking about ending your life or harming
> yourself, or someone else is in immediate danger, contact your local emergency services or a crisis line now.

## Why this matters

Suicide and self-harm are described as raised in group risk across most of the conditions this vault covers, yet
the research that names those risks is unusually easy to misread as a way to judge one person. Learning to hold
"the group is at higher risk" and "this factor barely predicts who will act" as two separate, true statements is
the point of this lesson, not any detail of how harm happens.

## The core model

Research groups outcomes along a spectrum - suicidal ideation, plans, attempts and death by suicide - often
studied together as suicidal thoughts and behaviours, with self-harm studied alongside them and written into the
borderline personality disorder construct.
[[P31129238 Septier 2019 Suicidal spectrum behaviors in ADHD#^p31129238-association|Septier 2019]]
[[P36853245 Leichsenring 2023 Borderline personality disorder review#^p36853245-features|Leichsenring 2023]]

Group-level associations are consistent and often large. Across 57 studies, ADHD carried higher odds of
ideation, plans, attempts and death by suicide, largely unchanged after adjustment.
[[P31129238 Septier 2019 Suicidal spectrum behaviors in ADHD#^p31129238-association|Septier 2019]]
[[P31129238 Septier 2019 Suicidal spectrum behaviors in ADHD#^p31129238-robust|Septier 2019]] In Swedish
registers, risk was highest in autistic people without intellectual disability who also had ADHD.
[[P31238998 Hirvikoski 2020 Suicide attempt and suicide in autism#^p31238998-adhd|Hirvikoski 2020]] A PTSD
diagnosis carried roughly twice the risk of death by suicide and about four times the risk of attempts.
[[P36385705 Akbar 2023 PTSD and suicidal behavior#^p36385705-suicide|Akbar 2023]]
[[P36385705 Akbar 2023 PTSD and suicidal behavior#^p36385705-attempts|Akbar 2023]] In a bipolar cohort,
depressive severity was by far the strongest correlate of suicidal ideation.
[[P40517219 Macellaro 2025 Mixed states and suicidal ideation#^p40517219-depression|Macellaro 2025]]

None of that lets the finding travel down to a person. A meta-analysis of 365 longitudinal studies found that
risk factors predicted suicidal thoughts, attempts and deaths only slightly better than chance, and that
prediction had not improved in fifty years.
[[P27841450 Franklin 2017 Risk factors for suicidal thoughts and behaviors#^p27841450-prediction|Franklin 2017]]
Low base rates are a big part of why: even a factor that multiplies risk several times over still flags far more
people who will never attempt suicide than people who will.
[[P27841450 Franklin 2017 Risk factors for suicidal thoughts and behaviors#^p27841450-caution-base-rate|Appraisal: Franklin 2017]]
[[P31238998 Hirvikoski 2020 Suicide attempt and suicide in autism#^p31238998-caution-register|Appraisal: Hirvikoski 2020]]

The habit worth building is to keep three questions apart: does the group carry raised risk, does a factor
predict who will act, and does an intervention change outcomes - each answered by a different kind of study.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice, about reading a claim, not about anyone's situation.

Imagine a fictional press summary: "New marker triples suicide risk in condition Z." Before treating that as
useful for spotting anyone, ask what "triples" is relative to: if the base rate is 1 in 500 a year, tripling
moves it to roughly 3 in 500 - a large relative change and a still-small absolute one. Next ask what "risk" was
measured (ideation, attempt and death are not interchangeable), and whether the study combined this marker with
others or tested it alone; most fifty years of studies tested single, similar factors rather than combinations.
[[P27841450 Franklin 2017 Risk factors for suicidal thoughts and behaviors#^p27841450-homogeneity|Franklin 2017]]
A marker that clears all three checks is still a group-level finding, not a verdict on one flagged person.

## Common confusions

- "A tripled relative risk means this person will attempt suicide." Absolute risk from a low base rate usually
  stays small even after a large relative increase.
- "If group risk is real, clinicians should be able to predict who acts." Decades of risk-factor research predict
  only slightly better than chance.
- "Self-harm and a suicide attempt are the same thing." They are studied as related but distinct outcomes.

## What the sources do not establish

None of these sources support predicting an individual's outcome, and several flag their own limits: the ADHD
odds rest on studies with very high heterogeneity, the PTSD death-by-suicide estimate on seven studies with a
wide interval, and the autism findings on largely register-based comorbidity that cannot separate cause from
co-occurrence. No source here describes methods of self-harm or suicide, and none should be read as one.

## Answer notes

1. Because absolute risk depends on the base rate; tripling a small base rate still leaves most flagged people
   unaffected, which is the base-rate problem.
2. Predictive accuracy had not improved in fifty years, and studies rarely combined risk factors.
3. Whether a group has raised risk, whether a factor predicts who will act, and whether an intervention changes
   outcomes.
