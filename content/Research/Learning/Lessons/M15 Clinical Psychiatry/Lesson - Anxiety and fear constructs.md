# Anxiety and fear constructs

**Module.** M15 Clinical Psychiatry and Psychopathology · **Topic.** [[Anxiety and fear constructs]]

## Why this matters

Fear and anxiety are adaptive responses that become a disorder only when they are excessive, persistent and
interfere with functioning. The distinction between the two, and the learning research behind extinction,
explains both why anxiety disorders are grouped the way they are and why exposure-based treatment is designed
the way it is.

## The core model

Fear is the response to a detectable threat that is present now; anxiety is the anticipatory state when a threat
is uncertain or distant. Anxiety disorders are presented as a family - generalised anxiety, panic disorder and
several phobia and fear-related patterns - defined by persistent, excessive worry or fear that interferes with
daily life, distinct from ordinary situational anxiety.
[[F81 NIMH Anxiety disorders#^f81-family|Anxiety Disorders]]
[[F81 NIMH Anxiety disorders#^f81-features|Anxiety Disorders]]

The learning account explains how a neutral cue can come to evoke a fear response. Classical conditioning pairs a
neutral cue with a meaningful stimulus so the cue alone eventually evokes the response, and extinction reduces
the conditioned response when the cue is repeatedly presented without the meaningful stimulus that used to
follow it.
[[F38 OpenStax Psychology 2e classical conditioning#^f38-pairing|Classical Conditioning]]
[[F38 OpenStax Psychology 2e classical conditioning#^f38-extinction|Classical Conditioning]]

Extinction is not erasure. The extinguished response can return through spontaneous recovery, reinstatement or
renewal - the passage of time, an unrelated bad experience, or a change of context can all bring it back. That
return is exactly the evidence that extinction is new learning competing with the original association, rather
than the original association being deleted; it is the laboratory basis for exposure-based treatment, and it is
why some relapse after successful exposure is expected rather than a sign that treatment failed.
[[P22129456 Milad 2012 Fear extinction#^p22129456-newlearning|Fear extinction]]
[[P22129456 Milad 2012 Fear extinction#^p22129456-model|Fear extinction]]

Avoidance maintains what it reduces: escaping a feared situation lowers anxiety in the moment, which reinforces
the avoidance and prevents the disconfirming experience that could update the threat belief. Exposure-based
treatment works by blocking avoidance so new learning can occur, which is why reassurance-seeking belongs in the
same family as outright escape behaviour.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This is an invented scenario about reasoning through a result, not a description of any real person's treatment.

Imagine someone in a fictional case completes an exposure-based programme, does well, and then months later, in
a new and unfamiliar setting, finds the old fear response returning. Read that return through the extinction
model rather than as proof the treatment failed: extinction is described as new learning that competes with the
original association, and a change of context is one of the documented routes by which an extinguished response
can reappear. The useful next question is not "did the treatment work," but "what changed" - context, time
elapsed, or a new stressor - since each points to a different, well-described route back to the old response.

## Common confusions

- "The fear coming back means the exposure treatment didn't work." Renewal and reinstatement are described
  routes by which an extinguished response returns; some relapse is expected.
- "Avoiding a feared situation is harmless in the short term." Avoidance is described as reinforcing itself and
  preventing the disconfirming experience that would reduce the threat belief.
- "OCD is just a form of anxiety." It is classified separately, illustrating that categories track pattern and
  mechanism, not only which emotion is present.

## What the sources do not establish

Conditioning and extinction mechanisms are well established in animals and healthy humans, and treatment effects
are established in randomised trials with effect sizes that vary by condition and comparator. Translating
extinction findings into better treatments has produced fewer clinical gains than researchers once expected, so
this literature does not establish that understanding the mechanism guarantees a proportionate treatment advance.
