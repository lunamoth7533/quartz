# Emotion dysregulation across conditions

**Module.** M15 Clinical Psychiatry and Psychopathology · **Topic.** [[Emotion dysregulation across conditions]]

## Why this matters

Difficulty shaping which emotions arise, how strong they get and how they settle is reported in ADHD, bipolar
disorder, autism and complex PTSD alike. Reading a "dysregulation" finding well means treating it as a dimension
that cuts across diagnoses rather than a fact owned by one of them, and checking what state a person was in when
it was measured.

## The core model

The one meta-analysis that compares conditions directly reads dysregulation as a transdiagnostic continuum
rather than a feature that belongs to a single diagnosis.
[[P37842774 De Prisco 2023 Emotion dysregulation in bipolar disorder#^p37842774-continuum|De Prisco 2023]]

In adult ADHD, people use non-adaptive regulation strategies more often than people without ADHD symptoms, and
dysregulation tracks symptom severity, executive functioning and co-occurring conditions.
[[P36608036 Soler-Gutierrez 2023 Emotion dysregulation in adult ADHD#^p36608036-strategies|Soler-Gutiérrez 2023]]
[[P36608036 Soler-Gutierrez 2023 Emotion dysregulation in adult ADHD#^p36608036-correlates|Soler-Gutiérrez 2023]]
Whether it belongs to the core of adult ADHD, rather than riding along with severity and co-occurring conditions,
is still listed as an open question.
[[P40948064 Cortese 2025 ADHD in adults evidence base#^p40948064-open|ADHD in adults: evidence base]]

In bipolar disorder, people resemble those with major depression on most regulation strategies, differing mainly
in positive rumination and risk-taking, and they show less dysregulation than borderline personality disorder.
[[P37842774 De Prisco 2023 Emotion dysregulation in bipolar disorder#^p37842774-mdd|De Prisco 2023]]
[[P37842774 De Prisco 2023 Emotion dysregulation in bipolar disorder#^p37842774-bpd|De Prisco 2023]] That matters
because bipolar I is defined by the occurrence of mania as a sustained episode with its own criteria, so a
regulation score taken during an episode is partly measuring the episode rather than a stable trait.
[[F16 NIMH Bipolar disorder#^f16-mania|Bipolar disorder]]

In autism, dysregulation is higher than in nonclinical controls but milder than in borderline personality
disorder, with sensory sensitivity and camouflaging as predictors specific to the autistic group.
[[P38110995 Bemmouna 2023 Emotion dysregulation in autistic adults#^p38110995-levels|Bemmouna 2023]]
[[P38110995 Bemmouna 2023 Emotion dysregulation in autistic adults#^p38110995-specific|Bemmouna 2023]]

The reading habit this builds: name the condition, the instrument, and the mood state at the moment of
measurement before comparing a dysregulation score across diagnoses.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This is an invented exercise about reading a comparison, not about diagnosing anyone.

Suppose a fictional study reports that "Group A scored higher than Group B on an emotion-dysregulation
questionnaire." Before treating that as evidence the two groups differ in some deep, trait-like way, ask three
things. First, were both groups assessed in a comparable state, or could Group A have been disproportionately
in an active mood episode - which would inflate a state-dependent score rather than reveal a stable trait, echoing
the caution that a bipolar comparison has to check mood state at testing. Second, what instrument was used, since
self-report questionnaires are one of several ways to measure the same construct. Third, does the paper report
group-level averages only, or does it also show the spread - because two people with the same average can arrive
there through very different patterns. Only after these checks does "Group A scored higher" become a claim worth
carrying forward, and even then it remains a group finding, not a description of any one person in either group.

## Common confusions

- "A higher score in diagnosis X proves a shared mechanism with diagnosis Y." A shared dimension and a shared
  cause are different claims; the meta-analysis compares scores, not mechanisms.
- "Dysregulation is the same thing as a mood episode." Bipolar I is defined by episodes with their own criteria;
  dysregulation describes reactivity within any state, including outside an episode.
- "If two groups share a predictor, the predictor explains both equally well." Some predictors look shared
  across autistic, borderline and other groups, while others - sensory sensitivity, camouflaging - look specific
  to one.

## What the sources do not establish

The comparative meta-analysis could only pool bipolar disorder against major depression and against borderline
personality disorder; comparisons with ADHD, anxiety, schizophrenia and PTSD were too sparse to pool. The adult
ADHD evidence is a narrative synthesis of scarce, heterogeneous studies, and the autism and bipolar-dimension
findings each come from single cross-sectional samples. None of this establishes a shared underlying mechanism,
only that similar scores appear across different diagnostic labels.

2. Which two conditions did the De Prisco 2023 meta-analysis actually manage to compare against bipolar disorder?
3. Name one predictor of dysregulation reported as specific to autistic adults rather than shared.

## Answer notes

1. Because the score partly reflects the episode itself, and dysregulation is defined as reactivity within any
   state, not as the episode.
2. Major depression and borderline personality disorder; other comparisons were too sparse to pool.
3. Sensory sensitivity or camouflaging.
