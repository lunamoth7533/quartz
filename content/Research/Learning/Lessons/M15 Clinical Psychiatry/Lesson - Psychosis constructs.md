# Psychosis constructs

**Module.** M15 Clinical Psychiatry and Psychopathology · **Topic.** [[Psychosis constructs]]

## Why this matters

Psychosis - altered perception and thought, including hallucinations and delusions - is a symptom domain carried
by several different diagnoses, not one disease with one story. Separating the descriptive label, the
mechanistic claim and the treatment-response claim is the core reading skill this construct family demands.

## The core model

Hallucinations and delusions define psychotic experience, but the conditions that carry them - schizophrenia-
spectrum presentations, affective psychoses, substance-induced and secondary psychoses - differ in course,
associated features and treatment implications. Descriptive similarity does not imply shared cause or prognosis,
which is why ruling out substance, medical and affective explanations is part of the construct's definition, not
an afterthought.
[[F83 NIMH Schizophrenia#^f83-features|Schizophrenia]]

Schizophrenia itself is described through symptom domains - positive, negative and cognitive - which is why two
people can meet the same diagnostic label with very different day-to-day profiles.
[[F83 NIMH Schizophrenia#^f83-psychosis|Schizophrenia]] Onset is typically in late adolescence or early
adulthood, and earlier treatment is associated with a better course, which has driven early-intervention
services even as research into underlying mechanisms continues.
[[F83 NIMH Schizophrenia#^f83-course|Schizophrenia]]

The continuum finding reframed how the field thinks about risk. Psychotic-like experiences occur in the general
population at lower intensity and are far more common than diagnosable psychotic disorders; most do not persist
or convert into one. That finding pushed the field toward continuum models and clinical-high-risk identification
- and it generated its own caution, because most people who screen positive do not go on to develop a psychotic
disorder, so high-risk services carry a real false-positive burden.
[[P28333488 Kotov 2017 HiTOP#^p28333488-structure|HiTOP]]
[[P28333488 Kotov 2017 HiTOP#^p28333488-problem|HiTOP]]

The clearest mechanism claim in this construct family is narrower than it first sounds. For dopamine-blocking
antipsychotic drugs, blockade of the D2 receptor is identified as the mechanism behind clinical response to
positive symptoms - but the same receptor produces endocrine and movement side effects through different
pathways, implying a therapeutic window rather than a simple more-is-better relationship, and other receptor
binding contributes to effects such as weight gain, sedation and low mood.
[[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-d2|Antipsychotics mechanisms]]
[[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-offtarget|Antipsychotics mechanisms]] The evidence
also states plainly that antipsychotics are ineffective for some patients and that response varies, so a
mechanism story does not translate into a uniform benefit.
[[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-limits|Antipsychotics mechanisms]]
[[P31299229 Kaar 2020 Antipsychotics mechanisms#^p31299229-limit|Antipsychotics mechanisms]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This is an invented exercise about reading a screening result, not a real clinical scenario.

Imagine a fictional community screening programme flags 200 people out of 2,000 as having elevated psychotic-like
experiences. Before treating a flag as meaningful for any one of those 200, recall that most such experiences do
not persist or convert into a disorder. If the true conversion rate in a flagged group is, say, 15%, then 170 of
the 200 flagged people will not go on to develop a psychotic disorder - a large false-positive group even though
the screening tool is doing something real at the population level. The honest next step for the programme is to
report both numbers together: how many were flagged, and what proportion of those actually converted, rather
than either the flag count or the word "at risk" alone.

## Common confusions

- "Hallucinations mean schizophrenia." Several different conditions carry psychotic symptoms, including
  affective, substance-induced and secondary causes.
- "The same diagnosis means the same underlying cause." Positive-symptom and negative-symptom profiles point to
  different functional consequences without identifying different causes.
- "Antipsychotics work the same way for everyone who takes them." Response is described as varying, and the
  medications are explicitly noted as ineffective for some patients.

## What the sources do not establish

Predicting who among people with elevated psychotic-like experiences will develop a psychotic disorder remains
unreliable, and no single mechanism explains the construct as a whole - the pathophysiology is described as
heterogeneous. The D2-blockade account explains a treatment response mechanism; it does not explain why
psychosis arises in the first place.

## Check yourself

1. Why doesn't a shared diagnostic label imply a shared cause in psychosis constructs?
2. What is the practical consequence of most psychotic-like experiences not converting to a disorder?
3. Why is "D2 blockade explains antipsychotic response" a narrower claim than it might sound?

## Answer notes

1. Because different developmental routes and different symptom-domain profiles can lead to the same label, and
   the differential (substance, medical, affective, primary) is part of the construct's definition.
2. Screening for elevated experiences produces a real false-positive burden, since most flagged people will not
   develop a psychotic disorder.
3. Because the same receptor mechanism also produces side effects through other pathways, response varies across
   patients, and the drugs are ineffective for some.
