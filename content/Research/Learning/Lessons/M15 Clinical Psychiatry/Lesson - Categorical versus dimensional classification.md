# Categorical versus dimensional classification

**Module.** M15 Clinical Psychiatry and Psychopathology · **Topic.** [[Categorical versus dimensional classification]]

## Why this matters

This lesson supplies a habit used in every other lesson in this module: naming which classification model a
claim depends on before judging it. Categories and dimensions are not rival truths about the mind; they are two
different instruments, each useful where its assumptions hold.

## The core model

Categories compress information into a shared label. ICD-11's clinical descriptions give each category essential
features, boundaries with neighbouring categories, and course qualifiers, precisely so a label means the same
thing to different clinicians - which is what lets services and treatment trials define who is being studied.
[[F93 WHO ICD-11 CDDR#^f93-structure|ICD-11 CDDR]]
[[F93 WHO ICD-11 CDDR#^f93-purpose|ICD-11 CDDR]]

That same structure has costs. Because a threshold has to fall somewhere, categories produce arbitrary
thresholds, unclear boundaries between neighbouring disorders, frequent co-occurrence, variation within a single
category, and instability - people near a boundary can move between labels across assessments. These documented
weaknesses are what motivated dimensional alternatives.
[[P28333488 Kotov 2017 HiTOP#^p28333488-problem|HiTOP]]

Dimensional research programmes respond by grouping covarying syndromes into continuous spectra, representing
heterogeneity rather than averaging it away, and mapping co-occurrence explicitly - while stating plainly that
this is a research programme rather than an installed clinical system.
[[P28333488 Kotov 2017 HiTOP#^p28333488-structure|HiTOP]]
[[P28333488 Kotov 2017 HiTOP#^p28333488-purpose|HiTOP]]

A third option treats symptoms as interacting components rather than as signs of one hidden disorder - the
disorder is the network of interactions itself - predicting sudden shifts and reading co-occurrence as overlap
between symptom networks rather than as two separate entities happening to coincide.
[[P28127906 Borsboom 2017 Network theory#^p28127906-claim|A network theory of mental disorders]]
[[P28127906 Borsboom 2017 Network theory#^p28127906-consequences|A network theory of mental disorders]]

Dimensional measurement is not assumption-free either. Comparing scores across groups or over time assumes
measurement invariance - that the same score means the same thing in each group or at each time point - and that
assumption has levels (configural, metric, scalar) that are testable and often left untested. Partial or absent
invariance changes what a comparison actually means, whichever framework produced the score.
[[P27942093 Putnick 2016 Measurement invariance#^p27942093-definition|Measurement invariance]]
[[P27942093 Putnick 2016 Measurement invariance#^p27942093-levels|Measurement invariance]]
[[P27942093 Putnick 2016 Measurement invariance#^p27942093-practice|Measurement invariance]]

The practical discipline this note asks for: descriptive claims such as "these features cluster together"
survive either framework; claims about thresholds, prevalence and diagnostic stability are framework-dependent;
and treatment evidence is organised by whatever definition a given trial used.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This is an invented exercise about comparing two studies, not a real dataset.

Suppose a fictional Study A reports a condition's prevalence at 8% using a strict categorical cut-off, while
fictional Study B reports 22% using a continuous severity scale with a lower threshold. Before concluding the
condition became three times more common, or that one study is wrong, check what each measured: different
frameworks (categorical versus dimensional) and possibly different thresholds within the dimensional one. Then
ask whether either study tested measurement invariance - whether their instrument means the same thing across
whatever subgroups it compares. Only after naming the framework, the threshold and the invariance evidence can
the two numbers be compared honestly, and even then, they may simply be answering different questions.

## Common confusions

- "Dimensions are simply the more accurate replacement for categories." Each system wins where its own
  assumptions hold; categories communicate efficiently and match service structures, while dimensions preserve
  information where variation is genuinely continuous.
- "A dimensional score doesn't need a threshold." Dimensional scores still require thresholds to be useful in
  services, so the practical debate partly relocates rather than disappearing.
- "Network theory has replaced categorical diagnosis." It is described as a research proposal with early
  empirical support, not a replacement for classification in clinical use.

## What the sources do not establish

The empirical case for dimensionality is described as strong for many constructs, but how to translate
dimensions into clinical decisions is unresolved, which is exactly why hybrid models - categorical diagnosis with
dimensional measures alongside it - are proposed rather than a clean replacement. The network approach's own
literature discusses its limitations rather than presenting it as settled.

## Check yourself

1. Name two documented costs of categorical classification that motivated dimensional alternatives.
2. What does the network approach say co-occurrence actually is?
3. Why can't two prevalence figures from different studies be compared without checking measurement invariance?

## Answer notes

1. Any two of: arbitrary thresholds, unclear boundaries between categories, frequent co-occurrence,
   within-category heterogeneity, diagnostic instability.
2. Overlap between two symptom networks, rather than two separate disorders that happen to co-occur.
3. Because a score might not mean the same thing across the groups or time points being compared; without
   invariance, a difference in scores could reflect measurement rather than a real difference.
