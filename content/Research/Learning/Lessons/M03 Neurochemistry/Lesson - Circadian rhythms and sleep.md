# Circadian rhythms and sleep

**Module.** M03 Neurochemistry · **Topic.** [[Circadian rhythms and sleep]]

## Why this matters

Sleep is the most accessible example of a two-process system, and the most frequently misunderstood: duration
and timing are different variables, and treatments that target one do not automatically move the other. The
bipolar psychotherapy literature depends on this distinction, which is why it sits in the foundation modules.

## The core model

Sleep cycles through REM and non-REM stages, each associated with different patterns of brain activity.
[[F14 NINDS Brain basics understanding sleep#^f14-stages|Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep]]

Sleep timing is regulated by circadian rhythms interacting with homeostatic sleep drive: how long you have been
awake pushes toward sleep, while the internal clock determines when sleep is possible.
[[F14 NINDS Brain basics understanding sleep#^f14-rhythm|Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep]]

The two-process model explains why regular wake time matters independently of total sleep. The homeostatic
component can be satisfied by sleeping at any hour; the circadian component needs regularity and light cues to
stay aligned with the day. When they separate — as in shift work, travel or irregular schedules — people can
get enough hours and still feel unrefreshed.

Stress physiology interacts here too: cortisol follows a daily rhythm, and acute stress responses alter sleep
timing and quality.
[[F08 OpenStax What is stress#^f08-axis|What Is Stress?]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. Two people both report sleeping seven hours. The first sleeps from
11 pm to 6 am on a steady schedule. The second sleeps from 3 am to 10 am on weekdays and 11 pm to 6 am on
weekends.

Compare the variables. Duration is similar, so a study measuring only hours would call them equivalent. Timing
and regularity differ, which affects the circadian component: the weekend shift is a recurring phase change that
the clock has to absorb, and the pattern is associated with poorer sleep quality in shift-work literature.

The refined summary: "both report seven hours; they differ in sleep timing regularity, which the two-process
model treats as a separate variable with its own consequences." That distinction is what makes social rhythm
interventions in bipolar care intelligible.

## Common confusions

- "Sleep is one variable." Duration, timing, regularity and quality are separable and measured differently.
- "More sleep fixes timing problems." Duration and circadian alignment are different processes.
- "The model is a law." It describes average regulation; illness, medication and light exposure complicate it.

## Check yourself

1. Name the two processes that regulate sleep timing.
2. Why can two people with the same sleep duration differ in sleep quality?
3. Why does regular wake time matter for circadian stability?

## Answer notes

1. Circadian rhythm and homeostatic sleep drive.
2. Because timing regularity and phase alignment differ, and the same duration at different clock times
   interacts differently with the circadian system.
3. Because light and activity at a consistent wake time anchor the clock, keeping the circadian component
   aligned with the day.
