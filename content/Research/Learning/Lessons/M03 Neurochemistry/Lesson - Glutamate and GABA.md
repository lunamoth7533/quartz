# Glutamate and GABA

**Module.** M03 Neurochemistry · **Topic.** [[Glutamate and GABA]]

## Why this matters

These are the workhorse transmitters: most fast excitation and inhibition in the adult central nervous system
runs on glutamate and GABA. That breadth is why "excitation-inhibition imbalance" shows up in so many
explanations, and why it so often collapses into a metaphor. This lesson keeps the mechanism and the
measurement problem visible at once.

## The core model

Glutamate receptors depolarise adult postsynaptic cells while glycine and GABA receptors hyperpolarise them.
[[F04 OpenStax Communication between neurons#^f04-receptor|Communication Between Neurons]]

The same receptor principle applies: glutamate has ionotropic and metabotropic receptors with different time
courses, and GABA has multiple receptor subtypes, so "glutamate excites" is a summary of the common case rather
than a law about every synapse.

Clinical genetics offers a concrete version of the balance idea. In autism exome data, risk-gene expression was
enriched in excitatory and inhibitory lineages, which the authors describe as consistent with multiple
imbalance paths rather than one mechanism.
[[P31981491 Satterstrom 2020 Autism exome sequencing#^p31981491-model|Autism exome sequencing study]]

Note what that finding is: an enrichment of gene expression across lineage types in a large sequencing study.
It supports the idea that both excitatory and inhibitory development contribute to risk. It does not measure
balance in any person's brain, and the authors do not claim a single pathway.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A review argues that a condition arises from "excessive excitation and
insufficient inhibition", citing reduced GABA receptor expression in post-mortem tissue and increased
glutamate release in an animal model.

Interrogate the inference. Post-mortem expression is a measure of tissue, taken at the end of life with
medication history and cause of death as confounds. Animal release measurements are from a different species,
often under anaesthesia or in slices. Neither measures circuit balance in a living person, and "excessive
relative to what baseline" is undefined.

The defensible claim: "findings in post-mortem tissue and animal models are consistent with altered excitatory
or inhibitory signalling in this condition; whether and where net balance is altered in living people has not
been established by these measurements."

## Common confusions

- "Excitation-inhibition balance is a brain-wide quantity." It is local, dynamic and defined at an integration
  point; global statements require a measurement that usually does not exist.
- "More excitation means more activity." Inhibition can sharpen timing or gate signals rather than simply
  reduce firing.
- "A gene expressed in inhibitory cells means inhibition is the cause." Enrichment is a clue about which
  lineages matter, not a direction of effect.

## What the sources do not establish

This vault has no direct measurement of excitation-inhibition balance in humans and no source that validates
the phrase as a clinical explanation. The autism genetic finding is an expression-enrichment result in a
case-control design, with the limits recorded in that source note.

## Check yourself

1. Which transmitters provide most fast inhibition and excitation in the adult central nervous system?
2. Why is "excessive excitation" meaningless without a specified baseline and location?
3. What kind of measurement would make an imbalance claim testable in humans?

## Answer notes

1. GABA (with glycine in some contexts) for inhibition and glutamate for excitation.
2. Because balance is defined locally and dynamically, so "excessive" requires a comparison point and a
   measurement site.
3. A local measure of excitatory and inhibitory conductances or their proxies, measured in the relevant circuit,
   with a defined baseline, ideally in vivo.
