# Synaptic signalling model

**Module.** M03 Neurochemistry · **Topic.** [[Synaptic signalling model]]

## Why this matters

Almost every claim about a transmitter, a medicine or a condition in this vault runs through one model:
release, receptor binding, signal termination, modulation. Learn the model and you can locate a claim; skip it
and you are left repeating brand names of chemicals.

## The core model

At a chemical synapse the action potential opens voltage-gated calcium channels, vesicles fuse with the
presynaptic membrane, transmitter is released into the cleft, and the signal is terminated by degradation or
by neuronal and glial reuptake.
[[F04 OpenStax Communication between neurons#^f04-release|Communication Between Neurons]]

The postsynaptic effect depends on the receptor rather than on the transmitter alone, so the same molecule can
excite or inhibit depending on which receptor it meets.
[[F04 OpenStax Communication between neurons#^f04-receptor|Communication Between Neurons]]

Transmitters in this vault — acetylcholine, dopamine, serotonin, noradrenaline, GABA and glutamate — are used
across systems with different associated functions, which is another way of saying the molecule does not
determine the message.
[[F06 OpenStax Cells of the nervous system#^f06-transmitters|Cells of the Nervous System]]

Electrical synapses formed by gap junctions are much less common than chemical synapses and much faster,
which is why most pharmacology targets the chemical machinery.
[[F06 OpenStax Cells of the nervous system#^f06-electrical|Cells of the Nervous System]]

Six places exist for something to change: how much transmitter is released, how long it stays in the cleft, how
the receptor responds, what second messengers do downstream, how the signal is terminated, and how the cell's
state changes what any of those mean.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A hypothetical compound increases a behavioural response in an animal
model, and the discussion claims it "increases dopamine signalling".

Locate the claim. Which step changed? Synthesis and packaging, release probability, reuptake, degradation,
receptor affinity, or downstream signalling? Increase in dopamine signalling measured how — extracellular
concentration, receptor occupancy, firing rate of dopamine neurons, or an indirect behavioural proxy? And in
which circuit: the effect of dopamine depends on the pathway and receptor subtype.

The precise sentence: "the compound increased [specific measure] in [specific circuit], consistent with
increased dopamine availability at [specific receptors]." If the steps cannot be filled, the original claim is
a heading, not a finding.

## Common confusions

- "Transmitter names describe experiences." They describe molecules; the effect depends on receptor, circuit and
  state.
- "More transmitter means more of the effect." Reuptake, receptor state and downstream adaptation all intervene.
- "The synaptic model covers everything." Volume transmission, glial release and neuromodulation are less tidy
  and are not covered by this vault's teaching sources.

## What the sources do not establish

The textbook pages describe the classical model and simplify the molecular detail; they do not quantify
transmission at any real synapse or cover extrasynaptic signalling. Specific claims about any compound or
condition need their own evidence, which this vault records in the relevant source notes.

## Check yourself

1. Name the steps of chemical synaptic transmission in order.
2. What determines whether a transmitter excites or inhibits the postsynaptic cell?
3. Give two ways a drug could change signalling without changing how much transmitter is released.

## Answer notes

1. Action potential, calcium entry, vesicle fusion, release, receptor binding and effect, then termination by
   degradation or reuptake.
2. The receptor (and its subtype) on the postsynaptic membrane, plus the cell's state and ion gradients.
3. Acting on receptors as an agonist or antagonist, or blocking reuptake or degradation to extend the
   transmitter's time in the cleft.
