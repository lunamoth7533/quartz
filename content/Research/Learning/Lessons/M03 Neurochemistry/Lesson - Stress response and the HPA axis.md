# Stress response and the HPA axis

**Module.** M03 Neurochemistry · **Topic.** [[Stress response and the HPA axis]]

## Why this matters

Stress physiology is where the chemistry of the body meets the psychology of appraisal, and it is where the
phrase "cortisol levels" gets used as if it were a single dial. This lesson installs the two-process model and
the measurement discipline that goes with it.

## The core model

The stress response begins with appraisal: the same demand can be experienced as a threat or a challenge, and
that difference changes the response.
[[F08 OpenStax What is stress#^f08-appraisal|What Is Stress?]]

The fast sympathetic branch mobilises the body quickly, while the HPA axis is slower and ends in cortisol
release, which mobilises energy and modulates many systems.
[[F08 OpenStax What is stress#^f08-axis|What Is Stress?]]

Cortisol is not a single stress molecule. It follows a daily rhythm, responds to many inputs including waking,
exercise and food, and acts as a permissive and modulatory hormone rather than a linear readout.

Acute stress responses are adaptive; chronic activation is associated with wear on regulation, mood and health.
[[F08 OpenStax What is stress#^f08-chronic|What Is Stress?]]

The autonomic contribution deserves its own mention here because the two systems interact: the sympathetic
branch is part of the same response, and the autonomic nervous system also maintains baseline stability at rest.
[[F01 OpenStax Nervous system structure and function#^f01-autonomic|Basic Structure and Function of the Nervous System]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A study measures morning cortisol once in a group of people under
chronic strain and finds no difference from controls. A commentary concludes that chronic stress does not
affect the stress axis.

Audit the inference. A single morning sample sits on top of a 24-hour rhythm, a waking response, day-to-day
variation and many influences including sleep, medication and illness. A null result from one sample cannot
speak to axis dysregulation, which is usually studied through patterns: diurnal slope, reactivity to challenge,
and recovery after a stressor, ideally repeated over time.

The accurate conclusion: "in this sample, one morning cortisol measurement did not differ between groups;
chronic axis dysregulation is a pattern measure and would need a different design to test."

## Common confusions

- "Cortisol means stress." It is a hormone with a rhythm and many roles; a single value is nearly uninterpretable.
- "Stress is what happens to you." Appraisal is part of the response, so the same event is not the same stressor
  for everyone.
- "Acute stress is bad." Acute mobilisation is adaptive; the concern is chronic activation and its wear.

## What the sources do not establish

The educational source is a general-audience overview of stress concepts and does not provide measurement
protocols or clinical thresholds. This vault holds no source that validates any cortisol measure as a
diagnostic or personal test.

## Answer notes

1. A fast sympathetic branch and a slower HPA axis ending in cortisol release.
2. Because cortisol follows a daily rhythm and responds to many inputs, and chronic dysregulation is usually a
   pattern rather than a level.
3. Diurnal patterns, reactivity and recovery to challenges, and repeated measurements over time, with sleep,
   medication and health covariates.
