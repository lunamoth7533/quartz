# Short-term versus long-term outcomes

**Module.** M08 ADHD · **Topic.** [[Short-term versus long-term outcomes]]

## Why this matters

Most treatment evidence covers weeks. People live for decades. The gap between those timescales is where
confidence outruns data, and it operates in both directions: short-term benefit does not prove long-term
benefit, and missing long-term data does not prove long-term harm.

## The core model

An ADHD network meta-analysis found insufficient data at 26 and 52 weeks and called for research on long-term
effects.
[[P30097390 Cortese 2018 ADHD medication efficacy and tolerability#^p30097390-longterm|ADHD medication network meta-analysis]]

A 2025 adult review keeps long-term treatment effects among its open questions.
[[P40948064 Cortese 2025 ADHD in adults evidence base#^p40948064-open|ADHD in adults review]]

And the 2025 adult synthesis reports that medications were not efficacious on additional outcomes such as
quality of life, which are closer to daily functioning than symptom scales.
[[P39701638 Ostinelli 2025 Comparative interventions for ADHD#^p39701638-longterm|Comparative efficacy of ADHD interventions in adults]]

There is a structural reason for the gap. Randomised trials of chronic conditions are hard to run over years:
people switch treatments, drop out, and the ethical case for continuing a control condition weakens when an
effective treatment exists. Observational cohorts can follow people for years and they inherit confounding,
because treatment is chosen partly on the basis of the factors that also predict outcome.

Continue by asking what "long-term" means in a given literature. In the ADHD syntheses it meant 26 and 52 weeks,
which was enough to expose the gap; in other conditions long-term studies run for years. A 12-week trial cannot
speak to either horizon, so write the actual interval next to the phrase whenever you read a summary.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A clinician says: "Twelve-week trials show symptom benefit, so we can
assume long-term benefit." A second says: "There is no long-term evidence, so we cannot assume benefit at all."

Both sentences overreach in opposite directions. The trial evidence supports a short-term average effect on
symptom outcomes; extending it to years is an assumption, not a finding, and the mechanism does not guarantee
that effects persist or that function follows. The absence of long-term randomised data is not evidence of
absence of effect either; it is a gap in the evidence base, and observational data may partly fill it while
carrying confounding.

The accurate position: "short-term symptom benefit is established on average; long-term effects on function,
quality of life and safety are not established, and the open question is genuine."

## Common confusions

- "If it works short-term it works long-term." Persistence of effect is an empirical question.
- "No long-term evidence means no long-term benefit." Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
- "Observational data will settle it." Cohort data help and carry treatment-selection confounding.

## What the sources do not establish
