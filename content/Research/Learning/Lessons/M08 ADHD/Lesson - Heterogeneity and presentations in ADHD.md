# Heterogeneity and presentations in ADHD

**Module.** M08 ADHD · **Topic.** [[Heterogeneity and presentations in ADHD]]

## Why this matters

Two people can carry the same ADHD diagnosis and struggle with almost nothing in common - one loses track of time, another cannot sit through a meeting, a third forgets instructions the moment they are given. Knowing that the diagnosis covers a genuinely heterogeneous set of profiles, rather than one shared deficit with different severities, changes how every later claim in this module should be read: a group average can be true of a subgroup and irrelevant to the person sitting across from it.

## The core model

The three ADHD presentations - predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive and combined - record which symptom family currently dominates, not which mechanism produced it. The definition used across this module is a developmental pattern of inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity that interferes with functioning, and two people who share a presentation label can still find completely different tasks hard. [[F86 NIMH ADHD#^f86-definition|ADHD]]

The strongest evidence for heterogeneity below the presentation level comes from a study that gave children with ADHD, their siblings and controls nine tasks measuring inhibitory control, delay aversion and timing. Rather than one core deficit, the three components behaved as separable pieces: some children were impaired on one, some on another, and the ADHD group differed from controls on all three overall. [[P20410727 Sonuga-Barke 2010 Beyond the dual pathway#^p20410727-design|Beyond the dual pathway model]] [[P20410727 Sonuga-Barke 2010 Beyond the dual pathway#^p20410727-finding|Beyond the dual pathway model]]

That pattern is a problem for any account that treats ADHD as one shared impairment differing only in severity. If the underlying components dissociate, different people can reach the same diagnosis by different routes, and a group difference measured on any single task will often be carried by only part of the sample - which is a plain reason cognitive findings in this area are hard to replicate from one study to the next. [[P20410727 Sonuga-Barke 2010 Beyond the dual pathway#^p20410727-implication|Beyond the dual pathway model]]

Heterogeneity also runs across the lifespan. Adult prevalence is estimated around 2.5%, and up to 70% of people diagnosed in childhood keep impairing symptoms as adults, sometimes without meeting the full childhood criteria any longer. The same evidence review lists late-onset presentations and the absence of an objective diagnostic test among its open questions, which means age-related heterogeneity is an active research problem rather than a settled footnote. [[P40948064 Cortese 2025 ADHD in adults evidence base#^p40948064-base|ADHD in adults: evidence base]] [[P40948064 Cortese 2025 ADHD in adults evidence base#^p40948064-open|ADHD in adults: evidence base]]

Brain-based work tells a similar story from a different angle. Clustering resting-state brain connectivity in two independent samples produced replicable subgroups that differed in intelligence and hyperactivity-impulsivity - but the subgroups cut straight across ADHD, autism, obsessive-compulsive disorder and typical development, rather than lining up with any one diagnosis. [[P36912839 Vandewouw 2023 Replicable transdiagnostic brain subgroups#^p36912839-subgroups|Vandewouw 2023]] [[P36912839 Vandewouw 2023 Replicable transdiagnostic brain subgroups#^p36912839-transdiagnostic|Vandewouw 2023]] That result describes variation among people, not a cause anyone could use to sort an individual, and it should not guide a decision about one person. [[P36912839 Vandewouw 2023 Replicable transdiagnostic brain subgroups#^p36912839-caution-subgroups|Appraisal: Vandewouw 2023]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

Imagine a fictional study reporting that "children with ADHD show impaired working memory," with a headline effect size in the medium range. Read it the way heterogeneity requires. First, a medium group difference means the two distributions overlap substantially - many children with ADHD will score in the typical range on that specific task, and some children without ADHD will score below them. Second, ask whether the sample was recruited from a clinic or a school, since referred samples tend to be more severe and less representative. Third, ask whether the paper reports how many individual children actually showed the deficit, rather than only the group mean - the nine-task design above shows that different components dissociate, so a working-memory result could easily be carried by a subset of the sample. The accurate summary is not "ADHD causes a working-memory deficit" but "in this sample, the ADHD group scored lower on average on this working-memory task, and the paper does not say whether that held for most of the group or a minority of it."

## Common confusions

- "ADHD is one deficit with different severities." The dissociable-components evidence argues against a single shared cause; different routes can produce the same diagnosis.
- "A named presentation (inattentive, hyperactive-impulsive, combined) tells you the mechanism." Presentations describe which symptoms dominate, not what produces them.
- "Because heterogeneity is well established, subtypes can now guide treatment choice." Subclassification schemes are proposed, not validated for predicting an individual's treatment response.

## What the sources do not establish

None of these sources identifies which mechanism applies to a given individual, and the brain-subgroup evidence explicitly cuts across diagnostic categories rather than confirming any ADHD-specific subtype. Whether matching a treatment to a cognitive or a brain-based profile improves outcomes remains an open research question, not a demonstrated practice.
