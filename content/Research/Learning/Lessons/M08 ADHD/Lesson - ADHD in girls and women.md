# ADHD in girls and women

**Module.** M08 ADHD · **Topic.** [[ADHD in girls and women]]

## Why this matters

Diagnosed ADHD is far more common in boys than girls in childhood, and that gap narrows by adulthood. How much
of that reflects a real difference in the condition versus who gets referred and diagnosed is a direct test of
the previous lesson's measurement skills, applied to a question with real consequences for recognition.

## The core model

**The diagnosed sex ratio shifts with age.** In Welsh national records for over 16,000 young people with ADHD,
males outnumbered females about four to one overall, nearly five to one before age 12, and under two to one
after 18.
[[P38864317 Martin 2024 Sex differences in ADHD diagnosis#^p38864317-ratio|Martin 2024]]
Adult screening tells a different story: across 42 countries, 21.4% of women and 18.1% of men screened as at
risk - a rate far above known prevalence, marking a prompt for assessment rather than a diagnosis.
[[P38180045 Lewczuk 2024 ADHD screener across 42 countries#^p38180045-atrisk|Lewczuk 2024]]

**Recognition comes later, often after other diagnoses.** Welsh females were diagnosed later on average (12.6
versus 10.9 years) and less often prescribed medication; they more often already had a recorded anxiety or
depression diagnosis, read by the authors as possible overshadowing. Records cannot say whether an earlier
diagnosis was a missed ADHD presentation, a genuine co-occurring condition, or both.
[[P38864317 Martin 2024 Sex differences in ADHD diagnosis#^p38864317-timing|Martin 2024]]
[[P38864317 Martin 2024 Sex differences in ADHD diagnosis#^p38864317-overshadowing|Martin 2024]]
[[P38864317 Martin 2024 Sex differences in ADHD diagnosis#^p38864317-caution-records|Appraisal: Martin 2024]]

**Presentation differences are real but small.** A meta-analysis of 52 diagnosed samples found males somewhat
more severe on hyperactivity-impulsivity in children and more inattentive than women in adults; every
significant difference was small, and clinical interviews - unlike rating scales - found no sex difference in
severity at any age. Because every sample was already diagnosed, this cannot show what unrecognised girls and
women look like.
[[P39494848 Young 2024 ADHD symptom severity in females and males#^p39494848-interview|Young 2024]]
[[P39494848 Young 2024 ADHD symptom severity in females and males#^p39494848-caution-diagnosed|Appraisal: Young 2024]]

**Co-occurring conditions shape recognition.** Diagnosed ADHD and PTSD together were more common in females, an
excess appearing only in adult samples - a pattern that can reflect exposure, recognition or referral as much as
vulnerability.
[[P40252259 Wilson 2025 Sex differences in ADHD and PTSD#^p40252259-sex|Wilson 2025]]
[[P40252259 Wilson 2025 Sex differences in ADHD and PTSD#^p40252259-caution-diagnosis|Appraisal: Wilson 2025]]

**Hormonal influences are a hypothesis, not a mechanism.** A small systematic review of eleven studies mostly
pointed to symptoms varying around puberty and the menstrual cycle, with menopause unstudied.
[[P40251875 Osianlis 2025 ADHD and sex hormones in females#^p40251875-evidence|Osianlis 2025]]

**Reading rule.** Before reading a sex difference as a difference in ADHD, check whether the sample was
diagnosed, referred or population-based; which instrument and informant were used; and whether the figure counts
diagnoses, prescriptions or symptoms.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A hypothetical magazine article claims: "Girls have a completely
different, quieter form of ADHD, only now being discovered, which explains why so many go undiagnosed."

A distinct presentation would need severity differences that hold up under the most rigorous measure available;
instead, clinical interviews found none at any age, and the small differences that do appear are confined to
rating scales - a rater-and-instrument effect, not proof of a different condition. A claim about how many girls
go undiagnosed cannot come from a meta-analysis built entirely from already-diagnosed samples, which by
definition says nothing about who was missed.

The defensible version is narrower: diagnosed females are recognised later, often after an anxiety or depression
diagnosis, and small, instrument-dependent differences exist among the already diagnosed. Whether an
unrecognised, systematically different presentation exists is an open question, not a confirmed discovery.

## Common confusions

- "Girls have a distinct, established form of ADHD." Clinical interviews found no sex difference in severity;
  rating-scale differences were small.
- "A screening rate this high means ADHD is more common in women." At-risk screening runs far above known
  prevalence and prompts assessment, not diagnosis.
- "An earlier anxiety or depression diagnosis was always a missed ADHD case." Records cannot distinguish
  overshadowing from a genuine co-occurring condition.

## What the sources do not establish

None of these sources shows how many girls and women with impairing ADHD go undiagnosed, since diagnosed-sample
designs cannot count people never diagnosed. No source establishes a hormonal mechanism, only a preliminary
association.
