# ADHD and substance use

**Module.** M08 ADHD · **Topic.** [[ADHD and substance use]]

## Why this matters

Whether treating childhood ADHD raises or lowers later substance risk has been argued for decades. It is also a
clean case study in how different observational designs each answer a different piece of a confounding problem
that no single study can solve alone.

## The core model

**Childhood ADHD predicts later substance problems.** A meta-analysis of prospective studies tracking children
into adulthood found those with ADHD more likely to try nicotine and other substances, and more likely to
develop abuse or dependence across several substances. Too few studies separated ADHD from co-occurring conduct
problems, so this comorbidity clouds any claim about ADHD's own contribution, and a prospective design fixes the
order of events without fixing the cause.
[[P21382538 Lee 2011 Childhood ADHD and later substance use#^p21382538-use|Lee 2011]]
[[P21382538 Lee 2011 Childhood ADHD and later substance use#^p21382538-dbd|Lee 2011]]
[[P21382538 Lee 2011 Childhood ADHD and later substance use#^p21382538-caution-confounding|Appraisal: Lee 2011]]

**Where the treatment worry comes from.** The concern about stimulant treatment draws on the reward account of
addiction, in which addictive drugs flood reward circuitry with dopamine far beyond natural rewards and reshape
circuits for reward, stress and self-control.
[[F87 NIDA Drugs and the brain#^f87-reward|NIDA: Drugs and the brain]]
A long-running controversy asked whether childhood stimulant treatment raises later substance risk, after
earlier reports had suggested the opposite - that it protects.
[[P23754458 Humphreys 2013 Stimulant medication and substance outcomes#^p23754458-question|Humphreys 2013]]

**Between-person evidence: no increase, no protection.** Across 15 longitudinal studies covering 2,565
participants, children with and without a stimulant history had comparable later rates of use, abuse and
dependence; treated and untreated children differ in severity and resources, so similar outcomes could still
hide offsetting effects.
[[P23754458 Humphreys 2013 Stimulant medication and substance outcomes#^p23754458-null|Humphreys 2013]]
[[P23754458 Humphreys 2013 Stimulant medication and substance outcomes#^p23754458-caution-comparison|Appraisal: Humphreys 2013]]

**Registry evidence.** In United States claims data for nearly 3 million people with ADHD, the same people had
lower odds of a substance-related emergency visit during medicated than unmedicated months. Comparing people with
themselves removes stable confounders but not changing circumstances, such as a crisis, that could affect both.
[[P28659039 Quinn 2017 ADHD medication and substance-related problems#^p28659039-concurrent|Quinn 2017]]
[[P28659039 Quinn 2017 ADHD medication and substance-related problems#^p28659039-caution-within|Appraisal: Quinn 2017]]
A Swedish cohort emulating a trial of early medication start found lower two-year mortality, driven by unnatural
causes including accidental poisoning - a design that makes the causal question explicit but still cannot
remove confounding by indication.
[[P38470385 Li 2024 ADHD medication and mortality#^p38470385-causes|Li 2024]]
[[P38470385 Li 2024 ADHD medication and mortality#^p38470385-caution-confounding|Appraisal: Li 2024]]

**What randomised evidence does not cover.** Randomised trials of ADHD medication are short, with insufficient
data even at 26 and 52 weeks, so long-term substance outcomes come only from observational designs, each open to
a different confound.

**Reading rule.** Ask whether a claim concerns risk, treatment, or mechanism, and which design produced it -
prospective cohorts, between-person, within-person and emulated-trial comparisons each remove different
confounders, and none is a randomised long-term test.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A hypothetical headline reads: "New Study Proves ADHD Medication
Prevents Addiction."

Ask what would actually prove that: a randomised trial following groups for years, which does not exist here.
Look instead at the real pattern: the between-person comparison found no difference either direction; the
within-person and emulated-trial analyses found lower odds of substance-related problems around treated periods,
compatible with a protective effect but also with confounding by indication or a crisis changing both behaviours
together.

The defensible rewrite: the evidence argues against treatment raising later substance risk, and some designs
find lower odds around treated periods; none of it proves prevention, since no randomised long-term trial has
tested it.

## Common confusions

- "Stimulant treatment causes future addiction." The between-person meta-analysis found no increase in risk.
- "This settles it - medication prevents addiction." Registry designs reduce some confounding but not all, and
  no randomised long-term trial exists.
- "ADHD causes substance use disorders on its own." Co-occurring conduct problems and shared liability cloud
  how much ADHD contributes alone.

## What the sources do not establish

No source here settles how much of the ADHD-substance association is causal versus explained by conduct
problems or shared liability, or whether reduced risk during treatment persists after it stops or holds equally
for women.

## Check yourself

1. What did the between-person meta-analysis of 15 longitudinal studies conclude about stimulant treatment and
   later substance use?
2. What confounder does a within-person comparison remove, and what can it not remove?
3. Why can randomised trials not answer the long-term substance-use question in this literature?

## Answer notes

1. Treated and untreated children had comparable later rates of use, abuse and dependence, so the authors
   concluded treatment neither protects against nor adds to that risk.
2. It removes confounders stable within a person, but not changing circumstances, such as a crisis, that could
   affect both medication use and substance use together.
3. Randomised trials run for weeks, with insufficient data even at 26 and 52 weeks, far short of the years
   needed to observe substance outcomes.
