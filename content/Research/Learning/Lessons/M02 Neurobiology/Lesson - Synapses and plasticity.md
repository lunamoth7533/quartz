# Synapses and plasticity

**Module.** M02 Neurobiology · **Topic.** [[Synapses and plasticity]]

## Why this matters

The synapse is where the nervous system changes. It is also where popular explanations of learning, memory and
medication do most of their hand-waving. This lesson establishes what is actually established here, and it
hands the long-term plasticity mechanisms to the next lesson.

## The core model

There are two kinds of connection between electrically active cells: chemical synapses, where neurotransmitter
carries the signal, and electrical synapses formed by gap junctions, which are much less common and much faster.
[[F04 OpenStax Communication between neurons#^f04-types|Communication Between Neurons]];
[[F06 OpenStax Cells of the nervous system#^f06-electrical|Cells of the Nervous System]]

At a chemical synapse, the action potential opens voltage-gated calcium channels, vesicles fuse with the
presynaptic membrane, transmitter is released, and it is cleared by degradation or by neuronal and glial
reuptake.
[[F04 OpenStax Communication between neurons#^f04-release|Communication Between Neurons]]

The postsynaptic effect depends on the receptor rather than the transmitter alone: glutamate receptors
depolarise adult cells while GABA and glycine receptors hyperpolarise them, and some receptor subtypes do the
opposite depending on context.
[[F04 OpenStax Communication between neurons#^f04-receptor|Communication Between Neurons]]

Metabotropic receptors act through second messengers that can modify ion channels and gene transcription, which
the textbook links cautiously to stronger connections and possibly to learning and memory.
[[F04 OpenStax Communication between neurons#^f04-metabotropic|Communication Between Neurons]]

That cautious link is exactly the boundary this lesson enforces: the textbook gives the machinery and a
possibility, not a theory of human learning. The next lesson takes up the specific long-term plasticity
mechanisms, where a dedicated source exists.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A popular article says "learning strengthens the synapses involved, so
the pathway gets faster".

Audit each step. Which synapses, identified how? Strengthening means a change in postsynaptic response to the
same presynaptic activity, sustained over what time course? Faster conduction is a property of myelinated
fibres, not of synaptic strength; a strengthened synapse changes the probability or size of a response, not
conduction velocity. And the observation that a pathway is involved in a task does not show that its synapses
were strengthened by learning; that needs the plasticity mechanisms in the next lesson.

The revised sentence: "In animal preparations, specific patterns of activity can durably change the response
of particular synapses; how such changes map onto human learning is the subject of the next lesson."

## Common confusions

- "Plasticity means improvement." Potentiation and depression are both plasticity; direction depends on the
  activity pattern.
- "Strengthening means faster." It means a larger or more reliable response to the same input.
- "More transmitter means more effect." Reuptake, receptor type and downstream state all intervene.

## What the sources do not establish

The textbook pages give a mechanism sketch and do not provide evidence about human learning; there is no
measurement here of synaptic change in a person. The topic note for this lesson records that long-term
potentiation and depression are taught separately, with their own verified source.

2. Why does the same transmitter produce different effects in different cells?

## Answer notes

1. Chemical synapses, which use transmitter and are slower and more flexible, and electrical synapses through
   gap junctions, which are faster and much less common.
2. Because the postsynaptic effect depends on receptor type and subtype, not on the transmitter alone.
3. Strengthening changes the size or reliability of the postsynaptic response to the same input; faster
   conduction is a property of the axon's insulation and geometry.
