# Myelin and saltatory conduction

**Module.** M02 Neurobiology · **Topic.** [[Myelin and saltatory conduction]]

## Why this matters

Multiple sclerosis and other myelin-related conditions are common reference points, and imaging reports often
describe "reduced myelin signal." Reading such claims well means knowing what myelin buys, so a change in
conduction is not mistaken for a change in connection count.

## The core model

Myelin is a multi-layered wrap that glial cells - oligodendrocytes centrally, Schwann cells peripherally - build
around an axon, segmenting it into insulated internodes separated by bare nodes of Ranvier.
[[F02 OpenStax Nervous tissue#^f02-myelin|Nervous Tissue]]

The insulation raises membrane resistance and lowers capacitance along each internode, reducing current leakage,
so the local current from a spike travels further before needing regeneration. Regeneration happens only at the
nodes, where sodium channels cluster, so the spike is rebuilt node by node rather than continuously - the
appearance of jumping that gives saltatory conduction its name.
[[F02 OpenStax Nervous tissue#^f02-myelin|Nervous Tissue]]
[[F03 OpenStax The action potential#^f03-signals|The Action Potential]]

Speed is not free: conduction velocity also rises with axon diameter, so the alternative to myelin is simply a
much thicker axon. Myelin buys comparable speed far more compactly and more cheaply metabolically, at the cost of
more components that can fail and a longer developmental timeline.
[[F02 OpenStax Nervous tissue#^f02-myelin|Nervous Tissue]]

Because conduction delay depends on both distance and myelination, myelin does not just make signals faster in
general - it sets their relative arrival times, so two pathways of different length can be tuned to arrive
together. Myelin shapes timing relationships more than it simply accelerates everything equally, and it continues
developing well past infancy, with association tracts maturing later than primary pathways.
[[F02 OpenStax Nervous tissue#^f02-myelin|Nervous Tissue]]
[[P19794405 Tau 2010 Normal development of brain circuits#^p19794405-timing|Normal development of brain circuits]]

Multiple sclerosis shows what happens when this system is attacked: an autoimmune process damages central myelin,
and demyelination slows or blocks conduction without changing the message an axon carries. The disease typically
begins relapsing-remitting and may become progressive, and diagnosis combines history, examination, imaging and
laboratory findings, because imaging changes resembling the disease can occur without it.
[[F75 MedlinePlus Multiple sclerosis#^f75-mechanism|Multiple Sclerosis]]
[[F75 MedlinePlus Multiple sclerosis#^f75-diagnosis|Multiple Sclerosis]]

The reading rule: conduction velocity and "connectivity" are different quantities. A myelinated pathway can be
fast and sparse; a slow, unmyelinated one can carry precise timing over short distances.
[[F75 MedlinePlus Multiple sclerosis#^f75-limit|Multiple Sclerosis]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A fictional imaging report states: "Reduced myelin signal was found in
tract X, indicating fewer connections in this pathway." Myelin loss changes conduction speed and reliability
along existing axons; it does not, by itself, mean axons are missing. A tract could have the same number of
fibres as before, each conducting more slowly because of thinner or damaged myelin.

The defensible rewrite: "reduced myelin signal in tract X is consistent with slower or less reliable conduction;
it does not by itself indicate fewer connections, which would need a separate measure of axon count."

## Common confusions

- "Losing myelin means losing connections." Demyelination changes conduction speed and reliability, not axon
  count.
- "Saltatory conduction happens continuously along the membrane." Regeneration occurs only at the nodes of
  Ranvier.
- "More myelin is simply better." Myelination tunes relative timing between pathways.

## What the sources do not establish

The biophysics of saltatory conduction are established directly from single-fibre recordings. Developmental
timelines for human myelination come from imaging proxies rather than myelin itself, and why remyelination
succeeds in some lesions and fails in others remains open.

## Answer notes

1. Only at the nodes of Ranvier, where sodium channels cluster; the internodes conduct passively, so the spike
   appears to jump node to node.
2. More components that can fail, plus a longer developmental timeline than an unmyelinated fibre needs.
3. Myelin changes conduction speed and reliability along existing axons, not how many axons are present.
