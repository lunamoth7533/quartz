# Membrane transport and ion channels

**Module.** M02 Neurobiology · **Topic.** [[Membrane transport and ion channels]]

## Why this matters

Almost every psychoactive drug works by nudging a pump, a channel or a transporter. A phrase like "blocks
reuptake" or "opens a channel" means little until you know which of the three it refers to, since they behave in
genuinely different ways.

## The core model

Three machines do three jobs. Passive channels follow the electrochemical gradient at no energy cost. Carriers
move a solute with or against its gradient depending on the carrier. Active pumps spend ATP to move ions against
their gradient - creating the gradients in the first place, including the one behind the resting potential.
[[F03 OpenStax The action potential#^f03-gradient|The Action Potential]]

Channels are selective by pore structure, but gating is where the interesting biology happens. During a spike,
sodium channels open at threshold and then inactivate, while potassium channels open and close more slowly,
producing the brief undershoot after. Inactivation is a distinct state from simply closing: a channel that has
just fired cannot immediately reopen, which is the physiological basis of the refractory period, not an artefact
of measurement.
[[F03 OpenStax The action potential#^f03-threshold|The Action Potential]]
[[F03 OpenStax The action potential#^f03-signals|The Action Potential]]

Location matters as much as identity. Sodium channels cluster densely at the axon's initial segment, making that
region the trigger zone where summed input becomes an all-or-none spike. The same logic applies at a synapse,
where which receptor a transmitter binds - not the transmitter itself - decides the effect.
[[F04 OpenStax Communication between neurons#^f04-initial-segment|Communication Between Neurons]]
[[F04 OpenStax Communication between neurons#^f04-receptor|Communication Between Neurons]]

Ending a signal is a transport problem, not just a receptor problem. Reuptake transporters clear transmitter from
the cleft - largely the monoamines' job - while separate vesicular transporters reload transmitter into vesicles,
and enzymatic breakdown offers a second clearance route. A drug that blocks a transporter is having a transport
effect, not a receptor effect, and the two are frequently confused.
[[F06 OpenStax Cells of the nervous system#^f06-receptor|Cells of the Nervous System]]
[[F108 Neuroscience Online acetylcholine#^f108-inactivation|Acetylcholine transmission]]

Because most psychoactive drugs act on this same machinery, and a molecular target usually appears in more than
one tissue, effects are rarely confined to a single outcome. Reading a drug's mechanism honestly needs the target
list plus where it is expressed, not the target's name alone.

[[F13 NIGMS How do medicines work#^f13-agonism|How do medicines work]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A fictional summary claims: "Compound Z blocks sodium channels, so it
will calm an overactive brain." Sodium channels come in several subtypes, expressed differently in brain, heart
and muscle; "blocks sodium channels" alone could describe a seizure medicine, a local anaesthetic or a cardiac
risk. Blocking a channel does not automatically mean "calming" - it depends on whether affected cells need that
current to fire at all, or only to fire faster than some threshold.

The defensible statement: "Compound Z blocks a class of sodium channels; the behavioural effect depends on the
subtype, the tissues expressing it, and how the relevant circuits depend on that current."

## Common confusions

- "A pump and a channel are the same protein." A pump spends ATP against a gradient; a channel lets ions cross
  down one that exists.
- "A reuptake inhibitor acts on the receptor." It acts on the transporter that clears transmitter from the
  cleft.
- "Inactivation just means the channel closed." It is a separate state that prevents immediate reopening.

## What the sources do not establish

Channel biophysics is one of the most quantitatively established areas of neuroscience. But the behavioural
consequences of any one channel are established mainly for a handful of specific mutations; for most common
variation, an effect on behaviour is inferred rather than demonstrated.

## Check yourself

1. Name the three types of membrane machinery here and how each moves material across the membrane.
2. Why is channel inactivation not the same as a channel simply closing?
3. Why does knowing a drug's molecular target not tell you its clinical effect?

## Answer notes

1. Channels (passive, down a gradient), carriers (with or against a gradient), pumps (active, against a
   gradient).
2. Inactivation prevents immediate reopening; closing alone would allow it.
3. The same target is usually expressed in more than one tissue and subtype, so effect depends on distribution,
   not the name alone.
