# Phase-specific bipolar treatment evidence

**Module.** M07 Bipolar I · **Topic.** [[Phase-specific bipolar treatment evidence]]

## Why this matters

This is the lesson where guideline language meets evidence reading. A "first-line" recommendation looks like a
fact and is actually a judgement, and knowing what it is built from changes how you use it.

## The core model

The guideline synthesis organises recommendations by phase and ranks options first-, second- and third-line.
[[P38695002 Keramatian 2023 CANMAT and ISBD bipolar treatment guidelines#^p38695002-phases|CANMAT and ISBD bipolar treatment guidelines]]

Ranking weighs efficacy, safety and tolerability together rather than efficacy alone.
[[P38695002 Keramatian 2023 CANMAT and ISBD bipolar treatment guidelines#^p38695002-balance|CANMAT and ISBD bipolar treatment guidelines]]

The guideline's stated purpose is to help clinicians navigate a rapidly changing treatment landscape by
summarising the evidence.
[[P38695002 Keramatian 2023 CANMAT and ISBD bipolar treatment guidelines#^p38695002-balance|CANMAT and ISBD bipolar treatment guidelines]]

NIMH patient material frames treatment as combining medication and psychotherapy across the lifetime course.
[[F16 NIMH Bipolar disorder#^f16-treatment|Bipolar Disorder]]

Read a ranking as: for this phase, a panel judged that the balance of benefit, safety and tolerability places
this option in this tier, based on the studies available at the time. It is a starting point for a decision
with a person, not a property of the drug.

Phase also shapes what a trial measures. Acute-phase trials typically score symptom change over weeks against a
control, while maintenance trials ask whether episodes recur over months and often express results as time to an
event. Two studies of the same drug can therefore look inconsistent while answering different questions, and a
reader who skips the phase line can compare numbers that were never commensurable.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A summary says "Drug X is first-line for acute mania". What would make
that statement stronger or weaker for a particular person?

Strengthen it with: trials in a matching population, outcomes that matter for the phase, a clear comparator,
and consistency across guidelines. Weaken it with: population mismatch, comorbidity or co-medication that
changes the risk-benefit balance, previous response or intolerance, and a newer trial that changed the picture.

Notice that "first-line" is doing a lot of work. Behind it are efficacy estimates for a phase, safety and
tolerability judgements, and the practical realities of monitoring. The reading skill is to keep asking which
of those you have and which you are assuming.

## Common confusions

- "Guideline ranking equals evidence strength." Rankings combine evidence with judgement and practical
  considerations.
- "First-line means first choice for everyone." It means a reasonable starting point in the panel's judgement
  for that phase.
- "Psychotherapy is optional." Adjunctive psychotherapy has its own evidence, which the next lessons cover.

## What the sources do not establish

This vault's guideline record is a summary of a published summary and carries that limit in its own note; it
contains no dosing, monitoring or selection guidance. Medication decisions belong to clinical care with the
current product information.
