# Bipolar I episodes and course

**Module.** M07 Bipolar I · **Topic.** [[Bipolar I episodes and course]]

## Why this matters

Bipolar I is defined by an episode, treated by phase, and experienced as a lifetime course. Those three
framings get mixed together constantly, and mixing them produces sentences that are true of none of them.
This lesson separates them.

## The core model

Bipolar I disorder is defined by the occurrence of mania; depressive episodes are common across the lifetime
course but are not required for the diagnosis.
[[F16 NIMH Bipolar disorder#^f16-mania|Bipolar Disorder]]

Clinical care considers acute symptoms and long-term stability together, because the treatment question differs
by state: bringing an episode under control is a different problem from reducing recurrence risk.
[[F16 NIMH Bipolar disorder#^f16-course|Bipolar Disorder]]

Guideline recommendations are organised by illness phase, which is the same distinction in clinical form.
[[P38695002 Keramatian 2023 CANMAT and ISBD bipolar treatment guidelines#^p38695002-phases|CANMAT and ISBD bipolar treatment guidelines]]

The population-level caveat is important: descriptions of course are averages from studied samples, so they
describe typical patterns rather than a personal trajectory. Individual trajectories differ in episode number,
timing, severity, recovery between episodes, triggers and comorbidity.

Read every bipolar statement with the three framings in mind. A sentence about what defines the disorder answers
the episode question; a sentence about treatment answers the phase question; a sentence about prognosis answers
the course question. Summaries routinely move a phase-trial result into a course claim, or turn a course average
into a personal prediction, and tracing which framing a sentence uses is often enough to see where it overreaches.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. Someone reads that bipolar disorder "gets worse over time" and asks
what that means for them.

Separate the framings. Episode: the diagnostic criterion is the occurrence of mania, and depressive episodes
commonly occur. Phase: care is organised around the current state, and the choice of treatment differs between
acute mania, acute depression and maintenance. Course: studies describe average patterns across groups, with
wide variation, and predictors of individual trajectories are weak.

The accurate answer: "studies describe average courses with substantial variation; what is known at the group
level does not determine one person's trajectory, and the practical questions are about the current phase and
maintenance planning."

## Common confusions

- "Bipolar means mood swings." It describes episodes of mania, with or without depressive episodes, not
  day-to-day fluctuations.
- "Recovery between episodes means no need for maintenance." Maintenance decisions are about recurrence risk,
  which is a separate question from current symptoms.
- "The course is predictable." Averages are not predictions for individuals.

## What the sources do not establish

The NIMH page is general-audience material and the guideline record is a summary of a summary; neither
establishes an individual prognosis, and this vault contains no source that predicts an individual's course.
Nothing here is a treatment plan.

## Answer notes

1. The occurrence of mania; a depressive episode is common but not required.
2. Because acute states and maintenance have different goals, evidence and risks.
3. An episode statement describes the current syndrome; a course statement describes patterns across a lifetime
   in a sample.
