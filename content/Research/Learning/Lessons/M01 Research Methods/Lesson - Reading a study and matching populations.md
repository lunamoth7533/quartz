# Reading a study and matching populations

**Module.** M01 Research Methods and Evidence Literacy · **Topic.** [[Reading a study and matching populations]]

## Why this matters

A result is indexed to a population, a measurement and a time window. Almost every misuse of evidence in
mental health happens when one of those three indices is quietly dropped. This lesson is a working method for
keeping them attached.

## The core model

Start with the population. Who was recruited, how were they identified, and who was excluded? A Cochrane review
of CBT for adult ADHD rated its evidence low quality and flagged short follow-up, heterogeneous outcomes and a
geographically narrow trial base.
[[P29566425 Lopez 2018 Cognitive behavioural interventions for ADHD#^p29566425-certainty|CBT for ADHD (Cochrane review)]]
Each of those is a boundary on where the result travels.

Then the measurement. Trials differ in who rated the outcome, and clinician-reported and self-reported results
can disagree.
[[P29566425 Lopez 2018 Cognitive behavioural interventions for ADHD#^p29566425-caution-rater|AI synthesis: CBT for ADHD (Cochrane review)]]
The rater is part of the measurement, not a formality.

Then transfer. Child intervention findings do not automatically transfer to adults; the constructs may overlap
while the outcome measures, developmental context and support needs differ.
[[P31949163 Lord 2020 Autism spectrum disorder primer#^p31949163-caution-adult|AI synthesis: Autism spectrum disorder primer]]

Finally, design. A study that describes co-occurrence cannot answer a causal question no matter how precisely
the population is defined.
[[F09 Noba Research designs#^f09-designs|Research Designs]]

The practical device is a single sentence you write before judging the result: *"In [population], [measurement]
over [time window] differed by [effect]"*. If you cannot fill the slots, you do not yet have the study.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. You read that "CBT improves ADHD in adults" and you are thinking about a
43-year-old who was diagnosed last year, takes medication, and has no other psychiatric history.

Fill the slots. Population: adults with a formal ADHD diagnosis in 14 randomised trials, mostly from the
northern hemisphere, 700 participants. Measurement: clinician-rated and self-rated core symptoms, with quality
of life inconsistent. Time window: short-term benefit, limited long-term follow-up. Effect: adjunctive CBT beat
medication alone on core symptoms, with low-quality evidence.

Your person shares the diagnosis and medication status but may differ in age at diagnosis, comorbidity,
environment and what "better" means to them. So the honest sentence is not "CBT will work for them"; it is
"in similar trial populations, adding CBT to medication improved rated core symptoms on average over the short
term, and the review rated that evidence low quality".

## Common confusions

- "The average applies to everyone." A mean effect is compatible with large benefit, no benefit and harm in
  different people; averages do not distribute themselves evenly.
- "Exclusion criteria are technicalities." They define who the result is about. A trial that excluded
  substance use or bipolar disorder tells you nothing direct about those groups.
- "More recent is better." Date is not quality; a 2018 review may be the best available evidence for a
  question, and a newer preprint is not automatically stronger.

## What the sources do not establish

This vault's sources are mostly small reviews or abstract-level records; none of them provides a
population-adjusted model that would let you project a trial average onto one person. Transfer is a judgement
you make explicit, not a computation the sources support.

## Check yourself

1. Name the three indices a result must carry, and give one example of each going missing.
2. Why does the identity of the rater change how you read a symptom improvement?
3. A trial excluded people with substance use disorders. What can you conclude about that group?
4. Write the population-measurement-window sentence for one source in this vault.

## Answer notes

1. Population (who was studied), measurement (what was assessed and by whom) and time window (how long the
   observation ran).
2. Different raters have different vantage points and biases; a change on one rater's scale may not appear on
   another, so the claim is only as strong as the rater it rests on.
3. Almost nothing directly; the trial provides no data on that group, and extending the result is an assumption.
4. A good sentence names each element concretely and does not add a causal verb.
