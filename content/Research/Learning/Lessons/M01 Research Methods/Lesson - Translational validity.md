# Translational validity

**Module.** M01 Research Methods and Evidence Literacy · **Topic.** [[Translational validity]]

## Why this matters

This module has spent twelve lessons on trusting a finding in the setting it was actually studied.
This last lesson asks what comes after: does a finding from one system - an animal model, a
laboratory task, an early biomarker - support a conclusion about a different system, up to a human
clinical outcome. A well-designed, well-replicated study can still fail this test.

## The core model

Animal and cellular models are judged against three standards. Face validity asks whether the
model looks like the phenomenon. Predictive validity asks whether its response forecasts how the
real phenomenon responds to a manipulation, such as a known-effective treatment. Construct validity
- hardest and most important - asks whether the model's biology actually matches the target
mechanism. A model can score well on one and poorly on another.
[[P20877280 Nestler 2010 Animal models#^p20877280-validity|Animal models]]
[[P20877280 Nestler 2010 Animal models#^p20877280-construct|Animal models]]

Face validity has historically carried more weight than it can bear - resembling a phenomenon does
not show a model shares its mechanism or predicts a real intervention's effect; predictive validity
is the harder, more informative test. It follows that no animal model reproduces a human
psychiatric condition, and a model's worth depends on the specific question it was built to answer
- a statement about scope, not quality: a model testing whether one receptor mechanism produces
one behaviour is informative for that question alone. [[P20877280 Nestler 2010 Animal models#^p20877280-limits|Animal models]]

Two separate translations are usually bundled together. Species translation assumes a mechanism is
conserved across evolutionary lines; setting translation assumes a laboratory task captures the
real-world phenomenon it stands for. A finding can cross one line and fail the other. Translation
therefore tends to fail in stages: a mechanism shown in an isolated preparation must hold in the
intact animal, then in humans, then in the affected population, then in the outcome that matters -
and failure at one stage does not undo an earlier one. Fear extinction is a clear example: the
mechanism is well characterised and human evidence keeps accumulating, while full clinical
translation stays incomplete.
[[P22129456 Milad 2012 Fear extinction#^p22129456-translation|Fear extinction]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A laboratory finding shows a candidate compound reduces a
fear-related behaviour in an animal model, and a press release calls it "a breakthrough for
anxiety." Walk the stages: does the behaviour share the human mechanism (construct validity) or
merely resemble it (face validity)? Does the model show predictive validity against treatments
already known to help humans? And even clearing both, does the effect survive the jump into human
biology, the affected population, and an outcome that matters, such as sustained function rather
than one lab measure?

The honest headline is narrower: in this model, the compound reduced one fear-related behaviour;
whether the mechanism is shared with human anxiety, and whether any human benefit follows, remain
open.

## Common confusions

- "If it works in animals, it will work in people." Species and setting translation are separate
  assumptions that can each fail independently.
- "A model that looks like the human condition measures the same mechanism." Face and construct
  validity are different standards.
- "A translation failure means the original mechanism was wrong." A mechanism can be well
  established at one stage and still fail to translate at a later one for unrelated reasons.

## What the sources do not establish

Predictive validity is often assessed only after the fact, weakening exactly the evidence that
would be most informative. Nothing here tells you which stage of translation a specific mechanism
elsewhere in this vault has actually reached; that has to be checked claim by claim.

## Check yourself

1. Name the three kinds of validity used to judge an animal or cellular model, and which one is
   considered hardest to establish.
2. Why is face validity alone considered weak evidence for a translation claim?
3. What is the difference between species translation and setting translation?
4. Why can a well-established mechanism still fail to produce a human clinical benefit without the
   original mechanism being wrong?

## Answer notes

1. Face, predictive and construct validity; construct validity is considered hardest and most
   important.
2. Resembling the target phenomenon does not show the same mechanism is at work or that the model
   predicts a real intervention's effect.
3. Species translation assumes conservation across species; setting translation assumes a
   laboratory task captures the real-world phenomenon.
4. Translation happens in stages, and failure at a later stage does not undo what was correctly
   shown at an earlier one.
