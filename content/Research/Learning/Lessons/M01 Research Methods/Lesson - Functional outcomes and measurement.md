# Functional outcomes and measurement

**Module.** M01 Research Methods and Evidence Literacy · **Topic.** [[Functional outcomes and measurement]]

## Why this matters

Trials usually measure symptoms because symptoms are easy to count. People usually care about functioning:
work, study, relationships, self-care and participation. The two do not move together automatically, and
knowing how functioning is defined and measured changes how you read every treatment claim.

## The core model

The WHO framework for functioning describes health and disability through body functions, activities,
participation and environmental factors, measured at individual and population levels.
[[F23 WHO International Classification of Functioning#^f23-framework|International Classification of Functioning]]

The WHO Disability Assessment Schedule (WHODAS 2.0) operationalises that framework with six domains: cognition,
mobility, self-care, getting along, life activities, and participation.
[[F23 WHO International Classification of Functioning#^f23-domains|International Classification of Functioning]]

That instrument is documented for cross-cultural use with reliability and validity field testing, which is
what makes its scores interpretable in the first place.
[[F23 WHO International Classification of Functioning#^f23-instrument|International Classification of Functioning]]
But a functioning score summarises reported activity and participation, not symptom severity or capacity.
[[F23 WHO International Classification of Functioning#^f23-caution-impairment|AI synthesis: International Classification of Functioning]]

Clinical evidence makes the distinction concrete. ICD-11 complex PTSD identifies a group with greater
functional impairment than PTSD alone, which is part of why the construct matters.
[[P29029837 Brewin 2017 ICD-11 PTSD proposals review#^p29029837-impairment|ICD-11 PTSD and complex PTSD proposals]]

And in adult ADHD, a large synthesis of 113 randomised trials found that medications were not efficacious on
additional outcomes such as quality of life, even while improving core symptoms at 12 weeks.
[[P39701638 Ostinelli 2025 Comparative interventions for ADHD#^p39701638-longterm|Comparative efficacy of ADHD interventions in adults]]
That is the whole lesson in one comparison: a symptom improvement is not automatically a functioning
improvement.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A 12-week trial of a treatment reports a medium improvement on a
symptom scale and no significant change on a functioning measure. The authors write that the treatment
"improved outcomes".

Rewrite it. Which outcome improved, on whose rating, over what window? If the symptom scale improved and the
functioning measure did not, the accurate summary is a symptom improvement with no demonstrated functional
change. Then ask which explanation fits: the trial may be too short for functional change, the functioning
measure may be insensitive or poorly matched to the population, or the treatment may genuinely affect symptoms
without touching daily life.

The decision-relevant question is not "did the number move" but "which number moved, and is it the one that
matters for this decision".

## Common confusions

- "Functional improvement follows symptom improvement." Sometimes, over some intervals, for some measures; the
  ADHD synthesis is a counterexample for quality of life.
- "Quality of life is a soft outcome." It is a different, harder-to-measure outcome; softness is not the issue,
  sensitivity and validity are.
- "A functioning score measures capacity." It measures reported activity and participation in context.

## What the sources do not establish

This vault has no study that validates a functioning measure for every condition it discusses, and no source
that establishes the right follow-up interval for functional change. Where the vault's clinical sources report
symptom outcomes only, the lessons say so and the coverage matrix records the gap.

## Check yourself

1. Name the six WHODAS 2.0 domains in your own words.
2. Why is a functioning score not a symptom score?
3. What is the correct summary when symptoms improve and functioning does not?
4. Which outcome would you require before calling a treatment clinically meaningful?

## Answer notes

1. Thinking and communicating; moving around; self-care; getting along with people; life activities such as
   work or household tasks; and participation in community life.
2. Because it records activity and participation in context, not the presence or severity of symptoms or the
   person's underlying capacity.
3. A symptom improvement with no demonstrated functional benefit, and an explicit statement of that limit.
4. A fair answer names an outcome tied to the decision (functioning, participation, quality of life or harm) in
   addition to symptoms, with its measure and time window.
