# Reviews, guidelines and preprints

**Module.** M01 Research Methods and Evidence Literacy · **Topic.** [[Reviews, guidelines and preprints]]

## Why this matters

Reviews, consensus statements and guidelines are how most people meet evidence, and they are also where
errors propagate fastest, because a summary is quoted far more often than the studies under it. This lesson
gives you a way to use them without surrendering judgement.

## The core model

A review is a layer, not a source of truth. The ADHD consensus statement curates 208 assertions using stated
inclusion rules, which makes it efficient and also means the rules, not the assertions, are what you audit.
[[P33549739 Faraone 2021 World Federation ADHD consensus statement#^p33549739-methods|World Federation ADHD consensus statement]]

Guidelines translate evidence into recommendations, and the translation adds judgement. The CANMAT/ISBD
summary organises recommendations by illness phase and ranks options first-, second- or third-line, weighing
efficacy, safety and tolerability together.
[[P38695002 Keramatian 2023 CANMAT and ISBD bipolar treatment guidelines#^p38695002-phases|CANMAT and ISBD bipolar treatment guidelines]]
That ranking is a professional judgement applied to a changing literature, not a measurement.

Cochrane methods make the layer explicit: reviews are expected to state certainty for each important outcome
and to avoid healthcare recommendations, while still describing actions consistent with particular values and
preferences.
[[F22 Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions#^f22-conclusions|Cochrane Handbook]]

Consensus statements in particular reflect judgement about a literature that keeps changing. When a treatment
question matters, check whether a newer primary study or a newer synthesis has appeared after the consensus.
[[P33549739 Faraone 2021 World Federation ADHD consensus statement#^p33549739-caution-consensus|AI synthesis: World Federation ADHD consensus statement]]

Preprints add a timing problem: they may be the newest thing available and they have not completed peer review.
Treat a preprint as a claim to check against its data, not as a settled result.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A guideline summary recommends an option as "first-line" for acute
mania, and you want to know what stands behind it.

Trace the type. A first-line ranking in a guideline summary usually rests on randomised trials for efficacy,
plus safety and tolerability data that may come from different designs, assembled by a guideline panel applying
explicit thresholds. The primary verification would be the randomised trials in the relevant phase and
population, and the panel's own description of its methods.

Now write what the guideline does not give you: the size of the benefit for the population in front of you, the
confidence in the comparison against the second-line option, or an individual recommendation. Those require the
trials, the population match, and clinical judgement.

## Common confusions

- "Guidelines are evidence." They are judgements about evidence, made by panels with methods and interests.
- "Peer review means correct." Peer review filters some errors; it does not replicate findings or fix bias.
- "Consensus means agreement about facts." Consensus documents can agree on what the literature currently
  supports while disagreeing about what to recommend.

## What the sources do not establish

This vault's guideline record is a summary of a summary and carries that limit in its own note. Nothing here
establishes how current any guideline is today, and no source in the vault measures how often recommendations
change after publication.

## Check yourself

1. What should you read first in a consensus statement: the assertions or the inclusion rules? Why?
2. Why does Cochrane guidance tell review authors to avoid recommendations while still describing actions?
3. A guideline recommends an option "first-line". Name two things you still do not know.
4. How would you handle a preprint that reports a striking result?

## Answer notes

1. The inclusion rules: they determine which studies could produce the assertions and how strong each one can be.
2. Because recommendations depend on values, preferences, cost and context that lie outside the evidence; the
   review can describe the balance without prescribing a decision.
3. A fair answer names the size of benefit for a specific population and the strength of the comparison with
   alternatives, among others.
4. Check the data and methods available, look for the peer-reviewed version, and record it as a claim under
   review rather than a finding.
