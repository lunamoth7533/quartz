# Qualitative designs

**Module.** M01 Research Methods and Evidence Literacy · **Topic.** [[Qualitative designs]]

## Why this matters

Every design covered so far answers some version of "how much" or "whether." Qualitative research
exists because some of the most useful questions here are "how" and "why." Judging a qualitative
study by the standards built for a trial is not rigour; it is asking the wrong question of the
wrong tool.

## The core model

Qualitative designs sample cases on purpose rather than at random - a typical case, an extreme
case, or a mix chosen for variation - because the goal is to characterise a process or meaning, not
estimate a number for a wider population. That is conceptual inference, not statistical, which is
why a qualitative finding cannot be handed a margin of error the way a survey result can.
[[Functional outcomes and measurement]]

Because the task differs, the criteria for judging quality differ too, and there is genuine
disagreement about what those criteria should be, including whether ideas built for statistical
sampling, such as generalisability, transfer to qualitative work at all.
[[P18626033 Qualitative research criteria#^p18626033-controversy|Qualitative research criteria]]
What most accounts converge on instead is transparency: whether question, method and analysis fit
together, whether the researcher's own position is made visible, and whether another reader could
follow the trail from material to conclusion.
[[P18626033 Qualitative research criteria#^p18626033-criteria|Qualitative research criteria]] A
credible account is judged by how thickly it describes what was found, not by a p-value.
[[P18626033 Qualitative research criteria#^p18626033-reading|Qualitative research criteria]]

This is also where some outcome measures originate: a qualitative study can surface what actually
matters to the people studied, which then becomes content a later quantitative scale is built to
capture.
[[P29942800 Boateng 2018 Developing and validating scales#^p29942800-content|Developing and validating scales]]
What it does not do is estimate a prevalence, test whether an intervention works, or license the
conclusion that findings hold beyond the group studied.
[[P18626033 Qualitative research criteria#^p18626033-limit|Qualitative research criteria]]
Mixed-methods work uses both families side by side for this reason: a trial shows an average
effect occurred, an interview study helps explain why, but neither checks the other's numbers.
[[P18626033 Qualitative research criteria#^p18626033-reading|Qualitative research criteria]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A study interviews fifteen adults about starting a new
coping strategy and reports three recurring themes: initial scepticism, a turning point tied to a
specific event, and a change in how they described their own capability afterward.

It cannot tell you what fraction of people who try the strategy experience these themes, because
fifteen purposively selected interviewees were not sampled to represent a wider population, and it
cannot tell you the strategy caused the change, since there is no comparison group or manipulation.
What it can do is describe a process in the participants' own terms and generate a specific,
testable hypothesis for later quantitative work - not establish that the pattern is common or that
the strategy caused it.

## Common confusions

- "Qualitative research is just anecdote." Systematic qualitative work has its own methods for
  sampling, analysis and checking.
- "A bigger qualitative sample makes it more generalisable." Sample size serves depth and
  saturation of themes, not statistical representativeness.
- "Qualitative findings need to be confirmed by a trial to count." The two answer different
  questions; neither validates the other's numbers.

## What the sources do not establish

The methodological literature documents ongoing disagreement about evaluation criteria rather than
one settled standard, so different studies here may be judged by different rules. Nothing here
tells you that a specific finding transfers beyond the people and setting it was drawn from; that
is always an argument, never an automatic property of the design.

## Check yourself

1. Why is qualitative sampling described as purposive rather than random?
2. What does "generalisability" mean for a qualitative study, if anything?
3. Name two things a qualitative study cannot establish on its own.
4. How do qualitative and quantitative findings typically combine in a mixed-methods study?

## Answer notes

1. Cases are chosen to be information-rich for the process or meaning under study, not to
   represent a population.
2. Transferability is an argument a reader makes from thick description, not a statistical
   guarantee.
3. Prevalence in a wider population, and whether an intervention causes an effect.
4. The quantitative strand estimates an effect or rate; the qualitative strand explains or
   contextualises it.
