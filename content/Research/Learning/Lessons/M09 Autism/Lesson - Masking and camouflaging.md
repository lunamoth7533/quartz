# Masking and camouflaging

**Module.** M09 Autism · **Topic.** [[Masking and camouflaging]]

## Why this matters

Many autistic people learn, deliberately or automatically, to hide or manage the features that would otherwise mark them as autistic in a social situation. That shapes both what daily life costs an autistic person and what a clinician or researcher can see in a single meeting. Read assessment guidance or camouflaging research without knowing this, and a calm, socially fluent clinic visit can look like evidence against autism when it may really be evidence of effort.

## The core model

Masking or camouflaging covers strategies used to manage or hide autistic features socially, ranging from a deliberate, rehearsed performance to a habitual, almost automatic compensation. The foundational qualitative study analysed accounts from 92 autistic adults and found several motivations, most commonly fitting in and connecting with others, and found that camouflaging blends distinct masking and compensation techniques rather than being one behaviour.
[[P28527095 Hull 2017 Social camouflaging#^p28527095-method|Social camouflaging]] [[P28527095 Hull 2017 Social camouflaging#^p28527095-strategies|Social camouflaging]]

Participants described camouflaging as demanding, linking it to exhaustion and a reduced sense of self - language that treats it as a substantial burden, not a harmless social skill.
[[P28527095 Hull 2017 Social camouflaging#^p28527095-consequence|Social camouflaging]]

This is why camouflaging complicates assessment. Adult guidance expects assessment to be considered when autistic features come with functional difficulties, and a screening score above threshold routes someone to a full, team-based assessment rather than straight to a diagnosis. Because features can be concealed in a clinical setting, that fuller assessment leans on informant history and behaviour across contexts rather than a single observation.
[[F24 NICE CG142 autism in adults diagnosis and management#^f24-identification|Autism in adults: diagnosis and management]] [[F24 NICE CG142 autism in adults diagnosis and management#^f24-screening|Autism in adults: diagnosis and management]] [[F24 NICE CG142 autism in adults diagnosis and management#^f24-comprehensive|Autism in adults: diagnosis and management]]

A screening score can produce false positives and false negatives, so it neither confirms nor rules out autism - it only decides who gets a closer look.
[[F24 NICE CG142 autism in adults diagnosis and management#^f24-caution|Appraisal: Autism in adults: diagnosis and management]]

A 2025 systematic review integrating studies across ages found camouflaging clusters into compensation, masking and assimilation, done for both self-protection and connection, and found females more likely to camouflage than males. Its designs are largely cross-sectional, so although camouflaging is associated with later identification and mental health burden, which direction causes which is unresolved.
[[P39370528 Klein 2025 Social camouflaging systematic review#^p39370528-motives|Klein 2025]] [[P39370528 Klein 2025 Social camouflaging systematic review#^p39370528-gender|Klein 2025]] [[P39370528 Klein 2025 Social camouflaging systematic review#^p39370528-direction|Klein 2025]] [[P39370528 Klein 2025 Social camouflaging systematic review#^p39370528-caution-direction|Appraisal: Klein 2025]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A clinician meets a new client for a single fifty-minute intake interview. The client makes steady eye contact, answers fluently, and seems at ease. The clinician writes "no evident autistic traits" and rules autism unlikely.

Trace what that conclusion assumes: that the interview captured typical presentation, that rehearsal did not change what was visible, and that one formal hour represents daily functioning. Each assumption is exactly what camouflaging research puts in doubt, since participants describe camouflaging as most effortful in high-stakes situations like a clinical interview.

A better-supported conclusion gathers history from people who see the client in unscripted settings, asks directly about post-social exhaustion, and treats a fluent interview as inconclusive rather than reassuring. None of this proves the client is autistic; it only shows a single fluent conversation cannot rule it out.

## Common confusions

- "Masking means someone is being dishonest." It is a coping strategy aimed at fitting in or staying safe, not deceit.
- "Hiding traits well means needing less support." Effortful concealment is reported as costly in itself, not evidence of lower need.
- "Camouflaging causes the anxiety and exhaustion researchers see in autistic adults." The association is real, but cross-sectional designs cannot establish which comes first.

## What the sources do not establish

The foundational study is a qualitative account of self-reported experience: it documents what participants describe and does not establish how common camouflaging is or that it causes later distress. The 2025 synthesis does not report pooled effect sizes, and its cross-sectional base cannot settle the direction between camouflaging and mental health. Guidance on adapting assessment is a design principle, not a tested claim about any one accommodation.
