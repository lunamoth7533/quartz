# Predictive processing accounts of autism

**Module.** M09 Autism · **Topic.** [[Predictive processing accounts of autism]]

## Why this matters

Predictive processing accounts try to explain autistic perception at the level of computation: how strongly prior expectations are weighted against incoming sensory evidence. A single imbalance could in principle explain sensory, social and behavioural features at once, which is why the accounts are ambitious - and why they need careful reading, since a framework broad enough to explain everything risks explaining nothing in particular.

## The core model

The first version proposed weaker priors, or "hypo-priors": perception follows the incoming sensory signal more closely and prior experience shapes it less, offered as an explanation for many sensory and other non-social autistic features.
[[P22959875 Pellicano 2012 Bayesian explanation of autistic perception#^p22959875-hypopriors|Pellicano 2012]]

A reply relocated the difference to precision - the weight given to sensory evidence relative to prior beliefs - arguing a range of behavioural and neurobiological findings fit better as a precision imbalance than as simply weaker priors.
[[P24860482 Lawson 2014 Aberrant precision account of autism#^p24860482-precision|Lawson 2014]]

These are not interchangeable theories that happen to share vocabulary: predictive-coding algorithms differ in their generative models and optimisation methods, so evidence for one version does not automatically support another, and a study needs a design that can tell them apart before it can favour either one.

A systematic review of the field's first decade of experiments found a slight majority of studies showing no group difference in prior integration, frequent low statistical power, and inconsistent methods; reduced prior influence showed up more for priors learned during an experiment than for priors held beforehand.
[[P36581168 Angeletos Chrysaitis 2023 Bayesian theories of autism review#^p36581168-mixed|Angeletos Chrysaitis 2023]] [[P36581168 Angeletos Chrysaitis 2023 Bayesian theories of autism review#^p36581168-methods|Angeletos Chrysaitis 2023]] [[P36581168 Angeletos Chrysaitis 2023 Bayesian theories of autism review#^p36581168-learned|Angeletos Chrysaitis 2023]]

A newer meta-analysis pooling twenty-three studies found the predicted difference on average, but with large unexplained heterogeneity no tested moderator could account for - the authors judge support limited rather than confirmatory, and point toward more complex, hierarchical models.
[[P40576893 Cui 2026 Simple Bayesian model of autism meta-analysis#^p40576893-effect|Cui 2026]] [[P40576893 Cui 2026 Simple Bayesian model of autism meta-analysis#^p40576893-heterogeneity|Cui 2026]]

The accounts therefore remain a family of live hypotheses rather than an established mechanism. The reading rule that follows: before counting a finding for or against "the" predictive account, name which version it tests, what kind of prior is involved - explicit, learned during the task, or long-standing - and which model term the design can actually isolate.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A press release announces: "New brain study proves autistic people have weaker priors, explaining all autistic traits." Apply the reading rule. Which version did the study test - hypo-priors, aberrant precision, or something else - since "weaker priors" is only one of several live candidates? Could the design distinguish a prior-strength difference from a sensory-precision difference, given the two often predict the same behaviour; if not, the finding cannot single one out. Was the prior explicit, learned during the task, or long-standing, since these behave differently in the review evidence? A more accurate headline names the version tested and notes that the field's larger reviews find mixed, heterogeneous results.

## Common confusions

- "Predictive coding is one theory, so one disconfirming study undermines the whole idea." The label covers several distinct algorithms; a result against one version does not touch the others.
- "A meta-analysis finding an average effect confirms the theory." The 2026 meta-analysis found a small pooled effect with large, unexplained heterogeneity - a sign something is unmodelled, not a settled confirmation.
- "Autistic perception simply ignores context and expectations." Evidence points more to differences in how priors are learned or updated in volatile situations, not a general absence of prior influence.

## What the sources do not establish

None of the sources here confirms any single predictive-processing version over the others, and the systematic review reports frequent low statistical power across the field's first decade. Whether laboratory measures of prior weighting connect to the everyday sensory experiences autistic people report is also not established by any study cited here.
