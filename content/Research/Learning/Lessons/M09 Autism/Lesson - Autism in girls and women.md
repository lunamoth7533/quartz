# Autism in girls and women

**Module.** M09 Autism · **Topic.** [[Autism in girls and women]]

## Why this matters

Autism has long been recorded far more often in males than females, and that ratio has shrunk as diagnosis reaches older ages. Untangling why matters because three explanations compete - fewer autistic females, a different presentation, or assessment that misses them - and each implies a different fix. This lesson gives you a way to tell which explanation a piece of evidence supports.

## The core model

Register data show the ratio is not fixed. In a Swedish cohort of about 2.76 million births followed to 2022, autism incidence peaked at ages ten to fourteen in males but not until fifteen to nineteen in females, and the male-to-female ratio fell steadily with later age at diagnosis and across calendar time, reaching 1.2 by age twenty in 2022, with parity projected soon after.
[[P41638711 Fyfe 2026 Male to female ratio in autism incidence#^p41638711-incidence|Fyfe 2026]] [[P41638711 Fyfe 2026 Male to female ratio in autism incidence#^p41638711-ratio|Fyfe 2026]]

That falling ratio is genuinely ambiguous on its own: a ratio built from recorded diagnoses mixes any true prevalence difference with differences in who is referred and recognised, so it cannot by itself decide between the explanations.
[[P41638711 Fyfe 2026 Male to female ratio in autism incidence#^p41638711-caution-recognition|Appraisal: Fyfe 2026]]

Two further findings bear on "assessment that misses them" specifically. Standard clinical measures may be tuned to a male presentation: across sixty-seven studies, autistic males scored as more affected on standard measures of core features and social interaction, while autistic females showed more cognitive and behavioural difficulties on other measures - read by the authors as bias built into the clinical procedures. A lower female score on an instrument built mainly from male samples cannot be read as milder autism until it is shown to measure both sexes the same way, a check called measurement invariance that is rarely reported for these tools.
[[P38285291 Cruz 2025 Male bias in autism diagnosis#^p38285291-phenotype|Cruz 2025]] [[P38285291 Cruz 2025 Male bias in autism diagnosis#^p38285291-conclusion|Cruz 2025]] [[P38285291 Cruz 2025 Male bias in autism diagnosis#^p38285291-caution-instruments|Appraisal: Cruz 2025]]

Other diagnoses often arrive first. Among 72,331 autistic people in Swedish registers, 54.2% of females and 40.9% of males had a psychiatric diagnosis, most often ADHD, anxiety or depression, before autism was recognised, and females with an earlier diagnosis reached their autism diagnosis later than males with the same earlier condition. Not every earlier diagnosis was necessarily a mistake - a lapsed one could reflect a real condition that resolved - but the pattern as a whole is a documented pathway: females travel through more diagnoses, over more years, before autism is recognised.
[[P40017336 Martini 2025 Psychiatric diagnoses preceding autism by sex#^p40017336-preceding|Martini 2025]] [[P40017336 Martini 2025 Psychiatric diagnoses preceding autism by sex#^p40017336-sex|Martini 2025]] [[P40017336 Martini 2025 Psychiatric diagnoses preceding autism by sex#^p40017336-caution-misdiagnosis|Appraisal: Martini 2025]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A summary claims: "Women are naturally less autistic than men, which is why fewer are diagnosed." If lower underlying severity were the whole story, instruments should show females scoring lower across the board with no instrument-specific pattern, and females should not carry more other diagnoses first or be diagnosed later once they do. Instead the evidence shows an instrument-specific pattern resembling measurement bias, a documented pathway through other diagnoses, and a shrinking ratio over time - none expected if severity alone explained the gap. The better-supported summary: recognition, not just presentation, is doing real work here.

## Common confusions

- "The falling sex ratio proves autism was always equally common in both sexes." A ratio of recorded diagnoses cannot separate true prevalence from recognition; it is evidence, not proof, for either explanation.
- "A lower score on a standard instrument means milder autism." It can also mean the instrument was not built or tested to measure that group accurately.
- "An earlier diagnosis of anxiety or depression before autism was a misdiagnosis." Some earlier diagnoses are genuine co-occurring conditions; the register data show a pathway, not a verdict on each case.

## What the sources do not establish

Register studies count recorded diagnoses rather than screening whole populations, so they cannot rule out real prevalence differences by sex. The phenotype and bias evidence comes from syntheses whose sourced abstracts report no pooled effect sizes or risk-of-bias grading. Most evidence concerns binary sex in Western, largely Swedish samples; gender-diverse and non-Western populations are little represented.
