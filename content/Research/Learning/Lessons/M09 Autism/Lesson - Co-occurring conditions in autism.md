# Co-occurring conditions in autism

**Module.** M09 Autism · **Topic.** [[Co-occurring conditions in autism]]

## Why this matters

Autistic people are not simply autistic and nothing else: mental health, neurological, sleep and gastrointestinal conditions all occur more often alongside autism than in the general population. The approximate scale matters for two risks pulling in opposite directions - under-diagnosing a real second condition, and over-attributing ordinary difficulties to "just being autistic" - and this lesson builds the reading habits to tell which risk applies in a given case.

## The core model

A meta-analysis restricted to clinically confirmed diagnoses found pooled prevalence of about 28% for ADHD, 20% for anxiety disorders, 13% for sleep-wake disorders, 11% for depressive disorders, 9% for obsessive-compulsive disorder, 5% for bipolar disorders and 4% for schizophrenia spectrum disorders - most well above general-population rates, and higher again in clinical than population or registry samples.
[[P31447415 Lai 2019 Co-occurring mental health diagnoses in autism#^p31447415-rates|Lai 2019]] [[P31447415 Lai 2019 Co-occurring mental health diagnoses in autism#^p31447415-moderators|Lai 2019]]

Those figures carry a caution that matters more than any single number: unexplained differences between the underlying studies were extreme, so each percentage is the centre of a wide range rather than a fixed rate for any one autistic person.
[[P31447415 Lai 2019 Co-occurring mental health diagnoses in autism#^p31447415-limit|Lai 2019]]

A larger meta-analysis of about 590,000 autistic children and adults found developmental coordination, sleep-wake, gastrointestinal, ADHD, anxiety, weight and feeding or eating problems among the most frequent co-occurring conditions, with epilepsy also more common than in the general population - about one in ten autistic people, more so in clinical samples, in adolescents and adults, and where intellectual disability is more common in the sample.
[[P37913872 Micai 2023 Co-occurring conditions in autism#^p37913872-scope|Micai 2023]] [[P37913872 Micai 2023 Co-occurring conditions in autism#^p37913872-frequent|Micai 2023]] [[P34510916 Liu 2022 Epilepsy prevalence in autism#^p34510916-prevalence|Liu 2022]] [[P34510916 Liu 2022 Epilepsy prevalence in autism#^p34510916-moderators|Liu 2022]]

Because epilepsy prevalence tracks intellectual disability so closely, a pooled figure depends heavily on the sample mix - a clinical sample skewed toward more support needs will show a higher rate without true risk having changed.

Diagnostic overshadowing explains why these numbers cut both ways in practice. It means attributing a new difficulty to a diagnosis someone already has, so a second, real condition goes unassessed; features overlap enough that a co-occurring condition can be missed or, less often, double-counted. The same overshadowing runs the other way too: many autistic people, especially women, carry a psychiatric diagnosis such as ADHD, anxiety or depression for years before autism itself is recognised.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. An autistic adult develops new, persistent low mood and sleep problems. A clinician's first instinct is to write "this is just autism" and move on. Apply the overshadowing check instead: is there a plausible alternative explanation - a mood or sleep disorder in its own right - that would be assessed in a non-autistic person presenting the same way? If yes, autism is a reason to adapt how the assessment is carried out, not a reason to skip it. The accurate response treats new or worsening difficulty as a question for assessment, using the same diagnostic categories available to anyone, while staying alert that autism-related features could make the presentation look different from a textbook case.

## Common confusions

- "A co-occurring condition rate applies equally to every autistic person." Pooled figures mix clinical, registry and population samples with very different rates; heterogeneity in the Lai 2019 estimates was extreme.
- "A new difficulty in an autistic person is probably just autism." Diagnostic overshadowing describes exactly this error, and it can leave a real, treatable second condition unassessed.
- "High co-occurrence rates mean autism causes these other conditions." The meta-analyses establish that conditions occur together more often than chance, not that one causes another.

## What the sources do not establish

The Micai 2023 and Liu 2022 abstracts sourced here report no pooled confidence intervals or heterogeneity statistics, so their figures are approximate. None of the sources establish how much of the apparent excess reflects genuinely distinct conditions versus overlapping features counted twice, and register studies more broadly miss undiagnosed autism and events that never reached clinical care.

## Answer notes

2. Overshadowing means attributing a new difficulty to an existing diagnosis so a second condition goes unassessed; it can cause a genuine second condition to be missed, or cause autism itself to go unrecognised behind an earlier psychiatric diagnosis.
