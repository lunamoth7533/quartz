# Neurodiversity paradigm

**Module.** M09 Autism · **Topic.** [[Neurodiversity paradigm]]

## Why this matters

The neurodiversity paradigm treats autism as one variant among many kinds of human mind rather than a disorder to be fixed. It changes what counts as a good research question and a good outcome, which is why theories like monotropism are treated here as insider knowledge rather than dismissed as anecdote. It also attracts real, substantive critique, and reading it well means holding the case and the critique together rather than picking a side by default.

## The core model

The paradigm's argument against conventional autism research names three limits: a heavy focus on deficits, attention to the individual rather than the environment around them, and a narrow range of perspectives shaping which questions get asked. The proposal is that core elements of neurodiversity thinking can answer each limit, changing what autism research asks and how it is carried out. The case was pressed first by autistic people and later taken up by non-autistic researchers.
[[P34730840 Pellicano 2022 Neurodiversity in autism science#^p34730840-challenges|Pellicano 2022]] [[P34730840 Pellicano 2022 Neurodiversity in autism science#^p34730840-implications|Pellicano 2022]] [[P34730840 Pellicano 2022 Neurodiversity in autism science#^p34730840-paradigm|Pellicano 2022]]

A common mistake is treating "neurodiversity" as one fixed position on disability. A conceptual review distinguishes a strong social model, where society alone creates disability, from a more careful, interactional reading in which individual characteristics and society both contribute, and calls the strong version controversial rather than a consensus position.
[[P36158596 Dwyer 2022 Neurodiversity approaches for researchers#^p36158596-meaning|Dwyer 2022]] [[P36158596 Dwyer 2022 Neurodiversity approaches for researchers#^p36158596-social-model|Dwyer 2022]]

A survey of 504 autism community members bears this out: the society-only view was common but far from universal, and even people who held it still endorsed some individually targeted help, such as teaching adaptive skills or treating co-occurring depression or epilepsy. Backing the movement went with favouring social change and accommodating environments and with less enthusiasm for normalising interventions - but most participants still supported teaching adaptive skills, which is not the all-or-nothing position sometimes attributed to it.
[[P39291753 Dwyer 2025 Community views of neurodiversity and intervention#^p39291753-social-model|Dwyer 2025]] [[P39291753 Dwyer 2025 Community views of neurodiversity and intervention#^p39291753-support|Dwyer 2025]]

The main critiques deserve equal weight. The most persistent concerns scope: to whom the approach applies and what it implies for diagnostic categories, especially for autistic people with intellectual disability and high support needs, whose views are least represented in surveys of engaged community members. A second critique is more basic: a paradigm can change which questions researchers ask without yet showing that the resulting research produces better lives, since the outcome studies needed to test that remain thin.
[[P36158596 Dwyer 2022 Neurodiversity approaches for researchers#^p36158596-caution-scope|Appraisal: Dwyer 2022]] [[P39291753 Dwyer 2025 Community views of neurodiversity and intervention#^p39291753-caution-sample|Appraisal: Dwyer 2025]] [[P34730840 Pellicano 2022 Neurodiversity in autism science#^p34730840-caution-paradigm|Appraisal: Pellicano 2022]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A blog post states: "The neurodiversity movement says autistic people should never receive individual support, only accommodations." If accurate, almost no supporter should endorse anything targeted at the individual. But the 504-person survey found the opposite: most participants, including supporters, still endorsed individually targeted help like adaptive-skills teaching or treating co-occurring conditions, while opposing interventions aimed at eliminating autistic traits. The accurate version is narrower: the paradigm favours social and environmental change and opposes normalisation-focused goals, while leaving room for individual support the person or community endorses.

## Common confusions

- "Neurodiversity means disability is entirely created by society." That is the strong social model, which the field's own conceptual review calls controversial rather than settled.
- "Supporting neurodiversity means opposing all individual support." Most supporters in the community survey still endorsed some individually targeted help.
- "A paradigm shift is itself evidence that outcomes improve." Asking different questions is not the same as showing the answers produce better long-term outcomes; that evidence is still limited.

## What the sources do not establish

The core statements are position papers and conceptual reviews that argue for a framework rather than test a hypothesis. The community survey measures attitudes in a self-selected, engaged sample, not whether neurodiversity-aligned support produces better long-term wellbeing, participation or quality of life than other approaches - no such trial is sourced in this library.
