# Social cognition accounts

**Module.** M09 Autism · **Topic.** [[Social cognition accounts]]

## Why this matters

Social cognition accounts produced the best-known experimental paradigms in autism research, the tasks that ask someone to infer what another person believes. They are also the accounts double empathy complicates: if misunderstanding can be relational, a task that scores only one person's inference about another needs careful reading. This lesson gives you the tools to read a mentalising-task finding without over- or under-crediting it.

## The core model

Autism is described as a construct covering early-emerging differences in social communication together with restricted, repetitive or sensory features, affecting just under one percent of people worldwide. Social cognition accounts propose that the social differences in this picture reflect differences in inferring other people's mental states, and theory-of-mind tasks are the paradigms the account is best known for.
[[P31949163 Lord 2020 Autism spectrum disorder primer#^p31949163-construct|Autism spectrum disorder primer]]

The account has a real limit: performance on these tasks correlates only weakly with everyday social outcomes, and many autistic people pass them outright. That correlation is the hinge for any clinical claim built on a task score - a difference with no reliable link to daily functioning supports a description of task performance, not a diagnosis of "cannot understand others."
[[P31949163 Lord 2020 Autism spectrum disorder primer#^p31949163-construct|Autism spectrum disorder primer]]

Measurement discipline matters just as much as task design. Comparing autistic and non-autistic groups on any social-cognition measure assumes the instrument works the same way in both - a property called measurement invariance, which has to be tested rather than assumed. Where it has not been tested, a group difference inherits an unknown amount of measurement artefact alongside whatever real difference exists.
[[P27942093 Putnick 2016 Measurement invariance#^p27942093-definition|Measurement invariance]] [[P27942093 Putnick 2016 Measurement invariance#^p27942093-practice|Measurement invariance]]

A 2025 pre-registered experiment shows what careful task design can reveal. It separated visual perspective-taking from belief reasoning and found autistic adults were no slower or less accurate overall than non-autistic adults, and made fewer errors; the one real difference was a steeper time cost as the angle between self and other grew - read by the authors as a difference in strategy rather than a deficit in the ability itself.
[[P39533155 Lara Green 2025 Perspective taking and belief reasoning#^p39533155-performance|Lara Green 2025]] [[P39533155 Lara Green 2025 Perspective taking and belief reasoning#^p39533155-rotation|Lara Green 2025]] [[P39533155 Lara Green 2025 Perspective taking and belief reasoning#^p39533155-interpretation|Lara Green 2025]]

The account is now treated as one partial description among several, alongside interactional accounts like double empathy and accounts built around sensory and communication access, rather than a single explanation that settles the field.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A study reports: "Autistic adults were significantly slower on a belief-reasoning task, proving a core deficit in understanding others." Before accepting the word "deficit," check three things. Was overall accuracy also lower, or only response time on the hardest trials - a slowdown on the hardest items looks more like a strategy difference than a missing ability. Has the instrument been shown to score both groups comparably, so a slower average is not partly a measurement artefact? And does the score predict anything about how the person manages relationships day to day? Without those checks, "proving a core deficit" is far stronger than the data can carry; "found a task-specific pattern consistent with a strategy difference" is what the evidence actually supports.

## Common confusions

- "Failing a theory-of-mind task means someone cannot understand other people." Task performance correlates only weakly with everyday social functioning.
- "A group difference on a mentalising task is automatically a real ability difference." It first requires the instrument to measure both groups the same way.
- "Social cognition accounts and double empathy must compete." They describe different levels - one what differs, the other how it plays out between people - and can both be partly right.

## What the sources do not establish

The primer is a review summarising the field, not a new study, and does not establish that any mentalising intervention improves daily functioning. Task-level findings, including the 2025 perspective-taking study, describe performance on that task in that sample; they do not by themselves show what happens in unscripted, everyday interaction.
