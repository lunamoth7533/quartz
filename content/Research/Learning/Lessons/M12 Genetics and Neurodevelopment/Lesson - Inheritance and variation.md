# Inheritance and variation

**Module.** M12 Genetics and Neurodevelopment · **Topic.** [[Inheritance and variation]]

## Why this matters

Almost every condition in this library - bipolar disorder, ADHD, autism, complex PTSD's underlying vulnerabilities - is genetically complex rather than single-gene. Knowing what separates the two kinds of inheritance, and where most human variation actually sits, keeps a family-history conversation from turning into Mendelian arithmetic that does not apply.

## The core model

Single-gene ("Mendelian") conditions follow recognisable inheritance patterns that produce predictable ratios within families. Complex traits are a different problem: many variants of small effect combine with environmental contributions, so family patterns do not produce clean ratios and cannot be read the same way. Almost none of the conditions in this library are single-gene, which limits how far Mendelian reasoning extends here. [[F07 OpenStax Human genetics#^f07-expression|Human genetics]]

Variation is the raw material both kinds of inheritance act on: single-letter changes, small insertions or deletions, and larger copy-number differences, whose consequence depends on where they sit - coding sequence, regulatory sequence, or sequence with no known function. Most variants found in large behavioural-genetics studies sit in regulatory territory, which is one reason the inference from "variant" to "changed protein" is usually indirect.

Most human genetic variation is also common and shared rather than rare and private to one family - which is why studies hunting for common variants need very large samples, while studies hunting for rare variants need sequencing and family data, since the relevant changes may be new to that family altogether. [[F67 NHGRI GWAS fact sheet#^f67-scale|GWAS fact sheet]] "New to that family" names a real category: de novo variants, which arise in a parent's gamete or the early embryo and are absent from both parents' own genomes - part of why careful sequencing studies genotype parents too. [[P31981491 Satterstrom 2020 Autism exome sequencing#^p31981491-model|Autism exome sequencing]]

Finally, relatives share genes and environments together, and the two are correlated - parents supply both - so raw family resemblance overstates genetic contribution until a design separates the two shares. That is the problem the next lesson's twin and adoption designs solve; heritability is not a fact you can read off a family tree by eye.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A parent notices three of four siblings share an unusual trait, and a relative says: "That looks exactly like a dominant gene - each child had a coin-flip chance." A true dominant pattern predicts a specific, testable ratio across many families, not just an impression from one family of four - far too small a sample to distinguish a 50% chance from a 75% observed rate - and it predicts the trait appears the same way regardless of environment. If the trait is continuously distributed and environmentally sensitive - mood reactivity, say - the polygenic model is the one generally supported, not the single-gene model. The honest statement is "this family shows a trait running in it," not "this family shows a dominant gene."

## Common confusions

- "A trait running in a family means one gene is responsible." Most behavioural traits are polygenic; clustering does not imply single-gene inheritance.
- "Rare means insignificant." Rare variants can have larger individual effects than common ones; frequency and importance are different axes.
- "A variant absent from public databases must be new in this family." It may simply be rare and undersampled; de novo status is established by testing the parents directly.

## What the sources do not establish

These sources establish general genetic architecture and the existence of de novo variation; they do not establish which specific variants matter for any named trait here, or how such variants combine - an open question for most psychiatric traits.

## Check yourself

1. Why can't Mendelian ratios be used to reason about most psychiatric traits?
2. What does it mean for a variant to be "de novo," and how is that established?
3. Why do rare-variant studies typically need family samples while common-variant studies need very large unrelated samples?

## Answer notes

1. Because complex traits involve many variants of small effect plus environmental contributions, not one gene with a clean dominant or recessive pattern.
2. It arose new in a parent's gamete or the early embryo rather than being inherited; established by sequencing the parents and confirming its absence in both.
3. Rare variants may be unique to a family, so finding them needs sequencing relatives directly; common variants have tiny individual effects, so detecting them needs averaging over very large samples.
