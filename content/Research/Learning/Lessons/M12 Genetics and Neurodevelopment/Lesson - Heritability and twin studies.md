# Heritability and twin studies

**Module.** M12 Genetics and Neurodevelopment · **Topic.** [[Heritability and twin studies]]

## Why this matters

Heritability numbers get quoted constantly for bipolar disorder, ADHD, autism and trauma-related conditions, and misread just as often - as if 74% heritable meant a person is "74% caused by genes." Reading these figures as statements about populations, not individuals, is a load-bearing skill in this domain.

## The core model

The core idea is a comparison. Identical twins share practically all their DNA sequence; fraternal twins share, on average, about half the DNA that varies between people - the same as any siblings. If identical twins resemble each other on a trait more than fraternal twins do, the extra resemblance is credited to genes; resemblance shared by both types beyond that is credited to shared environment. [[F07 OpenStax Human genetics#^f07-heritability|Human genetics]]

A worked case shows the arithmetic. A large meta-analysis of autism twin studies found identical-twin correlations of about .98 and fraternal-twin correlations of about .53 to .67, giving heritability estimates of 64% to 91%. [[P26709141 Tick 2016 Heritability of autism twin meta-analysis#^p26709141-correlations|Tick 2016]] That range matters, and the same meta-analysis found a shared-environment component only under certain modelling assumptions - detail lost when a figure travels without its method.

For ADHD, family, twin and adoption evidence converges on substantial familial clustering, with a commonly cited heritability of 74%. [[P29892054 Faraone 2019 Genetics of ADHD#^p29892054-heritability|Faraone 2019]] Comparable figures for bipolar disorder, autism and a lower, sex-differentiated SNP-based figure for PTSD appear elsewhere in this domain; each carries its own design and population and is not interchangeable with the others.

The part most often lost: heritability describes how much of the variation among people in one population, at one time, is statistically associated with genetic differences among them. It says nothing about how fixed the trait is, which genes are involved, or any one person's causal story. A high estimate in one population does not automatically transfer to a different population with different environmental variation.

There is also a documented gap between twin-based heritability and what molecular studies find. In ADHD, genome-wide studies attribute roughly a third of the twin-estimated heritability to measured common variants, with rare structural variants accounting for a further slice. [[P29892054 Faraone 2019 Genetics of ADHD#^p29892054-common|Faraone 2019]] Part of the gap is definitional: molecular estimates count only what current genotyping captures among unrelated people, so they sit below twin estimates by design - the gap is not automatically an "environmental" share.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A fictional twin study of "reading persistence" reports r = .80 for identical twins and r = .45 for fraternal twins, and a summary states: "This proves reading persistence is 80% genetic in every child." That gap is what would estimate heritability in that sample, using that model, in that population - it does not mean each child's persistence is "80% genetic," since heritability describes variation across the sample, not the composition of one person. It also says nothing about whether coaching or sleep could shift any individual child's score; a population statistic is fully compatible with individual scores being highly responsive to intervention.

## Common confusions

- "A heritability of 70% means a person is 70% shaped by genes." It describes variation across a population, not the composition of any individual.
- "High heritability means the trait cannot be changed by environment." A trait can be highly heritable in a population and still respond to intervention.
- "Twin-based and SNP-based heritability are the same number measured two ways." They estimate different things by design; the SNP-based figure is expected to be lower.

## What the sources do not establish

These estimates come from twin, family and SNP-based designs in specific, largely European-ancestry samples; they do not establish which genes are causal, whether the figure holds in other populations, or any one person's outcome. A twin-specific heritability estimate for PTSD or complex PTSD is not yet available in this library.

## Check yourself

1. What comparison does a twin design use to estimate heritability?
2. Why can two different heritability estimates for the same condition both be correct?
3. Why is the gap between twin-based and SNP-based heritability not automatically evidence of environmental influence?

## Answer notes

1. The extra resemblance of identical twins over fraternal twins, credited to their greater genetic similarity.
2. Because heritability is an output of a specific design, sample, population and set of modelling assumptions, so different studies can legitimately produce different figures.
3. SNP-based methods only count what current genotyping captures among unrelated people, so they are structurally expected to sit below twin estimates regardless of environment.
