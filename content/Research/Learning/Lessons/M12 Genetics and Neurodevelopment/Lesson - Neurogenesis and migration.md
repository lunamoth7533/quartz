# Neurogenesis and migration

**Module.** M12 Genetics and Neurodevelopment · **Topic.** [[Neurogenesis and migration]]

## Why this matters

This lesson opens the module's second half: how the genetic material covered so far actually builds a brain. Neurogenesis and migration are the earliest steps, and the pattern they establish - overlapping processes rather than a tidy checklist - makes the rest of neurodevelopment easier to read correctly.

## The core model

Development runs through several processes - neurogenesis, migration, differentiation, synapse formation, pruning and myelination - that overlap in time across brain regions rather than completing in strict sequence. A claim pinning one process to one exact age is always an approximation. [[P21042938 Stiles 2010 Basics of brain development#^p21042938-processes|The basics of brain development]] [[P21042938 Stiles 2010 Basics of brain development#^p21042938-overlap|The basics of brain development]] It still helps to hold the classical ordered version in mind, since the overlap description refines it rather than replacing it: proliferation, migration, differentiation, synapse formation, then experience-dependent refinement, roughly in that order. [[P19794405 Tau 2010 Normal development of brain circuits#^p19794405-sequence|Normal development of brain circuits]]

Structurally, the earliest step is the neural tube - a fold of tissue that becomes the entire central nervous system, with the fluid-filled ventricular system forming from its hollow centre. [[F29 OpenStax Embryologic perspective#^f29-tube|The Embryologic Perspective]]

Neurogenesis has an internal switch: early progenitor cells first divide symmetrically, expanding their own numbers, then switch to asymmetric division, producing neurons while maintaining some progenitors. The timing of that switch is part of what determines how many neurons a region ends up with.

Migration does most of the work of building the cortex's structure. Cortical neurons are generated near the brain's fluid-filled cavities and travel outward along guide cells (radial glia), in a specific order: the deepest cortical layers are populated by the earliest-born cells, and progressively more superficial layers by later-born ones - the "inside-out" pattern. Because of this ordering, a disruption to migration early in development produces a structurally different outcome than the same disruption occurring later. [[P21042938 Stiles 2010 Basics of brain development#^p21042938-processes|The basics of brain development]]

Where a migrating cell ends up is not pre-programmed from the start. Broad regional identity is set early by the neural tube's structure and signalling gradients within it, but migrating cells also carry intrinsic programmes and respond to local signals along the way - identity and position are negotiated between what a cell carries and what it encounters, not fixed in advance. [[F29 OpenStax Embryologic perspective#^f29-vesicles|The Embryologic Perspective]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A fictional animal study disrupts a migration-guiding molecule at an early embryonic stage in one group and at a later stage in a second group. The early-disruption group shows more severe reorganisation of the deepest cortical layers; the late-disruption group mainly affects the outermost layers. Apply the inside-out principle: the deepest layers are built by the earliest-migrating cells, so an early disruption hits the foundation, while the same disruption later mainly affects the last, most superficial cells still in transit. The outcome depends jointly on what is disrupted and exactly when in the overlapping sequence it happens - "when" is often the main determinant of the pattern, not a minor detail.

## Common confusions

- "Development is a strict, one-thing-at-a-time checklist." The named processes overlap substantially in time and across regions.
- "A migrating neuron's fate is fixed the moment it is born." Final identity depends on both an intrinsic programme and local signals encountered along the way.
- "Adult human neurogenesis works the same way as embryonic neurogenesis." Embryonic neurogenesis is well established; adult human neurogenesis remains an area of active methodological dispute.

## What the sources do not establish

Embryonic neurogenesis and migration are established from animal models and human tissue studies; claims about adult human neurogenesis are contested and rest on indirect, difficult-to-interpret markers.

## Check yourself

1. Why does the "inside-out" pattern mean that timing, not just the type of disruption, shapes a developmental outcome?
2. What determines a migrating neuron's eventual regional identity?
3. Why should a claim about brain development specify the process, the region and the species?

## Answer notes

1. The deepest layers are built by the earliest-migrating cells and outer layers by later ones, so the same disruption produces different consequences depending on timing.
2. Both broad signals set up early in the neural tube and the cell's own intrinsic programme, combined with local signals encountered during migration.
3. Developmental timing and mechanism both vary substantially by system and species, so a finding does not automatically transfer.
