# Axon guidance and synaptogenesis

**Module.** M12 Genetics and Neurodevelopment · **Topic.** [[Axon guidance and synaptogenesis]]

## Why this matters

Once a neuron has migrated to its final position, it still has to find the right partners to connect with, often at a distance. How that navigation works - with a small molecular toolkit - explains both the precision of brain wiring and why disrupting one guidance cue tends to affect specific pathways rather than the whole brain.

## The core model

A growing axon finds its target using a small number of conserved cue families - netrins, Slits, semaphorins and ephrins. Each family includes attractive and repulsive versions, works at short and long range, and is laid out in gradients and boundaries along the path. The axon reads the combination of cues present through the specific receptors it happens to be expressing. [[P12471249 Dickson 2002 Molecular mechanisms of axon guidance#^p12471249-cues|Molecular mechanisms of axon guidance]]

This combinatorial design is how a modest number of guidance molecules specifies enormously complex wiring: regulatory mechanisms control which receptors an axon expresses, and when, so the right axon responds to the right cue at the right moment. [[P12471249 Dickson 2002 Molecular mechanisms of axon guidance#^p12471249-precision|Molecular mechanisms of axon guidance]] One consequence follows directly: disrupting a single cue tends to shift only the axons depending on it, not wiring everywhere at once - the pattern seen in genetic disorders where specific guidance molecules are implicated. [[P33706917 Korb 2021 Axon guidance signalling#^p33706917-context|Axon guidance signalling]]

The decision-making happens at the growth cone, a sensing structure at the axon's tip, which responds to cues by remodelling its internal scaffolding (the cytoskeleton) to steer toward or away from a signal. Whether a cue attracts or repels is not fixed to the molecule - it depends on the growth cone's current receptor makeup, itself regulated and changeable, which is part of why the same cue can attract at one stage and repel at another. [[P12471249 Dickson 2002 Molecular mechanisms of axon guidance#^p12471249-growthcone|Molecular mechanisms of axon guidance]] [[P33706917 Korb 2021 Axon guidance signalling#^p33706917-context|Axon guidance signalling]]

Once an axon reaches its target, synapse formation follows a staged sequence: an adhesive contact stabilises, structures on both sides assemble in coordination, and receptor composition on the receiving side keeps changing after the connection is already functioning. An early synapse is therefore not simply a smaller mature one - some are structurally present but not yet fully functional ("silent synapses"), and findings from young circuits do not automatically apply to adults. [[F101 Neuroscience Online synapse formation and elimination#^f101-receptors|Synapse Formation, Survival, and Elimination]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A fictional study knocks out one semaphorin family member and finds auditory-pathway axons severely misrouted while nearby visual-pathway axons are unaffected. A summary claims: "This gene controls brain wiring." If auditory axons rely heavily on receptors tuned to that semaphorin at the relevant window, while visual axons at that stage read a different cue combination instead, the selective effect is exactly what the model predicts, not a paradox. The corrected claim: "this cue is one part of the combinatorial code some pathways, but not others, depend on" - more informative than "controls brain wiring," since it points to where the mechanism lives: receptor expression in the affected pathway.

## Common confusions

- "One guidance molecule controls all brain wiring." Guidance works combinatorially; a cue matters most to pathways expressing its receptor at the relevant time.
- "A cue is always attractive or always repulsive." The same molecule can do either, depending on the growth cone's receptor state.
- "A newly formed synapse works exactly like a mature one." Receptor composition and function keep changing after initial formation.

## What the sources do not establish

Guidance molecules and receptors are well established through genetic and biochemical work, mostly in animal models; their relevance to specific human developmental conditions is inferred from disease associations rather than directly demonstrated in human tissue.

## Check yourself

1. Why can a small number of guidance cue families specify highly complex, specific wiring patterns?
2. Why might disrupting one guidance molecule affect one neural pathway but not another?
3. Why is an early, newly formed synapse not just a smaller version of a mature synapse?

## Answer notes

1. Axons read combinations of cues through their own receptor complement, so a modest set of families can combine in many ways to produce specific outcomes.
2. Different pathways' axons express different receptor combinations at a given stage, so they depend on different cues to different degrees.
3. Synapse formation is staged - adhesion, coordinated assembly, then continuing receptor maturation - so an early synapse can be present without yet functioning maturely.
