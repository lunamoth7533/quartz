# Therapy models overview

**Module.** M04 Psychology · **Topic.** [[Therapy models overview]]

## Why this matters

Every psychotherapy claim here - "CBT works for X," "DBT reduces Y" - is a claim about a specific package, tested against a specific comparator, rated by a specific outcome. This lesson supplies the questions that turn a vague "therapy works" headline into something checkable.

## The core model

Psychotherapy orientations differ in what they treat as the mechanism of change, and naming that mechanism is what makes an approach testable, because it specifies what should change first. Behavioural approaches apply conditioning principles directly, including exposure; cognitive approaches target appraisals and beliefs, often combined with behavioural ones; psychodynamic approaches work with recurring relational patterns; humanistic approaches emphasise the therapeutic relationship; systemic approaches work with relationships rather than individuals. [[F49 OpenStax Psychology 2e types of treatment#^f49-psychotherapy|Types of Treatment]] [[F49 OpenStax Psychology 2e types of treatment#^f49-behavioural|Types of Treatment]]

Combination, not monotherapy, is the practical norm - medication and psychotherapy are frequently used together - and a combination trial answers a different question from a trial of either alone, so the two are not interchangeable evidence.

What a trial compares against decides how large its effect looks. Waiting list, treatment as usual and an active alternative therapy are three different bars, and "works" is only ever relative to whichever bar was used - beating a waiting list is not the same as beating standard care.

Non-specific factors are part of the mechanism problem: alliance, expectancy and allegiance (a researcher's enthusiasm for the treatment tested) contribute to outcomes across every orientation, so attributing an effect to one technique needs a design that holds those factors constant - which most trials skip. A Cochrane review of CBT for adult ADHD makes this concrete: gains over a waiting list were large, but the comparison against supportive psychotherapy was not significant, and certainty was mostly low or very low. [[P29566425 Lopez 2018 Cognitive behavioural interventions for ADHD#^p29566425-caution-rater|Appraisal: CBT for ADHD (Cochrane review)]]

Testing which part of a therapy does the work needs a dismantling or additive design - the full package against a version with one component removed - and most trials never do this, so a claim that one component is "the active ingredient" rests on a thinner base than the package-level evidence. A bipolar-disorder component analysis illustrates the alternative: adjunctive manualised psychotherapy was linked to lower recurrence than control, and psychoeducation with supervised skills practice was linked to fewer recurrences - while the method only estimates average contributions across heterogeneous protocols. [[P33052390 Miklowitz 2021 Adjunctive psychotherapy for bipolar disorder#^p33052390-recurrence|Adjunctive psychotherapy for bipolar disorder]] [[P33052390 Miklowitz 2021 Adjunctive psychotherapy for bipolar disorder#^p33052390-caution-ingredients|Appraisal: Adjunctive psychotherapy for bipolar disorder]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. Two headlines both say "Therapy A and Therapy B work equally well," based on a trial finding no significant difference between them.

Do not read "no significant difference" as "proven equivalent." Equivalence findings need the same scrutiny as superiority findings: what was the trial's power to detect a real difference, what horizon did it measure, and was it actually designed as a non-inferiority test - with a pre-specified margin - or simply a superiority trial that failed to reach significance? A null result from an underpowered or short trial supports only "no difference was detected here," which is weaker than "the two produce the same outcome"; only a trial deliberately built to test equivalence can support the stronger claim.

## Common confusions

- "If a therapy beat a waiting list, it beats standard care." The comparator sets the bar; beating a waiting list and beating treatment as usual are unequal claims.
- "A therapy's active ingredient is whatever its name emphasises." Identifying a component requires a dismantling design most trials lack; the honest default is a package that works with a partly tested mechanism.
- "No significant difference between two therapies proves equal effectiveness." That needs a trial specifically powered to test equivalence, not a superiority trial that came back null.

## What the sources do not establish

Evidence is uneven across orientations - strongest for cognitive-behavioural and behavioural methods, thinner for psychodynamic and humanistic approaches - and component analyses estimate average contributions across heterogeneous protocols, not what produced any one person's improvement.

## Check yourself

1. Why does naming a therapy's proposed mechanism matter for whether it can be tested?
2. Why can't "beat a waiting list" and "beat standard care" be treated as the same claim?
3. What kind of trial design is needed to identify a therapy's active ingredient, and why do most trials lack it?

## Answer notes

1. Because the named mechanism specifies what should change first, which is what a trial can then measure and check.
2. Because the comparator sets the bar an effect is measured against, and a waiting list is a much lower bar than ongoing standard care.
3. A dismantling or additive design, comparing the full package against a version missing one component; most trials test the whole package against a comparator and were not built to isolate components.
