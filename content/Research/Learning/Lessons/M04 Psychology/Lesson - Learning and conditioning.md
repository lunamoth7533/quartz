# Learning and conditioning

**Module.** M04 Psychology · **Topic.** [[Learning and conditioning]]

## Why this matters

Conditioning research explains how cues and consequences shape behaviour, and why old patterns return after a
change seems complete. It is the mechanism layer under exposure-based and habit-focused treatments, and it
offers one of the most practically useful findings in the vault: extinction is new learning, not erasure.

## The core model

Classical conditioning pairs a neutral cue with a meaningful stimulus so that the cue comes to evoke a response.
[[F10 Noba Conditioning and learning#^f10-classical|Conditioning and Learning]]

Operant conditioning changes behaviour through consequences: reinforcement makes a behaviour more likely and
punishment makes it less likely, with different side-effect profiles.
[[F10 Noba Conditioning and learning#^f10-operant|Conditioning and Learning]]

Those are functional definitions. A consequence counts as reinforcement when it increases how often the
behaviour occurs and as punishment when it decreases it, which is separate from whether the consequence feels
pleasant and separate from whether something is added or removed. Reward and punishment in everyday language do
not always map onto the function, which is why laboratory work measures the behaviour rather than the label.

Extinction is new learning that inhibits an old association rather than erasing it, which is why conditioned
responses can return.
[[F10 Noba Conditioning and learning#^f10-extinction|Conditioning and Learning]]

That last point changes how you read every relapse: the old association is still available, and its return in a
new context or after stress is predicted by the model rather than evidence that the earlier work failed. The
clinical implication is to vary contexts and to plan for the return of the response.

There is also a limit worth naming: Pavlovian and operant vocabulary is often stretched over complex human
behaviour, and real reinforcement schedules are messy, delayed and hard to observe.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. In a hypothetical laboratory study, a tone is paired with a mild
aversive stimulus until the tone alone produces a defensive response. The response is then extinguished by
presenting the tone repeatedly without the stimulus. Weeks later the tone is presented again in a different
room, and the defensive response returns.

Interpret it within the model. The return is consistent with the original association not having been erased
and with the new inhibitory learning being context- and time-dependent. Several distinct patterns can produce
a return, each with a different experimental signature: renewal, where returning to the original or a new
context releases the old association; spontaneous recovery, where the response reappears with the passage of
time; and reinstatement, where an unsignalled reminder of the aversive stimulus brings the response back.

The laboratory's next step is a test that separates these: reintroduce the tone in the original context, in the
extinction context, and after an unsignalled reminder, and compare the response across conditions. That design
answers which mechanism is operating; it says nothing about what any person should do.

## Common confusions

- "Extinction deletes the memory." It adds new learning that competes with the old association.
- "Punishment is the opposite of reinforcement and equally effective." The two have different effects and
  side-effect profiles; the source treats them separately.
- "Conditioning explains all behaviour." It is a powerful framework for learned associations and consequences,
  not a complete account of human action.

## What the sources do not establish

The teaching source covers principles with examples and does not provide clinical protocols; it does not
establish the effectiveness of any treatment built on conditioning, and this vault's therapy evidence comes
from separate sources.
