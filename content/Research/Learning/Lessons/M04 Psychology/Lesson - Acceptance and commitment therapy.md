# Acceptance and commitment therapy

**Module.** M04 Psychology · **Topic.** [[Acceptance and commitment therapy]]

## Why this matters

Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) is unusual among the therapies in this vault because its model names its own mechanism in detail - six specific components - making it possible to ask not just "does the package work?" but "do the named pieces do what the model says?" This lesson is about not letting laboratory support for the pieces stand in for clinical evidence about the whole.

## The core model

ACT is a third-wave cognitive behavioural therapy built on the psychological flexibility model, which names six components of its mechanism: acceptance, defusion (stepping back from the literal content of a thought), self as context, committed action, values, and contact with the present moment. Because the model names these pieces, ACT's evidence comes in two layers - whether components change the processes the model predicts, and whether changing those processes changes symptoms - and only the first has been tested directly and extensively. [[P23046777 Levin 2012 Psychological flexibility component studies#^p23046777-components|Levin 2012]]

In 66 laboratory studies, most named components - acceptance, defusion, present-moment contact, values, mixed mindfulness - each beat an inactive comparison on their target process. No separate significant effect was reported for two of six, self as context and committed action, so the model is tested unevenly. Experiential teaching (metaphors, exercises) outperformed plain explanation. These are brief manipulations, though: they show a component can move its process in one session, not that it carries clinical change across a full course. [[P23046777 Levin 2012 Psychological flexibility component studies#^p23046777-effects|Levin 2012]] [[P23046777 Levin 2012 Psychological flexibility component studies#^p23046777-experiential|Levin 2012]] [[P23046777 Levin 2012 Psychological flexibility component studies#^p23046777-caution-lab|Appraisal: Levin 2012]]

For clinical outcomes, in trauma-related symptoms: across 11 studies, people who received ACT reported fewer symptoms at treatment's end than comparison groups, a moderate effect with low heterogeneity. But the estimate covers only immediate post-treatment, comparison groups were mixed rather than an established trauma-focused treatment, and trauma symptoms are not an ICD-11 complex PTSD diagnosis - so this does not show how ACT compares against a benchmark, or whether the benefit lasts. [[P39374151 Rowe-Johnson 2025 ACT for trauma-related symptoms#^p39374151-effect|Rowe-Johnson 2025]] [[P39374151 Rowe-Johnson 2025 ACT for trauma-related symptoms#^p39374151-limit|Rowe-Johnson 2025]] [[P39374151 Rowe-Johnson 2025 ACT for trauma-related symptoms#^p39374151-caution-comparators|Appraisal: Rowe-Johnson 2025]]

A review across neurodevelopmental conditions - mostly autism, ADHD, learning disabilities - found 30 studies with mixed improvements and moderate-to-serious risk of bias, and some treated parents or staff rather than the autistic or ADHD person. That mix shows ACT has been tried across these groups, a modest claim, not that it reliably works for the people themselves. [[P34154376 Garcia 2022 ACT in neurodevelopmental disorders#^p34154376-designs|Garcia 2022]] [[P34154376 Garcia 2022 ACT in neurodevelopmental disorders#^p34154376-results|Garcia 2022]] [[P34154376 Garcia 2022 ACT in neurodevelopmental disorders#^p34154376-caution-mixed|Appraisal: Garcia 2022]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A promotional summary claims "laboratory studies prove ACT's six mechanisms work, so ACT is a scientifically validated treatment for trauma."

Separate the evidence types. Component studies show most - not all six - processes move under a brief manipulation; that is process-level evidence, not a clinical-outcome claim. The trauma meta-analysis is a separate layer: a moderate immediate effect against mixed comparisons, with no data on durability or comparison against established trauma-focused therapy. Neither layer supports "scientifically validated" in the strong sense implied - the accurate version keeps laboratory and clinical evidence in separate sentences, each with its own limits.

## Common confusions

- "Laboratory evidence that components work is clinical evidence that ACT works." Component studies test brief manipulations of single processes; trials test a full course against a real-world comparator.
- "All six components have equally strong support." Self as context and committed action had no separate significant effect in the component-study synthesis.
- "ACT's neurodevelopmental evidence shows it helps autistic and ADHD people." Some studies treated caregivers or staff, so the review shows ACT has been tried in these groups more than that it reliably works for them.

## What the sources do not establish

ACT has laboratory support for most named components and a moderate pooled effect on trauma-related symptoms immediately after treatment, but no comparison against established trauma-focused treatment, no follow-up data, and a neurodevelopmental base at moderate-to-serious risk of bias mixing caregiver and staff outcomes with those of autistic and ADHD people.

## Check yourself

1. Why does ACT's named six-component model create two distinct layers of evidence to evaluate separately?
2. Which two of the six components lacked a separately reported significant effect in the laboratory synthesis?
3. Why can't the neurodevelopmental-conditions review be read as showing that ACT reliably helps autistic and ADHD people?

## Answer notes

1. Because the model specifies both that its components change certain processes and that changing those processes changes symptoms - each claim needs its own evidence, and only the first has substantial direct laboratory support.
2. Self as context and committed action.
3. Because some of the reviewed studies treated parents or support staff rather than autistic or ADHD people directly, so the pooled result mixes different populations rather than isolating the people the therapy is meant for.
