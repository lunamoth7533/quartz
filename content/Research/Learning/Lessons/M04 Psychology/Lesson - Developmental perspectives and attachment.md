# Developmental perspectives and attachment

**Module.** M04 Psychology · **Topic.** [[Developmental perspectives and attachment]]

## Why this matters

Popular accounts of attachment often treat an infant's classification as a life sentence - "anxiously attached as a baby, anxiously attached forever." The evidence describes something more modest: a probabilistic, population-level pattern with real but small predictive value, sensitive to caregiving conditions and the measure used.

## The core model

Stage theories describe development as a sequence, but evidence for strict, universal stages is limited: change is often continuous rather than sharply staged, and culturally variable. Erikson's psychosocial stages extend this picture across the whole lifespan rather than stopping at childhood. [[F42 OpenStax Psychology 2e lifespan theories#^f42-stage|Lifespan Theories]] [[F42 OpenStax Psychology 2e lifespan theories#^f42-psychosocial|Lifespan Theories]]

Attachment classifications come from structured observation of how a caregiver and child interact, not from a single trait test. They are moderately stable when caregiving stays stable, and can change when caregiving changes - signalling that they measure something responsive to context, not a fixed internal property stamped in early life. [[F42 OpenStax Psychology 2e lifespan theories|Lifespan Theories]]

Sensitive windows in development are not a vague metaphor; where studied closely, they come with identifiable machinery. In the best-studied case, a window of heightened plasticity is triggered by maturing local inhibitory connections and closes as the extracellular matrix reorganises to consolidate learning. That machinery is specific to the system it was found in, and reactivating it is a research strategy, not a treatment - applying the concept loosely to attachment or social development is an extrapolation beyond what has been shown. [[P16261181 Hensch 2005 Critical period plasticity#^p16261181-trigger|Critical period plasticity]] [[P16261181 Hensch 2005 Critical period plasticity#^p16261181-consolidation|Critical period plasticity]]

Two recent studies quantify just how modest attachment's predictive reach is. In a pair of landmark longitudinal cohorts, covariate-adjusted associations between infant attachment security and later socioemotional outcomes came out at most around .10 to .15 - real, but small, and links with academic skills were as large or larger than the socioemotional ones the theory is usually invoked for. Two cohorts from one country cannot fix the association's size elsewhere, but the pattern - real, probabilistic, small - is the one to expect. [[P38086607 Nivison 2025 Predictive validity of the strange situation#^p38086607-socioemotional|Nivison 2025]] [[P38086607 Nivison 2025 Predictive validity of the strange situation#^p38086607-composites|Nivison 2025]]

That combination - real associations, small effect sizes, context-dependent measurement - is why the popular extension of attachment theory, reading an adult relationship pattern as the direct expression of an infant classification, outruns what the instruments and designs actually support.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. An adult reads a pop-psychology article claiming their difficulty trusting partners "traces back to" an insecure attachment classification they would have received as an infant, never actually assessed.

Separate three claims before accepting the story. A developmental mechanism demonstrated in one system (the inhibitory-circuit machinery behind a sensitive window) is not a generalisation across large samples. A generalisation across samples - attachment security predicting outcomes at around .10 to .15 - is not a claim about one individual's current pattern. And this person has no actual infant assessment, so the chain is a retrospective story, and retrospective accounts are reconstructions shaped by current mood. The honest statement is that early caregiving is one modest contributor among many, not a documented, individual-level explanation.

## Common confusions

- "An infant attachment classification determines adult relationship patterns." Longitudinal studies find real but small associations (roughly .10-.15), not a deterministic link.
- "A sensitive window means change is only possible during a fixed early period." Sensitive windows come with specific biological machinery in the systems where they have been studied, and applying the concept to attachment or social development without that evidence is an extrapolation.
- "My own memory of my early relationships is a reliable record." Retrospective accounts are reconstructions shaped by present mood and context, which is why prospective longitudinal designs carry more weight than recalled history.

## What the sources do not establish

Longitudinal studies support modest, probabilistic associations between early caregiving and later outcomes with substantial individual variation; they do not support a deterministic reading of any one person's attachment history, and the sensitive-window machinery described here comes from visual-cortex research, not from a demonstrated mechanism in attachment or social development.

2. What does it mean for a sensitive window to "come with machinery," and why does that limit how the concept can be applied elsewhere?
3. Why are retrospective reports of early relationships treated with caution in this literature?

## Answer notes

1. Because covariate-adjusted longitudinal associations between attachment security and later outcomes were at most around .10 to .15 - present and real, but modest, not deterministic.
2. It means the window is triggered and consolidated by identifiable biological processes in the system where it was studied; without equivalent evidence in another domain, applying the concept there is speculative rather than established.
3. Because memory is reconstructive and shaped by current mood and context, so a recalled account of early relationships is not a direct record of what happened.
