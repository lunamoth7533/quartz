# Rumination and repetitive negative thinking

**Module.** M04 Psychology · **Topic.** [[Rumination and repetitive negative thinking]]

## Why this matters

Repetitive negative thinking cuts across diagnostic lines here - it shows up next to intrusive memories in PTSD and next to both poles of bipolar disorder. Treating it as one transdiagnostic process, rather than separate topics per diagnosis, lets the same reading rules apply everywhere it turns up.

## The core model

Repetitive negative thinking (RNT) is the umbrella term for repeated thinking about negative content, with rumination and worry as its best-known specific forms. Studying it as transdiagnostic means asking the same questions in every diagnosis where it appears, rather than treating it as depression's or anxiety's private property - and the trial evidence backs up lumping the forms together: a common therapy reduced rumination, worry and general repetitive thinking by amounts that did not differ significantly. [[P39916353 Stenzel 2025 CBT for repetitive negative thinking#^p39916353-definition|Stenzel 2025]] [[P39916353 Stenzel 2025 CBT for repetitive negative thinking#^p39916353-effect|Stenzel 2025]]

In trauma, the association is large and consistent: across 64 samples of trauma-exposed adults, rumination correlated with posttraumatic stress symptoms at about r = .50, unchanged by time since trauma, gender, earlier trauma history or how rumination was measured. The link was strongest with intrusive re-experiencing, and rumination about the trauma itself gave a smaller effect than rumination as a general habit of mind - a reminder that a general tendency to dwell can matter as much as trauma-specific content. [[P28398085 Szabo 2017 Rumination and posttraumatic stress symptoms#^p28398085-association|Szabo 2017]] [[P28398085 Szabo 2017 Rumination and posttraumatic stress symptoms#^p28398085-clusters|Szabo 2017]]

Bipolar disorder is where rumination shows something unusual: among the emotion-regulation strategies compared in a network meta-analysis, rumination had the strongest association with depressive symptoms and also with (hypo)manic symptoms, while most other regulation strategies related mainly to the depressive side only. Repetitive thinking, in other words, is not tied to low mood exclusively - it tracks both poles of the illness, which argues against treating rumination as simply "depressive thinking." [[P39631486 Oliva 2025 Emotion regulation strategies in bipolar disorder#^p39631486-rumination|Oliva 2025]] [[P39631486 Oliva 2025 Emotion regulation strategies in bipolar disorder#^p39631486-depression|Oliva 2025]]

Both the trauma and bipolar findings come from correlations with current symptoms, which is a real limit: a cross-sectional correlation cannot separate rumination as a lasting habit that precedes and predicts an episode from rumination as simply part of the episode itself, happening at the same time as the symptoms it is correlated with. [[P39631486 Oliva 2025 Emotion regulation strategies in bipolar disorder#^p39631486-caution-state|Appraisal: Oliva 2025]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A summary claims "a new finding shows rumination causes manic episodes in bipolar disorder," citing the network meta-analysis linking rumination to both depressive and (hypo)manic symptoms.

Read the design before accepting "causes." The underlying studies are correlational and mostly cross-sectional - rumination and symptoms measured around the same time, not rumination first and episodes tracked afterward. That design can show they travel together; it cannot distinguish rumination contributing to an episode from rumination simply showing up once one has started. The accurate claim is "rumination is the strategy most associated with both poles, in cross-sectional data" - the causal version needs a longitudinal study, which the authors themselves call for.

## Common confusions

- "Rumination is just a depression symptom." In bipolar disorder it is linked to both depressive and (hypo)manic symptoms, and it is studied as a transdiagnostic process, not one disorder's private feature.
- "A strong correlation between rumination and symptoms shows rumination causes the episode." Cross-sectional correlational data cannot separate rumination as a cause from rumination as a concurrent part of the episode.
- "Rumination about the traumatic event itself is what matters most in PTSD." A general habit of rumination, not specifically about the trauma, showed at least as strong a link to posttraumatic stress symptoms.

## What the sources do not establish

The trauma and bipolar associations are consistent and moderate but correlational, largely cross-sectional and self-reported, so neither shows that rumination precedes or causes symptoms rather than accompanying them; the treatment evidence is stronger in design (55 randomised trials) but measures change in repetitive thinking itself at the end of treatment, not necessarily change in the disorders that process is linked to.

2. What is unusual about rumination's relationship to bipolar disorder compared with most other emotion-regulation strategies?
3. Why can't the correlation between rumination and PTSD or bipolar symptoms establish that rumination causes those symptoms?

## Answer notes

1. It means the same process is tracked across diagnoses rather than treated as one disorder's private symptom, and trial evidence supports this: a common therapy reduced rumination, worry and general repetitive thinking by similar amounts.
2. It is linked to both depressive and (hypo)manic symptoms, while most other regulation strategies relate mainly to depressive symptoms alone.
3. Because the underlying data are correlational and mostly cross-sectional, measuring rumination and symptoms at the same time rather than rumination before and episodes afterward.
