# Self-concept and identity

**Module.** M04 Psychology · **Topic.** [[Self-concept and identity]]

## Why this matters

"Negative self-concept" and "identity disturbance" sound like the same problem wearing two names, and get used almost interchangeably. The research keeps them apart - one is about the content of a self-view, the other its stability - a distinction that matters where complex PTSD and borderline personality disorder are both being considered.

## The core model

Two diagnoses put closely related but distinct ideas at their centre. Complex PTSD's wording describes a self-view that is persistently negative - a content problem, about what someone believes about themselves. Borderline personality disorder's wording instead stresses a self-image that keeps shifting - a stability problem, about how consistent the self-view is over time. One concerns the content of a self-concept; the other concerns its coherence and continuity. [[F17 VA National Center for PTSD Complex PTSD history and definitions#^f17-dso|VA: complex PTSD]] [[F84 NIMH Borderline personality disorder#^f84-pattern|NIMH: borderline personality disorder]]

That distinction matters for treatment too, because self-concept is not fixed: across 25 randomised trials, psychological treatments for PTSD improved negative self-concept with a moderate-to-large effect. The trials varied enormously, and that variation was not explained by whether a therapy was explicitly trauma-focused or included cognitive restructuring - more than one route to change appears to work. Self-concept was measured by self-report alongside symptoms, so part of the apparent change may track general relief rather than a specific shift. [[P36325255 Banz 2022 PTSD treatment and negative self-concept#^p36325255-effect|Banz 2022]] [[P36325255 Banz 2022 PTSD treatment and negative self-concept#^p36325255-moderators|Banz 2022]]

Self-concept also has a documented ADHD face that is easy to mislabel. Most controlled studies find lower self-esteem in adults with ADHD, higher symptom levels go with lower self-esteem, and self-esteem statistically mediates several ADHD-linked outcomes - but without controlling for co-occurring depression and anxiety, which independently lower self-esteem, this pattern cannot be assigned to ADHD itself. [[P38491855 Pedersen 2024 Self-esteem in adults with ADHD#^p38491855-lower|Pedersen 2024]] [[P38491855 Pedersen 2024 Self-esteem in adults with ADHD#^p38491855-mediation|Pedersen 2024]]

One proposed account for complex PTSD, the memory and identity theory, ties the two constructs together directly: trauma exposure interacts with individual vulnerability to produce both intrusive, sensation-based trauma memories and negative identities, which together generate the wider symptom picture. A single cross-sectional survey found predicted associations mostly held up - showing the pattern is consistent with data collected once, not that identity disturbance causes symptoms; the theory also comes from the same team that developed the diagnosis, a reason for care before treating it as independently confirmed.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A case summary describes someone with "a persistently negative view of themselves and an unstable sense of who they are," and a reader concludes this must mean both complex PTSD and borderline personality disorder are present.

Separate content from stability before reaching for two labels. Is the self-view negative in content (a stable but harsh judgement) or unstable in structure (shifting between different self-views over short periods) - different patterns pointing toward different constructs, though they can co-occur. A review comparing the two conditions finds most studies separate them by symptom profile while noting real co-occurrence, so overlapping features are not evidence the diagnoses are the same, nor proof both apply - that judgement needs the fuller picture, not one feature alone.

## Common confusions

- "Negative self-concept and identity disturbance are the same problem." One describes the content of a self-view (persistently negative); the other describes its stability (shifting, incoherent).
- "Lower self-esteem in adult ADHD is caused by ADHD itself." Most supporting studies did not control for co-occurring depression and anxiety, which independently lower self-esteem.
- "Because psychotherapy improved self-concept scores, it must have directly changed how people see themselves." Self-concept was measured alongside general symptoms, so some of the improvement may reflect broader symptom relief rather than a targeted shift.

## What the sources do not establish

Most of the evidence here is cross-sectional or a single structural test of one theory; the strongest design is the treatment meta-analysis, which shows randomised improvement in self-concept with large, unexplained variation between trials, and none of the sources establish whether negative self-concept in complex PTSD and identity disturbance in borderline personality disorder are genuinely distinct processes or two measurements of a related, overlapping one.

## Answer notes

1. Negative self-concept concerns the content of a self-view (how negative); identity disturbance concerns its stability and coherence over time (how consistent).
2. Psychotherapy for PTSD improved negative self-concept with a moderate-to-large effect, but self-concept was measured by self-report alongside symptoms, so some change may reflect general relief rather than a targeted shift.
3. Because the supporting studies did not control for co-occurring depression and anxiety, which independently lower self-esteem and could account for some or all of the association.
