# Mindfulness-based interventions

**Module.** M04 Psychology · **Topic.** [[Mindfulness-based interventions]]

## Why this matters

Mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) get marketed as a near-universal remedy, and the review literature is more measured: effective for some common problems, inconclusive or preliminary for several conditions covered here. Reading MBI evidence well means asking which outcome moved, and whether the review behind a pooled figure was methodologically sound.

## The core model

MBIs range from an established, manualised protocol to broader, mindfulness-informed therapies where mindfulness is one element among several - and the sourced evidence treats them mostly as general-purpose programmes rather than treatments built for one diagnosis. An overview of the review literature judges MBIs effective for common problems such as depression, anxiety and stress, but describes the evidence for PTSD, ADHD and autism as inconclusive or preliminary - and that breadth is also a limit, since one overall verdict then covers very different programmes and populations. [[P33884400 Zhang 2021 Mindfulness-based interventions overall review#^p33884400-agreement|Zhang 2021]] [[P33884400 Zhang 2021 Mindfulness-based interventions overall review#^p33884400-controversy|Zhang 2021]] [[P33884400 Zhang 2021 Mindfulness-based interventions overall review#^p33884400-caution-breadth|Appraisal: Zhang 2021]]

PTSD has the largest pooled effect among the conditions in this vault, and is the clearest example of why a review's own quality matters as much as its headline number. An umbrella review of 69 systematic reviews found a medium pooled reduction in PTSD symptoms, consistent across control conditions and, the authors say, comparable to second-line treatments - but that same umbrella review rated roughly two-thirds of the reviews it pooled as non-rigorous, so the effect inherits their weaknesses, and PTSD samples are not complex PTSD samples. [[P38761551 Jovanovic 2024 Mindfulness-based interventions for PTSD#^p38761551-effect|Jovanovic 2024]] [[P38761551 Jovanovic 2024 Mindfulness-based interventions for PTSD#^p38761551-quality|Jovanovic 2024]] [[P38761551 Jovanovic 2024 Mindfulness-based interventions for PTSD#^p38761551-caution-inherited|Appraisal: Jovanovic 2024]]

Bipolar disorder shows a narrower benefit. Across 13 studies, mindfulness-based and mindfulness-informed interventions had no significant effect on depression or mania, but a moderate effect specifically on anxiety - itself provisional, resting partly on imputed comparisons and short follow-up. "Mindfulness helps bipolar disorder" overstates this; "mindfulness may reduce anxiety alongside bipolar disorder, without a shown effect on core mood symptoms" is what the data support. [[P35102640 Burgos-Julian 2022 Mindfulness interventions in bipolar disorder#^p35102640-mood|Burgos-Julián 2022]] [[P35102640 Burgos-Julian 2022 Mindfulness interventions in bipolar disorder#^p35102640-anxiety|Burgos-Julián 2022]] [[P35102640 Burgos-Julian 2022 Mindfulness interventions in bipolar disorder#^p35102640-caution-design|Appraisal: Burgos-Julián 2022]]

Across these conditions, one reading rule recurs: ask whether the programme was an established protocol or a broader mindfulness-informed package, who rated the outcome, whether the review behind a pooled figure was rigorous, and - in mood disorders - which of mood, anxiety or relapse actually moved.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A wellness article states "mindfulness is a proven treatment for PTSD, backed by dozens of studies," citing the umbrella review of 69 systematic reviews.

Check the quality layer the headline skips. The review does report a medium pooled effect, the largest MBI effect sourced here - but it also rated about two-thirds of the 69 reviews beneath it as non-rigorous. A pooled effect built mostly from lower-quality reviews is not "dozens of high-quality studies confirm this." The accurate version names both facts together: a medium effect exists, and it needs real caution because of what it is built from - "proven" claims more certainty than this quality profile supports.

## Common confusions

- "Mindfulness works equally well across all conditions studied." The literature distinguishes solid support (depression, anxiety, stress) from inconclusive or preliminary evidence (PTSD, ADHD, autism).
- "A medium pooled effect means strong evidence." The PTSD umbrella review's medium effect is built mostly from non-rigorous reviews - effect size and evidence quality are separate questions.
- "Mindfulness helping anxiety in bipolar disorder means it helps generally." The same synthesis found no significant effect on depression or mania, only anxiety, and that result is provisional.

## What the sources do not establish

Across these conditions the evidence is modest and uneven: the PTSD effect rests on mostly non-rigorous reviews, the bipolar benefit is limited to anxiety and provisional, and no autism-specific trial or rigorous ADHD self-rated outcome is sourced here, so an unstudied outcome should read as absence of evidence, not evidence of no effect.

## Check yourself

1. Why does the overview review call MBI evidence "inconclusive or preliminary" for some conditions while calling it effective for others?
2. Why does the PTSD umbrella review's medium pooled effect need to be read alongside its quality rating of the reviews it pooled?
3. What exactly did the bipolar-disorder mindfulness synthesis find, and what did it not find?

## Answer notes

1. Because MBIs are studied as general-purpose programmes across very different conditions, and the underlying trial base is much stronger for depression, anxiety and stress than for PTSD, ADHD and autism.
2. Because about two-thirds of the 69 reviews behind the pooled estimate were rated non-rigorous, so the medium effect size inherits those reviews' weaknesses rather than standing on high-quality evidence alone.
