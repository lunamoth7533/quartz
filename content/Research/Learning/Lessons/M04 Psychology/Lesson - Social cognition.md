# Social cognition

**Module.** M04 Psychology · **Topic.** [[Social cognition]]

## Why this matters

Claims that someone lacks empathy or struggles to read others are among the most consequential and easily overstated claims in this vault's condition domains. Social cognition research supplies the discipline for reading them: what was measured, on which task, and whether the finding is about capacity, performance, or interaction between people.

## The core model

Social-cognition research sits inside a broader lesson from social psychology: situations and interpretations shape behaviour, offered as an alternative to explanations that locate behaviour entirely in stable dispositions. [[F46 OpenStax Psychology 2e social psychology#^f46-situational|What Is Social Psychology?]]

Attribution - how people explain behaviour - is where this plays out constantly. People routinely over-weight dispositional causes ("they did that because of who they are") when explaining other people's behaviour, a systematic asymmetry known as the fundamental attribution error. It is measurable, appears across cultures with varying strength, and shapes everyday judgements about responsibility and blame - which is why it turns up in clinical formulation and stigma research, not only in textbooks. [[F46 OpenStax Psychology 2e social psychology#^f46-attribution|What Is Social Psychology?]]

Mentalising - inferring what another person believes, wants or intends - is the construct behind claims that some groups have specific social difficulties. Theory-of-mind tasks are the best-known way of measuring it, and differences on these tasks have been used to explain autistic social differences. But task performance correlates only weakly with everyday social outcomes, and many autistic people pass the tasks, so a task-performance finding explains a subgroup at most. Extending a group difference on one inference task to a general claim about a group's capacity to understand minds requires ruling out task demands, language load, motivation and interest first. [[P31949163 Lord 2020 Autism spectrum disorder primer#^p31949163-construct|Autism spectrum disorder primer]]

A newer line of work reframes social difficulty as relational rather than located in one person: misunderstanding between two people often arises because their expectations differ from each other, not because one side lacks a general capacity - the double-empathy account. Autistic adults' own descriptions of managing self-presentation and compensating for others' expectations fit this framing, since the effort of bridging a gap runs in both directions, even when only one side's difficulty gets studied. [[P28527095 Hull 2017 Social camouflaging#^p28527095-strategies|Social camouflaging]] [[P28527095 Hull 2017 Social camouflaging#^p28527095-consequence|Social camouflaging]]

The reading rule that ties this together: name the construct, the task, the comparison group and who is reporting, and state whether the claim is about capacity, performance, or interaction - most disputes here are disputes about which of those three a study measured.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A summary says "a study found that group X scored lower on a theory-of-mind test, proving they struggle to understand other people's minds."

Slow the claim down. What was the task, specifically, and what does it require beyond mentalising, such as verbal comprehension or motivation to engage with an artificial scenario? Was the comparison group matched, and did the design include the reverse comparison - how well the "typical" group understands the tested group - given that double empathy predicts misunderstanding runs both ways? A defensible conclusion specifies the task and stops at "group X performed differently on this task" - not a general claim about capacity, which the task alone cannot establish.

## Common confusions

- "A theory-of-mind task measures someone's general capacity to understand other minds." It measures performance on one specific inference task, which correlates only weakly with everyday social functioning.
- "Social difficulty always means one person lacks a skill." The double-empathy account reframes many social difficulties as mismatched expectations between two people, not a deficit located in one.
- "The fundamental attribution error is a laboratory curiosity." It is a measurable, everyday bias in how behaviour gets explained, with real consequences for judgements about blame and responsibility.

## What the sources do not establish

Attribution and mentalising effects are well established in laboratory paradigms, but their power to explain real-world social outcomes is more limited than the paradigms alone suggest; a group comparison on any of these measures also assumes the measure works the same way in both groups, which is a testable property rather than an automatic one.

## Check yourself

1. What is the fundamental attribution error, and why does it matter outside the laboratory?
2. Why can't a theory-of-mind task result be generalised directly into a claim about a group's capacity to understand minds?
3. How does the double-empathy account reframe social difficulty compared with a pure mentalising-deficit account?

## Answer notes

1. The tendency to over-explain other people's behaviour by disposition rather than situation; it shapes everyday judgements about responsibility and blame.
2. Because performance on one task also reflects task demands, language load, motivation and interest, and because task performance correlates only weakly with everyday social outcomes.
3. It locates difficulty in a mismatch between two people's expectations rather than in one person's deficit, which is consistent with difficulty running in both directions rather than only one.
