# Alexithymia

**Module.** M04 Psychology · **Topic.** [[Alexithymia]]

## Why this matters

Alexithymia - difficulty identifying and describing one's own feelings - gets invoked as an explanation across autism, trauma and ADHD research here. It is also a case study in why "the questionnaire found a difference" and "the trait explains the difference" are not the same claim.

## The core model

The standard questionnaires score alexithymia along two facets - difficulty identifying feelings and difficulty describing them - together with a third dimension, externally oriented thinking. These facets do not behave identically: in a community sample, factor analysis separated a general alexithymia factor from a general-distress factor, and the difficulty-identifying-feelings facet of the most widely used scale, the 20-item Toronto Alexithymia Scale, loaded heavily onto the distress factor while a different questionnaire's scores did not. [[P38320659 Preece 2024 Discriminant validity of alexithymia questionnaires#^p38320659-factors|Preece 2024]] [[P38320659 Preece 2024 Discriminant validity of alexithymia questionnaires#^p38320659-dif|Preece 2024]]

That matters because a total score on the most common instrument can be partly measuring current distress rather than a stable trait. If part of that facet tracks how anxious or low someone feels right now, a study relating the questionnaire's totals to anxiety, depression or PTSD symptoms has some of the overlap built in before any real relationship is measured - so such studies need that overlap statistically controlled. [[P38320659 Preece 2024 Discriminant validity of alexithymia questionnaires#^p38320659-caution-overlap|Appraisal: Preece 2024]]

With that caveat in view, the autism association is large and consistent by the questionnaire's own terms: across a meta-analysis of 15 studies using the Toronto scale, autistic groups scored higher than neurotypical comparison groups on every subscale, and nearly half of autistic participants (49.93%) fell in the alexithymic range against under 5% (4.89%) of neurotypical participants - common, not universal, which is exactly why it can be tested as an alternative explanation for emotion-processing differences usually attributed to autism itself. [[P30399531 Kinnaird 2019 Alexithymia in autism meta-analysis#^p30399531-scores|Kinnaird 2019]] [[P30399531 Kinnaird 2019 Alexithymia in autism meta-analysis#^p30399531-prevalence|Kinnaird 2019]]

When that test has actually been run, the results disagree. In one behavioural study, autistic participants with high alexithymia misclassified more facial expressions than controls, while those with low alexithymia did not differ from controls - consistent with alexithymia, not autism, driving the difference. But in a separate fMRI study of 120 adults, autistic participants recognised facial affect less accurately regardless of alexithymia status, while alexithymia instead changed brain activation patterns rather than behavioural accuracy - two well-designed studies, same hypothesis, different measures, different conclusions. [[P39827966 Kirsch 2025 Alexithymia hypothesis of autism revisited#^p39827966-behaviour|Kirsch 2025]] [[P39827966 Kirsch 2025 Alexithymia hypothesis of autism revisited#^p39827966-brain|Kirsch 2025]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A summary claims "a new study proves that autistic people's emotion-recognition differences are really just alexithymia in disguise."

Check what the test requires. Showing alexithymia is common in autism (49.93% versus 4.89% by one cutoff) is not the same as showing it explains an emotion-recognition difference - that needs autistic people with and without alexithymia directly compared against non-autistic groups on the same task. When made, the comparison splits: one behavioural study found the difference tracked alexithymia status; a brain-imaging study found autistic participants differed regardless of alexithymia, which instead shaped brain activation. A defensible summary is "tested and contested, depending on whether behaviour or brain activity is measured" - not "proves."

## Common confusions

- "Alexithymia and autism are the same thing." Alexithymia is common but not universal in autistic samples (roughly half by one common cutoff), so it is a co-occurring, separately measurable trait.
- "A high alexithymia score always reflects a stable, trait-like difficulty." Part of the most widely used scale's score can reflect current distress rather than a fixed trait, which is why studies linking it to anxiety or depression need that overlap controlled.
- "Recent research settled whether alexithymia explains autistic emotion-recognition differences." Recent studies disagree with each other, with the answer depending on whether behaviour or brain activity was measured.

## What the sources do not establish

Prevalence figures for alexithymia in autism rest on a questionnaire cut-off and inherit both the measurement debate and that cut-off's uncertainty; whether alexithymia causally explains emotion-recognition differences in autism remains genuinely unresolved, with recent well-designed studies disagreeing depending on the task and the outcome measured.
