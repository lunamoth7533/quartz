# Perception

**Module.** M04 Psychology · **Topic.** [[Perception]]

## Why this matters

Every symptom report, questionnaire answer and clinical observation in this vault passes through perception before it becomes data. Learning that perception actively builds a scene rather than passively recording one changes how a claim about what someone "saw", "felt" or "noticed" should be read: the report is already an interpretation, not a printout of the senses.

## The core model

**Sensation** is the detection of a stimulus by a sensory receptor; **perception** is the organisation and interpretation of that signal into the scene a person experiences. A report always combines both stages, which is why the two words are not interchangeable. [[F37 OpenStax Psychology 2e sensation versus perception#^f37-sensation|Sensation versus Perception]]

An **absolute threshold** marks the weakest stimulus a person can detect, but a detection is not a pure sensory measurement. Signal detection methods separate sensory sensitivity from the decision criterion, or response bias, a person applies to an ambiguous case - two people with identical sensitivity can still disagree about whether they "noticed" something because one needs more certainty before saying yes. [[F37 OpenStax Psychology 2e sensation versus perception#^f37-absolute|Sensation versus Perception]]

The clearest evidence that perception is constructive comes from illusions: stable, repeatable demonstrations that identical input supports different interpreted scenes depending on context and expectation. An illusion is not a malfunction but the same inference process running all the time, combining input with assumptions about lighting, distance and object constancy - assumptions so well matched to the world that they only show when the world is arranged to violate them. That is also why bottom-up (receptor to cortex) and top-down (the effects of expectation and attention) describe an analytical division, not an anatomical one: real perception runs both at once, so a claim that expectation changed what someone perceived needs its own evidence. [[F37 OpenStax Psychology 2e sensation versus perception#^f37-illusions|Sensation versus Perception]] [[F37 OpenStax Psychology 2e sensation versus perception]]

The formal version of "perception as inference" is predictive coding: algorithms that fit a generative model to sensory data, so what is perceived is a prediction checked against input. These algorithms differ substantially in their models and optimisation, so evidence for one specific algorithm is not automatically evidence for the whole framework. [[P26809759 Spratling 2017 Predictive coding algorithms#^p26809759-common|Predictive coding algorithms]] [[P26809759 Spratling 2017 Predictive coding algorithms#^p26809759-difference|Predictive coding algorithms]]

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. Two volunteers view the same briefly flashed, ambiguous image: one reports a face, the other nothing recognisable.

This is not proof that perception is "just subjective." Ask a signal-detection question instead: is the input identical, and is the difference in sensitivity (genuinely extracting more information) or in criterion (needing less evidence before saying "face")? A study would present the same image repeatedly, mixed with clear faces and non-faces, and use the pattern of hits and false alarms to estimate sensitivity and bias separately. Only once a criterion difference is ruled out does "they perceived it differently" become supported.

## Common confusions

- "Perception is just a recording of what the senses pick up." It is an active, inferential construction that combines sensory input with prior assumptions.
- "An illusion means the visual system is broken." Illusions are ordinary evidence that perception infers a scene; they show the process working, not failing.
- "Bottom-up and top-down are two separate brain pathways." They are an analytical distinction about what is being explained, not two anatomical routes; real perception uses both together.

## What the sources do not establish

Predictive coding is a family of differing algorithms, not one theory, so a result consistent with one version does not confirm the general claim that perception is inference; that claim remains a modelling proposal rather than a settled fact about mechanism. The behavioural regularities behind constructive perception - illusions, threshold effects, context sensitivity - are much better established than any specific algorithm proposed to explain them.

2. Why do illusions count as evidence for constructive perception rather than as errors?
3. Why doesn't a finding that supports one predictive-coding algorithm support the whole family of such algorithms?

## Answer notes

1. Sensation is detection by a receptor; perception is the organisation and interpretation of that signal into an experienced scene. A report reflects both, so it cannot be read as a pure sensory measurement.
2. Illusions are stable, repeatable cases where the same input supports different interpreted scenes depending on context - exactly what a constructive, inferential process predicts, not a sign of malfunction.
3. Predictive-coding algorithms share only the general idea of fitting a generative model to sensory data; they differ substantially in their models and optimisation methods, so evidence is tied to the specific algorithm tested.
