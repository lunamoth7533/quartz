# Post-traumatic stress disorder

**Module.** M10 CPTSD · **Topic.** [[Post-traumatic stress disorder]]

## Why this matters

PTSD is the diagnosis every other idea in this module builds on: complex PTSD is PTSD plus more, fear learning
explains its first-line treatment, and memory research explains one of its core symptoms.

## The core model

**What the diagnosis covers.** PTSD can follow exposure to a traumatic event and is marked by re-experiencing,
avoidance and a persisting sense of threat or heightened arousal - a symptom-based diagnosis, distinct from the
ordinary stress response covered in the previous lesson.
[[P32644555 Torrico 2024 Posttraumatic stress disorder#^p32644555-definition|Torrico 2024]]

**Two manuals, overlapping but different rules.** DSM criteria describe symptoms in four areas after exposure:
intrusions, avoidance, negative shifts in mood and thinking, and heightened arousal. ICD-11 builds PTSD more
narrowly around three fear-related components: reliving the event, avoiding reminders, and a heightened sense of
current threat.
[[P32644555 Torrico 2024 Posttraumatic stress disorder#^p32644555-dsm|Torrico 2024]]
[[F17 VA National Center for PTSD Complex PTSD history and definitions#^f17-core-clusters|VA: Complex PTSD history and definitions]]
The two systems can pick out partly different people even when they agree on how many: a German survey found
DSM-5 and ICD-11 giving the same 4.7% prevalence but agreeing only substantially with each other (kappa 0.62).
[[P40859920 Pettrich 2025 DSM-5 versus ICD-11 PTSD prevalence#^p40859920-agreement|Pettrich 2025]]

**Exposure is common; the disorder is not.** Across 26 World Mental Health surveys with over 71,000 adults,
lifetime PTSD was 3.9% overall and 5.6% among the exposed - most people who experience trauma do not develop
PTSD. Markers of social disadvantage went with higher risk among the exposed, a group-level pattern rather than
an individual forecast, and about half of people with PTSD described their symptoms as persistent.
[[P28385165 Koenen 2017 PTSD in the World Mental Health Surveys#^p28385165-prevalence|Koenen 2017]]
[[P28385165 Koenen 2017 PTSD in the World Mental Health Surveys#^p28385165-risk|Koenen 2017]]

**First-line treatment, at the level of a class.** Across 14 international guidelines, most count both
psychological and pharmacological therapies as first-line, naming cognitive behavioural therapy and selective
serotonin reuptake inhibitors most often - recommendations about treatment classes for populations, not
individual advice.
[[P34575284 Martin 2021 Treatment guidelines for PTSD#^p34575284-firstline|Martin 2021]]
[[P34575284 Martin 2021 Treatment guidelines for PTSD#^p34575284-caution-classlevel|Appraisal: Martin 2021]]

**Relation to complex PTSD.** ICD-11 complex PTSD requires the PTSD criteria plus disturbances in
self-organisation, while DSM-5 has no separate complex PTSD diagnosis. In representative, non-war-exposed
samples, complex PTSD (about 4%) was actually more common than PTSD (about 2%) - siblings built on one
foundation, not points on one severity scale.
[[F17 VA National Center for PTSD Complex PTSD history and definitions#^f17-dso|VA: Complex PTSD history and definitions]]
[[P40652792 Fung 2025 ICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD prevalence#^p40652792-pooled|Fung 2025]]

**Reading rule.** Before comparing PTSD rates, name the manual, the instrument, and whether the figure is
lifetime, past-year or current.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. Two hypothetical surveys report PTSD prevalence in the same country -
2% and 5% - and a summary calls this "a rapid rise in PTSD."

Apply the reading rule first. Which manual did each use - DSM-5 and ICD-11 can agree closely on overall
prevalence while disagreeing substantially on who counts, so a manual switch alone could move the number. Which
instrument and time window - lifetime prevalence is higher than current by design. Only once these are matched
does a real change become a candidate explanation, rather than the first one considered.

The accurate statement is not "PTSD is rising" but a methods comparison: these figures used different
definitions or windows, so the gap needs matching measures before it can be read as a trend.

## Common confusions

- "PTSD is just being very stressed after something bad." It is a specific symptom-based diagnosis, distinct
  from the stress response most exposed people have without meeting criteria.
- "The rate of PTSD after trauma is a fixed number." It moves with the manual, instrument and time window used.
- "Complex PTSD is simply more severe PTSD." It is a related but separate ICD-11 diagnosis built on the PTSD
  criteria plus additional domains.

## What the sources do not establish

None of these sources explains why most exposed people do not develop PTSD while some do; social-disadvantage
markers describe group risk only. None gives dose, drug-choice or individual treatment guidance.

2. Why can two systems agree on "how many" people have PTSD while disagreeing on "who"?
3. What is the relationship between PTSD and ICD-11 complex PTSD?

## Answer notes

1. DSM describes symptoms across four areas; ICD-11 builds PTSD more narrowly around three fear-related
   components.
2. Their criteria select overlapping but not identical groups, so a shared prevalence estimate can still come
   with only moderate agreement on individual caseness.
3. Complex PTSD requires the PTSD criteria plus disturbances in self-organisation; it is ICD-11's separate
   diagnosis, not simply "more severe" PTSD.
