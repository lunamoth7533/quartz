# Psychological interventions for complex trauma

**Module.** M10 CPTSD · **Topic.** [[Psychological interventions for complex trauma]]

## Why this matters

This is the module's main efficacy evidence, and it is a good example of a meta-analysis that reports both a
positive result and the reasons to hold it carefully. The lesson is about reading pooled effects with their
moderators, not about deciding what any person should do.

## The core model

Pooled psychotherapy effects compared with control were significant for PTSD (k = 27), depression (k = 23),
anxiety (k = 13) and dissociation (k = 7) outcomes.
[[P40154799 Hu 2025 Psychological interventions for CPTSD#^p40154799-improvement|Psychological interventions for complex PTSD]]

At follow-up the effect sizes decreased slightly but remained significant, except for anxiety and dissociation
symptoms.
[[P40154799 Hu 2025 Psychological interventions for CPTSD#^p40154799-followup|Psychological interventions for complex PTSD]]

Participants with childhood trauma showed lower effects than those with other trauma types; risk of bias and
the proportion of female participants moderated results, and the authors report some indications of publication
bias.
[[P40154799 Hu 2025 Psychological interventions for CPTSD#^p40154799-moderators|Psychological interventions for complex PTSD]]

The authors call for work on how interventions can be tailored.
[[P40154799 Hu 2025 Psychological interventions for CPTSD#^p40154799-limit|Psychological interventions for complex PTSD]]

One boundary is easy to miss: complex trauma exposure in these trials is not the same category as a formal
ICD-11 complex PTSD diagnosis.
[[P40154799 Hu 2025 Psychological interventions for CPTSD#^p40154799-caution-category|AI synthesis: Psychological interventions for complex PTSD]]

So the honest summary has three parts: psychotherapy outperformed control on several outcomes on average;
effects attenuated somewhat at follow-up for some outcomes; and the pooled sample is heterogeneous, with
moderators and publication-bias indications that limit how far a single number travels.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. Someone reads that psychotherapy for complex trauma "works" with an
effect size of about 0.5 and asks what it means for a person with a long history of childhood trauma.

Apply the moderators. The pooled effect averages across samples with different trauma histories and
definitions; the analysis found lower effects in participants with childhood trauma. The person differs from
the average in a direction the data associate with smaller benefit — which does not mean treatment will fail,
because the moderator describes group averages, and it does not identify what would work instead.

The accurate answer: "across these trials psychotherapy outperformed control on average for several outcomes;
participants with childhood trauma showed smaller average effects, so the pooled number is a starting point,
not a prediction for you."

## Common confusions

- "Pooled effect equals expected benefit." It is an average across heterogeneous trials with moderators.
- "Follow-up attenuation means treatment does not last." Effects decreased slightly and mostly remained;
  anxiety and dissociation did not.
- "Publication-bias indications change the estimate." They indicate the estimate may be optimistic; the
  meta-analysis reports the indication rather than a corrected value.

## What the sources do not establish

The meta-analysis records complex trauma exposure rather than formal ICD-11 diagnoses in its samples, and it
provides no individual prediction or treatment recommendation. This vault holds no source that identifies which
intervention fits which person.

## Check yourself

1. On which outcomes were pooled effects significant?
2. What happened at follow-up?
3. Which sample characteristic was associated with lower effects, and what does that mean?

## Answer notes

1. PTSD, depression, anxiety and dissociation.
2. Effects decreased slightly and remained significant except for anxiety and dissociation.
3. Childhood trauma, associated with lower average effects in the pooled analysis; it describes a group
   pattern, not an individual prognosis.
