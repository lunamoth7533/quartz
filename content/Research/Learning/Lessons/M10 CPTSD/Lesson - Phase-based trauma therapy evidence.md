# Phase-based trauma therapy evidence

**Module.** M10 CPTSD · **Topic.** [[Phase-based trauma therapy evidence]]

## Why this matters

Clinical guidance has long recommended stabilisation before trauma processing. This lesson is about whether the
comparative evidence supports requiring that sequence, and about the difference between "no clear difference
was found" and "the sequence does not matter".

## The core model

Across most outcomes there were no significant differences between phase-based and non-phase-based
interventions.
[[P41949043 Lee 2026 Phase-based versus non-phase-based trauma therapy#^p41949043-comparison|Phase-based versus non-phase-based trauma therapy]]

Multi-phase interventions had superior effects on PTSD symptoms, and phase-based, multi-phase and exposure
designs did better on affect regulation.
[[P41949043 Lee 2026 Phase-based versus non-phase-based trauma therapy#^p41949043-outcomes|Phase-based versus non-phase-based trauma therapy]]

The authors' reading is nuanced: in many situations, approaches without a stabilisation phase or formal
exposure can be as effective as structured approaches, while some outcomes may still benefit from structure.
[[P41949043 Lee 2026 Phase-based versus non-phase-based trauma therapy#^p41949043-conclusion|Phase-based versus non-phase-based trauma therapy]]

The methodological limit is decisive for interpretation: the comparison is between packaged protocols rather
than a randomised test of sequencing inside one protocol.
[[P41949043 Lee 2026 Phase-based versus non-phase-based trauma therapy#^p41949043-caution-sequence|AI synthesis: Phase-based versus non-phase-based trauma therapy]]

The trial base is small, with a single analysis time point and heterogeneous outcome measures, as the authors
state.
[[P41949043 Lee 2026 Phase-based versus non-phase-based trauma therapy#^p41949043-limit|Phase-based versus non-phase-based trauma therapy]]

So the accurate summary is: the evidence does not support an absolute requirement for stabilisation before
processing, and it does not show that sequence is irrelevant; it shows that packages with and without a
stabilisation phase often produce similar average outcomes.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. Design the trial that would actually test sequencing.

Take one protocol and randomise the order of its components: an immediate-processing arm and a
stabilisation-first arm, with identical total contact time, identical components and identical therapists where
possible. Pre-specify PTSD symptoms and affect regulation as outcomes, measure baseline dissociation, affect
regulation and trauma complexity as moderators, and follow participants long enough to detect delayed effects.
Record dropout and harms, because both explanations predict specific attrition patterns.

What this design can show that the packaged comparison cannot: whether the order of the same components changes
outcomes, in whom, and at what cost. What it still cannot show: whether any specific component is the active
ingredient, which needs further component work.

## Common confusions

- "No difference means sequencing is unnecessary." It means the packaged approaches did not clearly differ on
  average; individual and subgroup effects may still differ.
- "Stabilisation is disproven." The comparison does not test stabilisation as a process inside one protocol.
- "One time point settles durability." It does not.

## What the sources do not establish

The meta-analysis has a small trial base, a single time point and heterogeneous outcomes; it does not establish
what any individual needs and provides no clinical recommendation. This vault holds no within-protocol
sequencing trial.
