# Adverse childhood experiences

**Module.** M10 CPTSD · **Topic.** [[Adverse childhood experiences]]

## Why this matters

The ACE score is one of the most widely quoted numbers in writing about childhood adversity, often used as if it
predicts what will happen to a particular child. This lesson gives the accurate version: a real, graded
population pattern that fails, specifically and measurably, as a tool for one person.

## The core model

**Counting rather than cataloguing.** Adverse childhood experiences often occur together, so the ACE framework
sums how many types a person experienced rather than studying one at a time; syntheses often compare four-or-more
against none. A count of this kind says nothing about which adversities, what severity, or what timing.
[[P29253477 Hughes 2017 Multiple adverse childhood experiences and health#^p29253477-definition|Hughes 2017]]
[[P29253477 Hughes 2017 Multiple adverse childhood experiences and health#^p29253477-caution-association|Appraisal: Hughes 2017]]

**A real, graded association.** Across 37 studies of over 253,000 adults, people reporting four or more ACEs had
higher risk than those reporting none on all 23 health outcomes studied, with strength varying widely - under 2
odds ratio for inactivity, above 7 for problematic drug use and for violence. In a separate twin cohort, each
additional ACE raised later mental and physical health risk beyond sex, disadvantage and earlier health.
[[P29253477 Hughes 2017 Multiple adverse childhood experiences and health#^p29253477-dose|Hughes 2017]]
[[P33492366 Baldwin 2021 ACE scores and individual prediction#^p33492366-group|Baldwin 2021]]

**How much is causal.** Quasi-experimental designs address that directly. For childhood maltreatment, the
unadjusted association with later mental health problems fell substantially once twin, sibling, adoption and
fixed-effects designs addressed confounding, and the smaller estimate held across designs - read as a real but
smaller causal contribution, with part of the raw association reflecting shared genetic and environmental risk.
[[P36628513 Baldwin 2023 Childhood maltreatment quasi-experimental meta-analysis#^p36628513-attenuation|Baldwin 2023]]
[[P36628513 Baldwin 2023 Childhood maltreatment quasi-experimental meta-analysis#^p36628513-interpretation|Baldwin 2023]]

**Retrospective and prospective counts identify different people.** Across 16 studies with both kinds of
measure, prospective records and adult recall of maltreatment agreed poorly, picking out different people who
may follow different risk pathways - neither is simply correct, since records miss undetected maltreatment and
recall is shaped by memory and current state.
[[P30892562 Baldwin 2019 Prospective versus retrospective maltreatment measures#^p30892562-agreement|Baldwin 2019]]
[[P30892562 Baldwin 2019 Prospective versus retrospective maltreatment measures#^p30892562-groups|Baldwin 2019]]

**Group risk is not individual prediction.** In two large birth cohorts, ACE scores discriminated only a little
above chance between individuals who did and did not later develop problems, whether the measure was prospective
or retrospective. The authors judged ACE-based targeting likely ineffective, and a score cannot substitute for
assessing a particular person.
[[P33492366 Baldwin 2021 ACE scores and individual prediction#^p33492366-individual|Baldwin 2021]]
[[P33492366 Baldwin 2021 ACE scores and individual prediction#^p33492366-caution-screening|Appraisal: Baldwin 2021]]

**A proposed pathway.** One route is cumulative physiological wear from repeated stress responses.
[[P17290796 McEwen 2006 Stress mediators#^p17290796-load|McEwen 2006]]

**Reading rule.** Ask whether an ACE figure comes from prospective records or adult recall, whether it contrasts
extreme counts or a per-ACE gradient, whether confounding was addressed, and whether the claim concerns groups
or a person.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

This scenario is invented for practice. A hypothetical school proposes giving every student an ACE
questionnaire and automatically assigning anyone scoring four or above to an intensive support track.

Check the plan against the individual-prediction evidence: ACE scores discriminated only a little better than
chance in two large cohorts, so a cutoff-based policy will misclassify students both ways - some assigned to
support who would not have developed problems, some left out who would have. The graded, population-level
association is real; it does not license sorting individual students by score.

The defensible version: ACE counts describe a real risk gradient across large groups, and are not validated as
a tool for deciding what happens to one student.

## Common confusions

- "A high ACE score means someone will definitely have problems." Discrimination between individuals is barely
  better than chance.
- "The graded association proves ACEs cause the outcomes." A smaller but real causal contribution survived
  designs that addressed confounding, not the full raw association.
- "Retrospective recall and records measure the same experiences." They agree poorly and may identify different
  people.

## What the sources do not establish

None of these sources says which specific adversities, severities or timings matter most, or which pathway
carries the causal share for a given person. No source tests whether services organised around ACE scores
improve outcomes.
